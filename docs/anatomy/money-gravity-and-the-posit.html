Where the project began: money against gravity, Popper and the humanities, the anatomy of an abstract theory, four research reports, Psillos, and a machine that predicts without posits — twenty-three questions and their answers

What this conversation covers

Money is made by agreement; gravity describes a regularity that holds without minds. Theories in both domains share one architecture — measured quantities plus posits fixed by their role — but prediction alone cannot certify those posits, since a trained network predicts without them. What is left is a choice: drop the hidden thing behind the regularities, and keep the discipline that follows.

This is the conversation the project began from. It moves from money and gravity to the humanities and Popper, then to the anatomy of an abstract theory, a research prompt and the reports it produced, the theoretician’s dilemma and the charge of circularity, and finally structural realism, Psillos, the structure-of-structures objection, machine learning, and the noumenon.

Part One

Money, gravity and the humanities

The conversation opens with money against gravity, sorts the humanities into their own category, tests them against Popper, and turns to a video arguing that reality corrects only the sciences.

01Money versus gravity

Why money and gravity are different kinds of abstraction.

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 1“So, money is an abstract concept, but we use a symbol to represent it. How is this different from abstract explanations like gravity, which explain physical phenomena? Check past chats.”
Claude’s answer

They’re different in kind, not just degree — and the distinction is one you’ve already been circling in your correlationism work.

Money is a Searlean institutional fact. Its existence is constituted by collective intentionality. The symbol doesn’t merely represent value — given the institutional frame, the symbol is the value-bearer. Remove collective agreement (currency collapse, demonetization) and the “thing” literally evaporates. There is no money-in-itself behind the appearance. Just paper, metal, or bits.

Gravity is a descriptive model of a mind-independent regularity. F = Gm₁m₂/r² doesn’t constitute gravity; it describes a phenomenon that would persist if every mind vanished tomorrow. Stones fell before Newton, and would fall if humans went extinct.

Three sharper cuts:

  1. Direction of fit. Money has mind-to-world fit — we make it real by representing it. Gravity has world-to-mind fit — the symbol tries to track what’s already there.
  2. Counterfactual stability. Money is fragile under belief change. Gravity is invariant to belief.
  3. Hacking’s distinction. Money is a looping kind — the concept reshapes its referent (declare a currency invalid and it becomes invalid). Gravity is an indifferent kind — masses don’t care what we call them.

This is exactly where your passport thought-experiment cuts: for money, the correlationist is right by construction — there’s no ancestral existence outside minds. For gravity, Meillassoux’s arche-fossil argument bites hard — gravity operated 13 billion years before any mind existed for it to “correlate” with.

A third category worth keeping in view: mathematical objects. Neither money-like (institutional) nor gravity-like (physical regularity). That’s the genuinely hard case — Frege says mind-independent abstracta, formalists say institutional, Platonists posit a third realm. Your ZFC-upward project is essentially the question of which.

02What kind are humanities theories?

Where the humanities sit between physics and institutions.

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 2“So what types are the theories in humanities studies?”
Claude’s answer

Mostly money-like — but with twists that make them their own third category, not just a special case.

The objects studied:

  • Political theory, sociology, anthropology — states, norms, sovereignty, kinship: Searlean institutional facts.
  • Literary/art theory — texts and canons exist because interpretive communities sustain them.
  • History — partly brute substrate (Hastings happened) wrapped in interpretively constructed meaning, causation, significance.
  • Gender/race/cultural studies — paradigm looping kinds. Foucault’s “homosexual” as a 19th-century invention; Hacking’s “making up people.”

But two structural features separate humanities theories from mere theories-of-institutional-facts:

  1. Reflexive looping. Theories of gravity don’t change gravity. Marxist theory of class changed class consciousness. Feminist theory altered gender practice. The theory partly constitutes its own subject over time — Hacking’s looping effect of human kinds.

  2. Verstehen, not Erklären (Dilthey, Weber). The humanities don’t seek nomothetic laws. They seek understanding from within — grasping meaning, intention, significance. Since the object is itself a meaning-bearer, the theory must be hermeneutic, not just causal.

So a cleaner tripartite scheme:

  • Gravity-like: physics, chemistry. Indifferent kinds, world-to-mind fit, no looping.
  • Money-like: institutional facts. Looping kinds, mind-to-world fit. Economics tries to be gravity-like about money-like objects — hence its permanent methodological anxiety.
  • Humanities-like: theories about meaning-bearers, themselves reshaping their subject through interpretation. The hermeneutic circle is built in, not a bug.

This is exactly your intra-formal vs synthetic labor point from before. Humanities resist automation because they don’t operate inside a fixed frame — they interrogate frames, generate new conceptual space, and shift what they study by studying it. The opposite of syntactic labor, and the reason “the hardest hard science” framing in your Sinhala essay holds up.

03Popper and the humanities

Whether the humanities patch failing theories instead of dropping them.

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 3“But still, isn’t this an issue shown by Popper? In science, if an abstract theory is not working, we dump it. In the humanities, instead of dumping it, they change it or create more abstract reasons?”
Claude’s answer

Popper is partly right — but his binary hides three different cases.

1. Genuine degeneration (Popper lands). Orthodox Marxism predicted revolution in industrial Britain and Germany; it happened in agrarian Russia and China. Defenders added auxiliary hypotheses — false consciousness, imperialism exporting contradictions, Gramscian hegemony. Each patch was generated to save the theory, not to predict anything new. Exactly Popper’s pseudoscience.

2. Progressive interpretive deepening (different game). Some humanities aren’t trying to predict at all. When literary theory moves from New Criticism → structuralism → deconstruction → ecocriticism, that’s not “rescuing” a falsified theory. It’s enriching an interpretive vocabulary for a non-predictive object. Asking Frye’s archetypes to be falsifiable is a category mistake, like asking a sonnet to be true or false.

3. Self-sealing closure (the real pathology). The genuine Popperian sin in humanities is the “if you disagree, that proves you’re ideologically captured” move. False consciousness, internalized oppression, your-objection-confirms-my-theory. Structurally identical to the worst psychoanalytic moves (“your resistance to the interpretation is itself transference”). This is genuinely unfalsifiable in the bad way.

Two corrections to Popper you’d want even when defending science:

  • Quine-Duhem: physics can also always be saved by adjusting auxiliaries. Newtonian mechanics was rescued by positing Neptune. The naive rule “dump failed theories” never matched what science actually does.
  • Lakatos’s refinement: the real question isn’t “is it falsifiable?” but “is the research program progressive or degenerating?” Progressive: auxiliary hypotheses predict novel facts. Degenerating: they only patch past failures. This test cuts cleanly across both physics and humanities.

So the sharper diagnosis: humanities theories often should be dumped, but the failure mode isn’t abstraction itself — it’s degenerating programs and self-sealing closures. Good humanities (Weber on bureaucracy, Polanyi on the market, Geertz on thick description) keep generating new readings of new phenomena. Bad humanities just immunize themselves against counterexamples while calling the immunization “deepening.”

Which connects to your earlier point: pathological humanities work pretends to be synthetic but is actually patching a frame it refuses to surrender. Real synthetic work changes frames when they break.

04Does empirical reality come first?

Whether an abstraction is valid only if it deals with empirical reality.

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 4“Either in science or the humanities, if it’s an abstraction, the validity of the abstraction depends on how well it deals with empirical realities.

What comes first is empirical reality. An abstraction is valid only as a mere fairy tale unless it is not practical?”
Claude’s answer

Tempting — and there’s a real pragmatist tradition behind it (Peirce, James, Dewey). But the position is more contested than it looks, and contested in ways you’ve already engaged with.

First problem: the claim “abstractions are valid only if they handle empirical realities” is itself an abstraction. What empirical test confirms it? This is the self-undermining trap that killed logical positivism. Ayer’s verification principle (“only the empirically verifiable is meaningful”) was itself neither empirically verifiable nor analytic. It destroyed itself.

Four further problems:

  1. The Riemann case. Non-Euclidean geometry was pure abstraction in the 1850s — useless, “fairy tale” by your criterion. Then Einstein needed it 60 years later for general relativity. Group theory was decades of play before becoming essential to physics, chemistry, cryptography. Hardy famously boasted number theory was useless — then came RSA. If we’d dumped these as fairy tales they wouldn’t have been there when reality finally needed them.

  2. Theory-ladenness of observation (Kuhn, Sellars). There is no naked “empirical reality” you compare abstractions against. You always need a conceptual scheme to identify what even counts as data. So “empirical reality first” is itself a metaphysical position, not a neutral default — your correlationism / passport work already shows you know this.

  3. Kantian conditions of possibility. Some abstractions — number, identity, causation, substance — aren’t validated by experience. They’re the preconditions for there being any experience at all. The abstraction doesn’t follow the world; it structures what counts as a world for you.

  4. The reverse direction. Sometimes abstraction discovers reality. The Higgs boson, black holes, gravitational waves, antimatter — all predicted from mathematical structure decades before observation. The abstraction wasn’t validated by reality. Reality was found by following the abstraction.

A better picture: a dialectic, not a hierarchy. Abstractions are constrained by empirical reality (you can’t make stuff up forever). But empirical reality is conceptually structured (no access without abstractions). And “practical” is value-laden — practical for what? Survival, prediction, beauty, understanding? Each yields a different validity.

The genuine version of your insight is closer to Peirce / Lakatos: not “useful = true,” but that meaning is constituted by long-run consequences for inquiry. That allows pure math, philosophy, and unfalsifiable-but-fertile frameworks to count — provided they participate in the larger fabric of investigation. The fairy-tale verdict is reserved for abstractions that have no feedback with anything, ever — not for ones that haven’t paid off yet.

This is Gad Saad on PragerU, and his argument is exactly the strong empiricist position you were defending two messages ago — “reality is the autocorrective mechanism; humanities lacks it; therefore humanities breeds nonsense.” So my pushback there applies to him too.

Three problems with Saad’s “autocorrect” frame:

  1. Quine-Duhem cuts both ways. Engineering and economics also rescue failing theories with auxiliary hypotheses. Newtonian mechanics was saved by positing Neptune. Macroeconomists have been patching DSGE models for 40 years. The “reality will tell you you suck” story is cleaner in his clip than in actual practice.

  2. Selection bias on the examples. “Bridges built with indigenous physics” was never a serious proposal anywhere — it’s a strawman. Meanwhile, eugenics, lobotomy, frontal-lobe leucotomy, and Watsonian behaviourism all came out of “autocorrected” STEM, with real body counts. The autocorrect mechanism didn’t catch them for decades.

  3. STEM rests on humanities work. Frege wasn’t building bridges; modern logic and computer science exist because of him. Wittgenstein, Husserl, Tarski — all humanities-shaped. The “parasitic” frame can’t explain why your concept of truth, function, proof, or meaning didn’t come from a wind tunnel.

Now the Sinhala commenter (Ritigala Jayasena or whoever): partly right, partly too generous.

Right: Humanities operates on looping kinds and institutional facts — what I called the third category. Judging a literary or political theory by whether a bridge falls is a category error. “Imagined reality” does have its own validity conditions.

Too generous: The fact that something survives in constructed reality doesn’t automatically make it a novel idea. Some humanities work really is degenerate by its own standards — the self-sealing closures, the Lakatosian degenerating programs, the internal incoherence dressed as profundity. “Imagined reality” is not a free pass.

So both are too coarse. Saad collapses three cases (genuine degeneration, progressive interpretive deepening, self-sealing closure) into one bucket called parasitic. The commenter collapses them into one bucket called novel within imagined reality. The honest diagnostic isn’t STEM vs humanities or real vs imagined reality — it’s Lakatos: progressive vs degenerating program. Weber, Geertz, Polanyi pass. Some critical theory passes. Some doesn’t. Plenty of engineering and economics fails this test too.

On Orwell: the quote is real, from Notes on Nationalism (1945) — roughly “one has to belong to the intelligentsia to believe things like that; no ordinary man could be such a fool.” So Saad isn’t fabricating. But Orwell’s target was specifically nationalism among intellectuals, not humanities as a domain. The commenter’s “out of context” charge is half-fair: Orwell’s diagnosis is real, but Saad is laundering it into a broader anti-humanities argument Orwell himself never made.

Part Two

The anatomy of an abstract theory

The central question: whether a posit like gravity can be derived from measured quantities like mass and distance, how the concepts hook together, and whether structure means anything without empirical bearing.

07The anatomy of an abstract theory

The question the whole project began from.

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 7“But what is the anatomy of the abstract theory? Now, in gravitational theory, mass and length are properties that are created by generalizing observable phenomena. They are abstractions, but made by generalizing concrete stuff/properties.

But now, what is the concept of gravity, or the concept by Newton? What is its relation to the other generalized concepts within the gravitational theory?

Can gravitational force or field be derived from a generalization of mass or distance?”
Claude’s answer

Your question is sharper than it looks. The short answer: no, gravity cannot be derived by generalizing mass and distance. It’s a different kind of abstraction, and recognizing that opens up the real anatomy.

Two layers of abstraction in any physical theory:

Layer 1 — first-order abstractions (empirical generalizations). Mass, length, time, position, velocity. These come from observation by induction — many instances of “heaviness” collapse into the property mass; many instances of “extension” into length. They are measurable and reducible (in principle) to operations on observables.

Layer 2 — theoretical posits (not derived by generalization). Force, field, energy, potential, gravity itself. These are NOT extracted from observation by induction. They are introduced by the theorist to explain why the Layer 1 quantities correlate the way they do. You don’t see a force. You see an apple fall. You infer force using a theoretical framework that posits it.

So Newton’s gravity is not a generalization of mass and distance. It is a posited explanatory entity that relates them by a formal law:

F = G(m₁m₂/r²)

Here m and r are Layer 1 quantities. F is Layer 2. G is a parameter introduced to make the equation fit observations. The equation itself is mathematical structure — also imposed, not extracted.

Newton himself knew this. His famous “hypotheses non fingo” — I frame no hypotheses — meant: I describe how gravity behaves mathematically, but I refuse to say what gravity is. He was explicitly agnostic about whether “force” referred to anything real.

Einstein’s reformulation proves the point. In general relativity, there is no force of gravity. There is only spacetime curvature induced by mass-energy. So Newton’s “gravity-as-force” was a useful posit that got swapped out once a deeper theory arrived. Layer 1 stayed the same (apples fall, planets orbit). Layer 2 was replaced.

The actual anatomy of a physical theory:

  1. Empirical phenomena (things fall, springs oscillate)
  2. First-order abstractions (mass, length, time — derived by generalization)
  3. Theoretical posits (force, field, energy — introduced to explain, not extracted from data)
  4. Mathematical structure (the equations relating both — also imposed)
  5. Interpretive framework (what the posit means — instrumentalist, realist, structural realist)

Why this matters for everything we’ve discussed:

The Layer 1 / Layer 2 distinction undermines strong empiricism even inside physics. Gravity itself — the paradigm “gravity-like” abstraction from our money-vs-gravity discussion — has a non-empirical posit at its core. The measurable quantities (mass, distance) are empirical. The unifying concept (force, field, curvature) is a theoretical construct.

Humanities theories share the architecture exactly:

  • Layer 1: texts, behaviors, events, institutions
  • Layer 2: ideology, discourse, episteme, habitus, capital, hegemony — posits introduced to explain Layer 1
  • Mathematical structure replaced by hermeneutic / interpretive structure

The difference isn’t the presence of theoretical posits — both have them. The difference is in what kind of feedback the posits have to engage with. Physics gets quantitative prediction. Humanities gets interpretive illumination, internal coherence, and (via Lakatos) progressive vs degenerating fertility.

This is why Saad’s framing collapses once you see the structure: he treats Layer 2 in physics as if it were Layer 1, and Layer 2 in humanities as if it had no validity criteria at all. Both moves are wrong.

08How gravity “interacts” with mass

The structure of the concept, not physical interaction.

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 8“So how exactly do these abstract concepts like gravity “interact” with other generalized abstract concepts like mass? Not physical interaction — I am asking about the structure of the concept.”
Claude’s answer

The “interaction” is structural and definitional, not physical. Three nested ways the concepts hook into each other:

1. Through the equations (mathematical structure).

F = Gm₁m₂/r² isn’t merely a description — it is the conceptual link. “Gravity” and “mass” are partly defined by the position they occupy in this equation. Same with F = ma. Newton’s second law and his gravitational law are two equations that jointly constrain what each term can mean. You cannot shift the meaning of one without disturbing the other.

2. Through implicit definition (the Hilbert-style move).

In modern axiomatic theories, terms are not defined separately and then dropped into laws. The laws implicitly define the terms by specifying their structural role. “Mass” means: whatever quantity satisfies F=ma, F=Gm₁m₂/r², conservation of momentum, equivalence with energy — all at once. “Gravity” means: whatever links masses by the inverse-square law and produces accelerations consistent with F=ma.

This is the Ramsey-Lewis picture: take all of Newtonian mechanics, replace every theoretical term with a variable, existentially quantify the whole thing. The theoretical terms become placeholders for whatever uniquely satisfies the structural pattern. There is no Cartesian “Force-entity” sitting behind the structure — there is just what plays the force-role.

3. Through inferential roles (semantic holism).

Sellars and Brandom: a concept’s meaning is its place in the “space of reasons” — what follows from it, what supports it, what it excludes. “Gravity” inferentially connects to mass, acceleration, geodesics, time dilation, escape velocity, orbital period, energy. To know what “gravity” means is to know how it licenses inferences across this network.

A useful analogy: chess.

“Pawn” and “Bishop” don’t physically interact. They structurally interact through the rules — “a pawn promotes to a bishop on the eighth rank,” “a bishop captures a pawn diagonally.” The meaning of each piece is its role in the rule structure. Remove the rules and you don’t have two simpler chess concepts; you have wooden objects.

Mass and gravity are like this. Remove Newton’s laws (or GR’s field equations) and you don’t have two physical concepts standing alone — you have undefined symbols. The laws constitute the conceptual interaction.

The deeper point.

A theoretical posit like “gravity” has no meaning outside its structural relations. There is no concept-of-gravity-considered-by-itself that then enters into relations with the concept of mass. The concept is the bundle of structural relations. Worrall’s structural realism takes this seriously: what is real is the structure of relations; “force” and “mass” are nodes in that structure with no independent essence behind them.

So at the level of concept, gravity doesn’t “interact” with mass the way two billiard balls interact. Gravity is constituted by its lawful relation to mass (and to acceleration, distance, time, energy). The two “things” don’t pre-exist their interaction. They come into existence together as positions in a single relational web — defined by the network, not by themselves.

This is the same architecture you find in any rich theory, including humanities ones. “Ideology” doesn’t interact with “class” because ideology is some pre-existing thing that bumps into class. They are co-defined positions in a Marxist theoretical structure, just as “force” and “mass” are co-defined positions in a Newtonian one. The difference is in the kind of structure (mathematical-predictive vs hermeneutic-interpretive), not in whether posits exist independently of their structural roles. They never do.

09Theories without empirical bearing

Whether structure means anything without empirical bearing.

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 9“So, even the structural relations of an abstract theory wouldn’t have any meaning if they don’t have empirical bearing? We don’t just create abstract theories for fun?”
Claude’s answer

You’re partly right and partly wrong, and the cut between them matters.

The partly right part: an abstract theory completely disconnected from everything — no empirical bearing, no internal coherence, no connection to other inquiry, no conceptual clarification, no application even in principle — would be meaningless. Pure isolation = pure noise. So far so empiricist.

But the strong version of this collapses on examples.

Pure mathematicians often work on structures with no foreseeable empirical use. Hardy famously boasted that number theory had “no practical application whatever” — then RSA cryptography happened. Riemann developed non-Euclidean geometry as pure formal exploration; 60 years later Einstein needed exactly that to write general relativity. Galois group theory was abstract play for decades before becoming foundational to physics, chemistry, and cryptography.

If Hardy or Riemann had asked your question and answered “no, we shouldn’t do this for its own sake,” the empirical theories that later needed their work would have had no formal vocabulary available.

The right criterion isn’t direct empirical bearing but participation in some larger inquiry.

This is the Peirce / Lakatos position. Validity comes from being part of an ongoing network of investigation with feedback. The network includes:

  • Empirical testing (physics)
  • Internal logical consistency (math)
  • Inferential connections to tested theories (theoretical physics, applied math)
  • Conceptual clarification (philosophy)
  • Interpretive illumination of meaning-bearers (humanities)
  • Long-run accumulation of structures available for later use (pure math)

A theory has meaning if it participates in any of these networks. Empirical bearing in the strict sense is one mode of participation, not the only one.

On “for fun”:

You’re underestimating something here. A great deal of pure mathematics, foundational logic, and philosophical analysis IS done because the practitioner finds it beautiful or structurally compelling. Hardy literally said his work was justified by aesthetic value. Cantor explored transfinite arithmetic because the structures fascinated him. Gödel proved incompleteness because the limits of formal systems gripped him.

And this “for fun” exploration turns out to be cognitively essential. It populates a library of structures that later inquiry — including empirical inquiry — picks from. It’s the cognitive equivalent of evolutionary play: the species explores possibility-space without immediate purpose, and that exploration is what makes adaptation possible later. Without Riemann’s “useless” geometry, no Einstein.

The honest picture:

Theories need to engage with something with feedback — empirical reality, formal coherence, interpretive demand, conceptual challenge. They cannot be completely isolated. But the feedback can be delayed, indirect, formal, or interpretive. Your picture — “abstract theory → empirical test → meaning” — leaves out where most of mathematics, much of theoretical physics, all of philosophy, and most of humanities actually live and have always lived.

So: we don’t create theories purely for fun in the sense of arbitrary doodling. But we do create them often without immediate empirical purpose, and this is not a defect. It’s how the cognitive ecosystem actually functions. The fairy-tale verdict is reserved for theories that participate in no feedback structure of any kind — not for ones that haven’t paid off yet, or that participate in a non-empirical feedback structure.

Summary: The Gemini PDF on Theoretical Posits

Core thesis: Theoretical posits across both sciences and humanities acquire meaning not by induction from observation but through their structural role within an inferentially articulated network. The same architecture operates in both domains; what differs is the feedback mechanism that validates them.

1. The two-layer architecture and crisis of operationalism

Mature theories stratify into (a) first-order empirical abstractions (mass, length, time — coordinated with measurement) and (b) theoretical posits (force, field, gravity — introduced to explain regularities). Newtonian gravitation is the paradigm: observed orbits sit on layer one; gravitational force is a layer-two posit unifying them via F = Gm₁m₂/r². Bridgman’s operationalism tried to collapse the two layers by equating concepts with their measurement operations but failed because different operations (e.g., a ruler vs laser interferometry for length) would imply different concepts, blocking theoretical unification. Carnap’s bilateral reduction sentences partially repaired this but invited Hempel’s theoretician’s dilemma: if theoretical terms work, they’re dispensable; if they don’t, they’re useless. Hempel resolved it by appeal to inductive fertility — theoretical posits enable projections and unifications that flat observational language cannot.

2. The Ramsey-Lewis account

Ramsey (1929) proposed replacing each theoretical term with an existentially quantified variable, yielding a Ramsey sentence asserting that something satisfies the theory’s structural pattern. Lewis (1970) extended this with three explicit conditions: theoretical terms refer if there is a unique realization (CL1), are denotationless if there is no realization (CL2), and also denotationless if multiply realized (CL3). The ontological upshot: theoretical entities are not exotic posits but ordinary entities known relationally — defined by the role they play.

3. Implicit definition (Hilbert-style)

In Hilbert’s axiomatic geometry, “point” and “line” are defined only by the axioms that relate them. The same applies in physics: mass and force are co-defined by F = ma and F = Gm₁m₂/r²; the metric tensor gμν and stress-energy tensor Tμν are co-defined by Einstein’s field equations; the state vector and observables are co-defined by Hilbert space axioms, the Born rule, and the Schrödinger equation. Theoretical content is structural and role-dependent.

4. Semantic holism and inferentialism

Sellars (1956) attacked the “Myth of the Given”: sensory inputs cause but do not justify; knowledge belongs to the normative space of reasons. Brandom (1994) developed this into inferentialism — concepts are constituted by three inferential dimensions: commitment-preserving, entitlement-preserving, and incompatibility relations. The picture is holist: no concept exists in isolation. Quine’s “Two Dogmas” reinforces this: empirical significance attaches to whole theories, not isolated statements; theoretical posits are placeholders for organizing experience.

5. Structural realism and Newman’s objection

Worrall’s structural realism navigates between the no-miracles argument and the pessimistic meta-induction: theory change discards ontology but preserves mathematical structure (Fresnel → Maxwell). Two variants: epistemic SR (we know only relational structure) and ontic SR (Ladyman, French, Ross — structure is all there is). Newman’s objection trivializes naive SR: any domain of sufficient cardinality instantiates any pure structure. Three responses: (a) mixed predicates that resist Ramsification, (b) restriction to natural/causally significant relations, (c) OSR dissolution — by treating relations as physical/modal rather than set-theoretic, the trivialization cannot arise.

6. Parallel architectures in humanities

The essay maps the same architecture onto four canonical posits:

  • Althusser’s “Subject” — implicitly defined by interpellation through Ideological State Apparatuses; reconstructable as a Ramsey-Lewis functional placeholder
  • Bourdieu’s habitus — defined by its mediating role in (Habitus × Capital) + Field = Practice
  • Foucault’s episteme — implicitly defined by the rules of statement-formation it authorizes within a historical epoch
  • Gramsci’s hegemony — defined by its function in producing consent through civil-society institutions

All four operate inside closed inferential networks where changing one concept disturbs the others.

7. Validity criteria across domains

Lakatos’s methodology of scientific research programmes distinguishes progressive programmes (auxiliary hypotheses generate novel confirmed predictions) from degenerating ones (auxiliaries merely accommodate failures). Applied to classical Marxism: the failure of revolution in industrial states led to Althusserian and Gramscian additions to the protective belt — progressive or degenerating depending on whether they expand explanatory scope. Hacking’s looping kinds add a third criterion: reflexive normative adequacy. Because humanistic categories alter the populations they classify, validity must include the category’s capacity to remain explanatory after its objects have changed in response to being classified.

8. Isomorphisms and disanalogies

Isomorphisms: non-inductive derivation of posits; implicit coordination through the theoretical matrix; holistic inferential structure in a Sellarsian space of reasons.

Disanalogies: physical posits track mind-independent (indifferent) kinds, humanistic ones track mind-dependent (interactive) kinds; physical systems lack linguistic feedback loops, social systems exhibit them; physical theories aim at nomological prediction, humanistic theories at hermeneutic and emancipatory critique.

Conclusion

Verificationism’s demand that meaning reduce to observation failed even within classical physics. Once that demand is dropped, ontological legitimacy rests on inferential fertility and indispensability within a holistic model. Pure mathematics, physical theories, and humanistic frameworks all participate in the same rational enterprise — constructing inferentially articulated maps of reality. Humanities posits are not fairy tales; they are subject to rigorous criteria (Lakatosian progressive heuristics, reflexive normative adequacy) appropriate to their domain. The architecture is common; the feedback differs.

The two-layer architecture, refined.

Mature theories stratify into:

  • First-order empirical abstractions — mass, length, time. Derived by generalization from observables, tied partially (never completely) to measurement. Bridgman tried to make the tie complete via operationalism; the attempt failed because the same theoretical quantity is measurable by mutually divergent procedures (spring scale, torsion balance, accelerometer, gravitational lensing all measure “force” but share an object only because we already presuppose they are getting at the same theoretical role).
  • Theoretical posits — force, field, energy, gravity. NOT derived from observation by induction. Introduced as variables in equations that systematically generate observational predictions.

The distinction is porous (the observational terms of one theory may be theoretical from another’s standpoint) but real (the route to content genuinely differs).

Gravity cannot be derived from mass and distance.

Newton’s gravitational force is not a generalization of any observational pattern. It enters the theory as a posit in three equations that jointly fix what “force,” “mass,” and “gravity” mean:

  • F = ma (defines force by its acceleration-producing role on mass)
  • F = Gm₁m₂/r² (defines gravity by the inverse-square dependency)
  • ∇²Φ = 4πGρ (defines the gravitational potential field)

None of these is derivable from the others by induction. The equations are themselves imposed structure, not extracted patterns. Newton’s hypotheses non fingo — I describe how gravity behaves mathematically, I refuse to say what it is — is the founding acknowledgment of this. Einstein later swapped Newton’s gravity-as-force for spacetime curvature; Layer 1 observations stayed the same, the Layer 2 posit was replaced.

The “interaction” question now splits into three complementary forms.

This is where the research reports add the most. There is one architecture, but three formal mechanisms describing different facets of how mass and gravity “interact” as concepts:

(a) Referential interaction — Ramsey-Lewis. Take all of Newtonian mechanics, replace every theoretical term with a variable, existentially quantify the whole thing: ∃X₁…∃Xₙ T[X₁,…,Xₙ; observations]. “Mass” and “gravity” become placeholders. Lewis added precision: if there is a unique realizer (CL1), the terms refer; if no realizer (CL2) or multiple realizers (CL3), they fail to refer. So gravity and mass don’t pre-exist their interaction. They are defined together as the unique pair of quantities that jointly satisfy the theory’s structural commitments.

(b) Combinatorial interaction — Hilbert. The axioms in which terms jointly appear constitute their meaning. The Einstein field equations Gμν + Λgμν = (8πG/c⁴) Tμν simultaneously define the spacetime metric AND the stress-energy tensor; neither is given antecedently, and the equation cannot be tested by independent interpretation of the two sides. Frege’s challenge to Hilbert is the right pressure here: chess pieces are implicitly defined by their roles too. What distinguishes physics from chess? Answer: connection to the world via measurement discipline, which is external to the formal structure.

(c) Pragmatic interaction — Sellars-Brandom. A concept’s meaning is its inferential role. “Gravity” inferentially connects to mass, acceleration, geodesics, time dilation, escape velocity, orbital period, energy. Brandom’s three dimensions: commitment-preserving, entitlement-preserving, and incompatibility relations. To grasp gravity is to know how it functions in the deontic scorecard of physical reasoning.

The three accounts are not competitors. They formalize three faces of the same architecture: referential, combinatorial, pragmatic. A working theoretical posit is a node in all three respects simultaneously.

The architecture is technically fragile.

This is what the original answer hid. Newman’s 1928 objection: any sufficiently large domain can be organized to satisfy any purely structural description. So the Ramsey sentence is satisfied trivially given enough cardinality. Demopoulos and Friedman 1985 revived this; Ketland 2004 formalized it: a theory’s Ramsey sentence is true iff the theory is empirically adequate AND the cardinality condition is met. Without external constraint, the architecture collapses into “there exists a relation with the right structure” — which is a theorem of set theory, not a substantive claim about the world.

Three defenses keep physics afloat:

  • Mixed predicates that resist Ramsification (reintroduces the theory/observation distinction structural realism was supposed to bypass)
  • Intensional restriction to natural relations (Lewis 1984: only Lewis’s “elite” properties like mass and charge are eligible quantifiers)
  • OSR dissolution (Ladyman/French): structure is modal-causal, not set-theoretic, so trivial set-theoretic constructions don’t count

Psillos’s deeper challenge stands: structural preservation alone cannot underwrite the “no miracles” inference, because that inference requires genuine reference, not just isomorphism. The structural realist faces a dilemma — weaken the position enough to dodge pessimistic meta-induction and you lose the no-miracles ground; strengthen it enough to keep that ground and you reintroduce the burdens of full realism.

What ultimately protects physics from Newman collapse is external: the nomological discipline of measurement. Mass values in F = ma must match mass values in F = Gm₁m₂/r² to within experimental error, and that matching is enforced by procedures not themselves dependent on the equations. This external discipline is what makes Newtonian physics differ from chess piece semantics.

The Wigner puzzle.

If theoretical posits are constituted by inferential structure rather than read off the world, why does the structure track reality so well? Saying “math passes the inferential-discipline test” relocates the puzzle, not dissolves it. Why does inferential discipline track reality? Three open answers: the world is genuinely structural (OSR); the world admits multiple structural descriptions and we converge on usable ones (van Fraassen); cognition itself imposes the structure that experience must conform to (Kantian). The question is honestly open.

Putting it back together for your original questions:

  • Can gravity be derived from generalization of mass and distance? No. Gravity is a theoretical posit that enters the equations relating mass and distance; the equations themselves are imposed, not extracted.
  • How does gravity “interact” with mass at the level of concept?   • What is the anatomy of abstract theory? A two-layer structure where the second layer acquires meaning structurally through three nested mechanisms — referential, combinatorial, pragmatic — that together constitute the formal architecture. This architecture is technically fragile (Newman objection, Psillos’s reference challenge) and what protects it from collapse into triviality is external: the nomological discipline of measurement in physics; the absence of comparable discipline in humanities is exactly the disanalogy that makes the social-theoretic application of the same architecture epistemically looser.

The deepest point: there is no concept-of-gravity-considered-by-itself that then enters into relations with the concept of mass. They come into existence together as positions in a single relational web — defined by the network, protected from triviality by external measurement discipline, and tracking reality for reasons the structural account itself cannot fully explain.

The dilemma looks devastating but rests on a false dichotomy. The resolution comes in three connected moves.

Move 1: Distinguish deductive from inductive systematization (Hempel’s own response).

Hempel himself solved it in the same 1958 paper. The dilemma assumes theoretical posits earn their keep only by deductively connecting observation sentences. If that were the only work they did, Craig’s theorem (1953) shows they’re formally eliminable — any theory with theoretical terms can be replaced by an observational theory yielding the same deductive consequences.

But theoretical posits also do inductive work — projecting regularities into new cases not yet observed. The Craigian transform handles deductive relations among the existing observational record but cannot replicate inductive projections, because it has no machinery for “unobserved cases that would behave like this if our posit is correct.” So even where deductive eliminability holds de jure, the inductive content of the theoretical apparatus has no observational surrogate. The second horn — “empirically ungrounded” — collapses: the inductive content is empirically constrained (it makes predictions that can fail), it just isn’t empirically reducible.

Move 2: Theoretical posits carry surplus meaning that future inquiry cashes out.

This is the historical answer. Maxwell’s electromagnetic field, when introduced, “did more” than summarize then-available observations of electricity and magnetism. Critics (Mach, Duhem) accused it of being ungrounded — pure mathematical fiction. Maxwell’s equations then predicted electromagnetic waves nobody had observed. Hertz observed them in 1887. The surplus content turned out to be empirically substantive; it just couldn’t be cashed out at the moment of introduction.

This generalizes. Theoretical posits carry inferential commitments — predictions, structural implications, connections to other theories — that exceed their current empirical anchoring but remain testable in principle. The dilemma’s second horn presupposes that “doing more than summarizing observables” means “doing something never testable.” That’s false. It means “doing something whose tests have not yet been run.”

Move 3: The Quinean dissolution.

The dilemma also presupposes that posits can be evaluated in isolation — that we can ask of each theoretical term “what observations does it summarize, and what does it add?” Quine’s “Two Dogmas of Empiricism” denies this. The unit of empirical significance is the whole theory, not the individual statement. Theoretical posits face the tribunal of experience collectively, as part of a web of belief whose total observational consequences are what get tested.

On this view, asking “is gravity an unnecessary summary or an ungrounded posit?” is malformed. Gravity is a node in Newtonian mechanics. Newtonian mechanics as a whole has observational consequences. If those consequences match observation, the whole web is empirically supported, including its theoretical nodes. If they don’t, the whole web is in trouble, and we can decide where to repair it. Individual posits aren’t separately summaries-or-ungrounded; they’re load-bearing components of an empirically constrained network.

The combined resolution.

Theoretical posits are neither summaries of observables nor ungrounded inventions. They are inferentially articulated nodes in a network that:

  • Establishes inductive projections no observational surrogate can replicate (Hempel)
  • Carries surplus content that later inquiry tests (the Maxwell-Hertz pattern)
  • Faces empirical constraint holistically rather than statement-by-statement (Quine)

The dilemma’s mistake is treating empirical grounding as a property of individual terms rather than a property of total theoretical structures with observational consequences. Once you fix that mistake, the dilemma dissolves. Theoretical posits “do more” than summarize observables (Move 1, 2) while remaining empirically constrained (Move 3). The disjunction was never exclusive.

A closing note worth keeping in view: this resolution buys the legitimacy of theoretical posits, not their guaranteed reference. A posit can be empirically constrained, do inductive work, carry surplus meaning, and still turn out to refer to nothing (phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether). The dilemma is solved at the level of methodology — we are entitled to introduce theoretical posits — without settling what those posits ultimately track. That further question is what structural realism, the pessimistic meta-induction, and Psillos’s reference challenge are about.

17Is Newton’s move circular?

Whether describing gravity through mass and acceleration is circular.

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 17“So basically, Newton is saying there is something called gravity, but he is describing how its first-order observables behave mathematically. First we generalize observables to create mass and acceleration. Then we create a second-order abstraction to explain their behaviour. But Newton is saying that although we can’t know this thing — the second-order abstract concept — directly, what he is doing is again describing gravity’s properties, like force, in relation to first-order observables like mass and acceleration, as F = ma? So basically, this is a sort of circular reasoning?”
Claude’s answer

You’ve put your finger on something real — and it’s exactly the worry that’s haunted philosophy of science since the failure of operationalism. The answer is: yes, there IS a circularity, but it’s non-vicious for specific reasons that distinguish theoretical knowledge from tautology.

The circle, made explicit.

Stated minimally, Newton’s move looks exactly as you describe:

  • We notice masses accelerate
  • We posit “force” to explain this
  • We define force by F = ma
  • So “force is whatever produces acceleration in mass”
  • Which is a renamed redescription of the original observation

If this were the whole picture, the worry would be devastating. Newton would have invented a label for what we already knew, and the theoretical posit would carry no content.

What breaks the circle: the constraint network.

The structure isn’t F = ma alone. It’s:

  • F = ma (force in terms of acceleration)
  • F = Gm₁m₂/r² (force in terms of distance)
  • F = −dV/dx (force in terms of potential gradient)
  • Conservation of momentum (constrains all forces collectively)
  • Newton’s third law (forces come in equal/opposite pairs)
  • And so on

“Force” is now constrained by all of these simultaneously. Consider an apple falling. The force on it can be calculated three ways:

  • From its mass and observed acceleration (F = ma)
  • From Earth’s mass, the apple’s mass, and their distance (F = Gm₁m₂/r²)
  • From the gradient of gravitational potential

These three calculations must give the same answer, within experimental error. This is not a definition. It is an empirical claim that could fail, and if it failed the whole theory would collapse. It doesn’t fail in the classical regime, and that non-failure is genuinely informative — it’s not a tautology, it’s a discovery.

Same point about mass. Mass measured inertially (the m in F = ma) must equal mass measured gravitationally (the m in F = Gm₁m₂/r²). This is the equivalence principle. It’s not a definitional truth. Newton assumed it and was right by accident; Einstein recognized it as a deep empirical fact requiring explanation, which led to general relativity. Had inertial and gravitational mass come apart, the “circular” definition would have shown its cracks — and they could have come apart in principle.

The Ramsey-Lewis formalization of why this works.

Instead of defining force by any single equation, write the Ramsey sentence of the whole theory:

∃X (X satisfies F = ma AND F = Gm₁m₂/r² AND conservation laws AND third law AND …)

“Force” does not denote the first equation. It denotes whatever uniquely satisfies the conjunction. The conjunction is testable as a whole. If the conjunction has no realizer (the equations are jointly inconsistent with experience), the theory is empirically refuted. If it has a unique realizer, the term refers.

So the apparent circularity is broken by the totality of constraints. No single equation defines force; the whole network does, and the whole network has predictions that go beyond any individual equation.

Vicious vs non-vicious circles.

A vicious circle is one where the system is internally consistent but compatible with anything. Chess piece semantics is like this — bishops are defined by what bishops can do, and there’s no external check. Any move you can make with bishops is by definition a bishop-move.

A non-vicious circle is one where the system is internally constrained AND has external predictions that can fail. Newtonian mechanics passes this test in the classical regime — its predictions about Mercury’s orbit, for instance, turned out to be wrong (the perihelion precession anomaly), which is part of what led to GR. The circle had external traction; reality could push back on it.

Test: does the whole system make predictions that exceed its definitional content? If yes, non-vicious. If no, vicious.

Newton’s own awareness.

This is exactly what hypotheses non fingo means. Newton didn’t claim to know what gravity IS. He claimed only that gravity behaves mathematically in such-and-such a way, and that the mathematical behavior generates correct predictions across many cases that weren’t built into the original equations. The theoretical posit is implicitly defined by its role; the role is empirically constrained by the totality of predictions, including predictions that go beyond the originating observations.

The deeper point you’ve stumbled onto.

All theoretical knowledge has this structure. There is no theoretical concept that is independently anchored outside its inferential network. The operationalist dream of breaking this circularity by tying every concept directly to one observation failed — both because no single operation captures a theoretical concept (force can be measured by spring scales, torsion balances, accelerometers, gravitational lensing), and because the same operation can implement different concepts depending on theoretical context.

The honest position: theoretical concepts are constituted by their role in a network; the network is tested holistically; what protects this from tautology is the predictive overhang — the equations have to work in cases we haven’t measured yet, including cases we hadn’t imagined when we set up the equations.

That overhang is what makes the circle non-vicious. It’s also what failed for phlogiston and caloric — those theories had circular definitions whose predictive overhang turned out to be wrong, and the theories died. The mark of an empty theoretical posit isn’t circularity (every theoretical posit is circular in your sense). It’s circularity without predictive overhang.

So: yes, Newton’s move is circular. But the circularity is the structural feature of all theoretical knowledge. What separates good theoretical work from empty re-labeling is whether the closed loop of definitions also generates open predictions that the world can refuse.

Realism, structure and the hidden thing

A Saturn V launch raises the question of what gravity represents. Psillos’s challenge, the structure-of-structures objection, and a machine that predicts without posits lead to dropping the noumenon itself.

19Saturn V and the noumenon

Why a rocket reaches the Moon, and what that says about gravity.

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 19“OK, here is another thing that came into my mind. Let’s say we are sending a Saturn V rocket to the Moon. So that is a concrete event.

And we plan, calculate, infer or predict it using second-order and first-order abstractions.

And for real, it happened.

Now, what happened here in this scenario? We create first-order abstractions like mass by generalizing, and then we create some abstract hypotheses as causes or reasons. Then we assume their effects — that means the effects of a second-order abstraction like gravity on a first-order abstraction like mass — can be measured or observed.

And we also assume the “uniform continuity” of these effects and concepts.

But Saturn V shows those assumptions work.

Am I correct?

But why does it work? Does gravity represent some absolute reality that cannot be accessed by any modern tool? (Note: this is not a human limit of cognition like Kant is saying, but maybe what it refers to is close to Kant’s noumena?)

But then again, the same observables can be predicted by another, different second-order abstraction, like general relativity?

So it doesn’t represent anything in itself?”
Claude’s answer

Your reasoning is largely right, and the question at the end is one of the deepest in philosophy of science. Let me work through it carefully.

Your analysis of the Saturn V case is correct.

We use first-order abstractions (mass, distance, velocity) and second-order posits (force, gravity) to predict the rocket’s trajectory. The prediction works. This is the no-miracles argument in its most concrete form: if our theoretical posits weren’t tracking something real, how could they be so reliably predictive?

You’re also right about the “uniform continuity” assumption. This is the principle of induction — that laws holding in tested cases hold in new cases. Hume showed this principle cannot be justified empirically without circularity. We assume it because we have to.

But your follow-up observation is the killing move against naive realism.

The same Saturn V trajectory can be predicted by Newtonian mechanics (force, gravitational attraction at a distance) and by general relativity (spacetime curvature, no force at all). These two theories are ontologically incompatible: Newton says there’s a force pulling the rocket; Einstein says there is no force, the rocket follows a geodesic in curved spacetime. Yet both predict the trajectory accurately enough to land on the moon.

This is theory underdetermination by evidence, and it’s one of the deepest results in philosophy of science. If two incompatible ontologies can predict the same observables, in what sense does either represent what’s really there?

Three responses to this:

1. Scientific realism (Boyd, Psillos). Successful theories approximately track reality. The only explanation for predictive success is reference — Newton’s “force” approximately refers to something even though it’s been superseded; Einstein’s “curvature” refers more accurately; we approach truth asymptotically.

Problem: Laudan’s pessimistic meta-induction. The history of science is a graveyard of “approximately referring” terms that turned out to refer to nothing — phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether, vital forces. By induction, our current terms are probably similarly empty.

2. Constructive empiricism (van Fraassen). Theories aim at empirical adequacy, not truth. The Saturn V case shows Newton is empirically adequate for that domain. Whether “force” refers is irrelevant. Theoretical posits are useful instruments.

Problem: this can’t explain why empirical adequacy in one domain generates empirical adequacy in another. Maxwell’s equations predicted electromagnetic waves nobody had observed; Hertz then observed them. If the equations were just instruments, why did they unlock genuinely new empirical content?

3. Structural realism (Worrall, Ladyman). What’s preserved across theory change is mathematical structure. Newton’s inverse-square law isn’t discarded by Einstein — it emerges as a limiting case of Einstein’s field equations in weak gravitational fields and slow speeds. So whatever reality is, it has the structure that makes both theories approximately correct in their regimes. The entities (force vs curvature) are interchangeable descriptions of the structure.

This is the strongest position, but it faces Psillos’s challenge: structural preservation alone doesn’t underwrite reference. Knowing structure is not knowing what’s structured.

Now to your Kantian intuition — which is sharp.

You ask: “Is gravity some absolute reality not accessible by any modern tool? Is it close to Kant’s noumena?”

This is genuinely the question. Four positions:

(a) Kantian reading. Newton and Einstein both give us phenomena — reality as it appears under conceptual schemes. Whether either captures the noumenon is impossible to answer. The fact that two ontologically different schemes produce the same phenomena confirms Kant: access is filtered, and we cannot peek behind the filter.

(b) Structural realist response to Kant. Worrall would say: we do have access to the noumena — to their structure. The mathematical structure preserved across theory change is not an artifact of our scheme; it’s what makes both Newton and Einstein approximately work.

(c) Ontic structural realism. Ladyman/French go further: the question “is reality really forces or really curvature?” is malformed. Reality has no intrinsic nature beneath its modal-structural properties. Both theories describe the same underlying structure with different conceptual handles. Asking which is “really real” presupposes a substance-level reality that doesn’t exist.

(d) Correlationist reading (Meillassoux). Newton and Einstein are both correlationist gestures — they describe reality only as it correlates with our schemes. The Saturn V case doesn’t break correlationism; it shows multiple correlation-structures yielding the same phenomenal output. Meillassoux’s project: find a way out, by identifying mathematical statements that hold independent of any conceptual scheme.

Where this leaves us.

Your conclusion — “so it doesn’t represent any thing in itself?” — is one defensible reading. The honest position is more nuanced:

  1. Empirical adequacy doesn’t entail reference. Saturn V working doesn’t prove “force” exists. Strict realism overshoots.
  2. But empirical adequacy isn’t nothing. That two schemes converge on the same predictions for moon trajectories, light bending, Mercury’s precession, gravitational waves — this suggests there IS something both schemes are partial perspectives on. Strict instrumentalism undersells what’s going on.
  3. Structure is the safest realist commitment. The inverse-square dependence, the relationship between mass and energy, the geometry of geodesics — these are preserved across theory change. They are candidates for “what’s really there.” Not Kant’s noumenal substance; the relational structure.
  4. Newton’s “force” probably doesn’t refer; the structure it captures does. When Einstein superseded Newton, force-talk became a useful fiction in the classical regime. Curvature-talk might similarly become fiction in a future deeper theory. What survives is the mathematical relationship.
  5. The Kantian intuition is half-right. We don’t know the intrinsic nature of reality. But we may know its structure. So Kant’s noumena are partially accessible — through what structural realism preserves — but not fully. Modern philosophy has weakened the strict Kantian dichotomy without abolishing it.

One important wrinkle for your correlationism work:

The Newton-vs-Einstein situation is a perfect test case for Meillassoux. The strict correlationist says: we never break out of the correlation; different schemes give different correlations; reality-in-itself remains inaccessible. But Meillassoux argues that mathematical statements about ancestral facts — facts that obtained before any consciousness existed to correlate with — must be objectively true in a non-correlationist sense.

Your Saturn V case actually demonstrates this. The moon was where it was, with the gravitational properties it has, billions of years before any rocket. The mathematical relationships describing its trajectory are not just our scheme; they are constraints that hold on a reality predating us. Different theoretical schemes capture these constraints with different conceptual handles. The constraints themselves are not constituted by our schemes.

This is the position that may settle out as defensible: structurally realist about constraints, anti-realist about intrinsic entities. Newton’s force may not refer; Einstein’s curvature may not refer; but the inverse-square dependency of attraction on distance, in the relevant regime, does refer — to a structural feature of reality holding independent of our descriptions of it.

That’s the position that survives both Psillos’s challenge and Kant’s restriction. It’s also exactly what your work on Meillassoux is groping toward — and the Saturn V case is actually a stronger version of the arche-fossil argument than Meillassoux’s original example. He needed prehistoric objects; you just need the fact that two ontologically incompatible theories converge on the same predictions, which means the convergence itself must be tracking something neither theory exclusively owns.

20Psillos’s challenge, simply

Why structure alone may not explain success.

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 20
This is the strongest position, but it faces Psillos’s challenge: structural preservation alone doesn’t underwrite referenceQuoted from the previous answer
“What is this? Explain deeply but simply.”
Claude’s answer

Psillos’s challenge in one sentence: structural realism wants to keep what our successful theories preserve (mathematical structure) without committing to what makes them work (reference to actual things). And you can’t have one without the other.

Why be a scientific realist at all?

The motivation is the no-miracles argument: it would be a miracle if our theories made such accurate predictions unless they were tracking something real. Newton’s planetary predictions, Maxwell’s electromagnetic waves, GR’s Mercury precession — these would be inexplicable cosmic luck if our theories weren’t getting reality right. So we infer: our theories track reality, and their theoretical terms refer to real things. That’s why they work.

Why structural realism tried to retreat.

The pessimistic meta-induction pushed back: phlogiston, caloric, the ether — central terms of past successful theories turned out to refer to nothing. By induction, our current terms (“force,” “field,” “wavefunction”) will probably also be empty in some future theory.

Structural realism’s clever escape: only commit to structure, not entities. The ether didn’t survive, but Fresnel’s equations for light propagation did, embedded in Maxwell. Newton’s gravitational law survives as a limiting case of Einstein. So believe the structure, stay agnostic about the entities. This seems to give you the no-miracles intuition (something is preserved) while dodging the pessimistic meta-induction (entities can come and go).

Psillos’s challenge.

The no-miracles argument requires reference, not just structure. Here’s the simple way to see it:

Imagine you have a map. The map has structure (lines arranged in certain patterns at certain distances). The map also has reference (each line picks out an actual street). Why does the map work — why can you use it to navigate? Because the lines refer to streets. If the lines were merely an abstract pattern that happened to be isomorphic to the city, it would be a coincidence that you could use it to navigate. A miracle, in fact.

The same abstract pattern could be isomorphic to many different cities, or to no city at all. What makes it a map of this city is the reference relation between its symbols and the actual streets.

Now transpose this to scientific theories. The “structure” structural realism wants to preserve is an abstract relational pattern. But abstract patterns can be isomorphic to many things, or to nothing. What explains a theory’s predictive success — what makes it track reality — is that its terms pick out features of the world that have the structural relations the theory describes.

So Psillos’s move: if you want to explain why the theory works, you can’t appeal to structure alone. You need reference. But reference is precisely what structural realism was trying to avoid committing to.

The dilemma.

This puts the structural realist in a bind with no exit:

  • Weaken your position enough to dodge the pessimistic meta-induction (only structure, not entities) → you lose the no-miracles ground, because mere structure can’t explain predictive success without reference.
  • Strengthen it enough to keep the no-miracles ground (reference of terms) → you’re back to full scientific realism with all the original problems.

Structural realism wants to occupy a middle position that doesn’t exist. The motivation that brought you to realism (no miracles) won’t stop short of reference. The reasons for retreating from full realism (pessimistic meta-induction) push past structure all the way to instrumentalism. There’s nothing stable in between.

Why this matters for the architecture analysis.

This is the unresolved fault line under everything we’ve been building. The “same architecture, different feedback” thesis rests on structural realism providing a defensible middle position. Psillos’s challenge says that middle position is unstable.

If Psillos is right, the architecture analysis vindicates humanities theories only to the extent that structural realism in physics is itself defensible. And structural realism’s defense remains contested. So the whole defense — “humanities participates in the same architecture as physics” — is built on a foundation that physicists themselves haven’t fully secured.

This doesn’t refute the architecture analysis. It shows the analysis is borrowing the credibility of a position whose own credibility is in dispute. The defenders of humanities theory using structural realism inherit not just its strengths but also its scandals — including this one, which is the standing scandal of the structuralist tradition.

21The structure of structures

Charith’s own objection to structural realism.

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 21“But there is also another issue with structural realism. Let S be the structure of a theory. Now, the generalization and abstraction of these S-type structures is also a structure; let’s call it mS. But this structure of structures is only valid because we observe that the structures of theories are preserved. In other words, in order for mS to be possible, something other than structure alone is necessary.”
Claude’s answer

You’ve put your finger on something serious — and it’s actually distinct from both Newman’s objection and Psillos’s challenge, though it’s in the same family. Let me reconstruct the argument and then take it seriously.

Your argument, formalized:

  • P1. Structural realism claims we can only know structure.
  • P2. The thesis “structure is preserved across theory change” is itself a knowledge claim — call it mS.
  • P3. For mS to be established, we have to observe that structures actually get preserved — comparing Fresnel to Maxwell, Newton to Einstein, and so on.
  • P4. That observation is not itself purely structural. To recognize two theories as sharing structure, we need to identify what counts as a “theory,” what counts as a “successor,” what counts as the “same” relational pattern across different formalisms. These are non-structural judgments.
  • Conclusion: Structural realism’s foundational claim requires non-structural knowledge, contradicting its own central thesis.

Why this is sharper than Newman.

Newman’s objection works at the object level: any sufficiently large domain can be organized to satisfy any abstract structure, so individual structural claims about reality threaten to become trivial.

Your objection works at the meta level: the very thesis that licenses structural realism (mS) cannot itself be a structural claim without infinite regress; but if it’s not a structural claim, then structural realism rests on non-structural foundations.

This is the same diagnostic that killed logical positivism. The verification principle (“every meaningful claim must be empirically verifiable or analytic”) was itself neither. It cut off the branch it was sitting on. Your argument applies the same move to structural realism: the claim “we can only know structure” requires knowing something non-structural (that structures actually persist across the history of science). It saws off its own foundation.

Structural realist responses, evaluated.

Response 1: mS is itself a structural claim at the meta-level — a “structure of structures.”
This just pushes the problem up. What validates THIS meta-meta-structural claim? An mS² claim? Infinite regress.

Response 2: Distinguish object-level structural realism from meta-level epistemology. The meta-level operates outside the scope of the thesis.
Ad hoc. If we have non-structural knowledge at the meta-level, why not at the object level? You need a principled reason for the scope restriction, and none is forthcoming.

Response 3: mS is an empirical-historical observation about science.
Even on this reading, it concedes your point: structural realism rests on non-structural empirical knowledge. The position is no longer a pure thesis about the limits of knowledge; it becomes a methodological recommendation backed by historical induction. Much weaker than it sounded.

Response 4 (ontic structural realism’s move): Reality is fundamentally relational; everything we know reflects that.
This shifts the problem: how do we know reality is structural all the way down? Through structural observation? That’s circular. The OSR commitment is itself a metaphysical claim that cannot be grounded by the kind of access it postulates.

What survives.

I think your argument lands. The honest version of structural realism that survives is not “all we can know is structure” but something more modest:

“Given the historical observation that mathematical structure tends to be preserved across theory change while entities tend to be replaced, we have inductive reason to prefer structural commitments to entity commitments — but this is a methodological preference grounded in non-structural historical observation, not a strict thesis about the limits of knowledge.”

This is not really structural realism anymore. It’s structural pragmatism. It says: structure is what science has historically been good at preserving, so bet on it.

Notice what this preserves and what it loses:

  • Preserves: a defensible reason to prefer structural over entity commitments in scientific reasoning
  • Loses: the strong epistemological thesis that structure is somehow specially accessible while entities aren’t

Where this lands us in the broader picture.

Combined with Psillos’s challenge, your argument pushes structural realism into a corner where it must either:

(a) Concede it’s a methodological position, not an epistemological thesis (losing strong realist commitments)
(b) Accept it requires non-structural foundations (contradicting itself)
(c) Retreat to ontic structural realism and accept that “reality is structure all the way down” is a metaphysical commitment that cannot be grounded through structural knowledge alone (becoming a faith commitment)

None is fatal, but none delivers what strong structural realism wanted. The honest landing place is closer to “structurally realist about constraints, anti-realist about intrinsic entities, methodologically grounded rather than epistemologically certain.”

Connection to your correlationism work.

This matters for your broader project. The structural realist tried to claim a form of access to reality beyond the correlation — a way to know the world’s structure even if not its intrinsic nature. Your argument shows that even this limited access is partly constituted by the correlation: by our judgments about what counts as preserved structure, what counts as a successor theory, what counts as identity of relational pattern across different formalisms.

The correlation is harder to escape than the structural realist thought. Every attempt to claim direct access to mind-independent reality has so far required some non-structural anchor that pulls the position back inside the correlation. Meillassoux saw this; that’s why he tried to ground non-correlationist knowledge in absolute contingency rather than structure. Whether that move succeeds is a separate question, but your argument confirms what he diagnosed: structuralist escapes from correlationism do not work as advertised.

What you’ve identified is essentially a hidden Kantian moment in structural realism — the recognition that the very framework that lets us identify “structure” is itself a contribution of the knowing subject, not a transparent window onto the world. That’s a sharper diagnosis than most of the literature manages.

22Prediction without posits

What a machine that predicts without theory shows.

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 22“So let’s give this data to an ML model. It will make the same prediction without a second-order abstract theory. How?”
Claude’s answer

You’ve identified one of the most important questions in current philosophy of science. Let me work through it carefully.

Yes, ML can do this. Here’s how.

Give a neural network the inputs (initial positions, velocities, masses) and outputs (final positions over time) for thousands of projectile launches, orbital motions, or rocket trajectories. The network learns weights that map inputs to outputs accurately enough to predict new cases. It never represents “gravity” as such. No node says “force.” No edge says “inverse-square.” It just learns a function.

AlphaFold predicts protein folding without using explicit physics. Modern ML weather models outperform physics-based weather models. Neural networks have been trained to predict planetary trajectories from data alone, getting Kepler’s laws as emergent behavior without ever being told them.

So the prediction half of science can be done without explicit theoretical posits. This is real.

Three things this seems to imply that threaten theoretical posits:

  1. Predictive success doesn’t require theoretical commitment. If ML predicts as well as Newton without believing in force, then predictive success alone doesn’t license the no-miracles inference to theoretical entities. This boosts van Fraassen’s constructive empiricism: theories aim at empirical adequacy; theoretical posits are useful instruments, not pictures of reality.

  2. Theoretical posits look like one compression technology among many. Newton’s force compresses planetary motion into a few equations. A neural network compresses the same data into a few million weights. Different technologies, similar function: both reduce vast empirical regularities to compact representations.

  3. The “Wigner question” sharpens. Why does ML work? Because the world has stable regularities. But that’s exactly why theoretical posits work. So both face the same explanatory burden, and neither has special claim to “explaining the success of the other.”

Three things this does NOT imply:

  1. ML weights ARE a kind of theoretical posit, just opaque ones. The trained network encodes structural relations among inputs and outputs. Those relations are internally similar to what Newton’s equations encode. The structural realist gets to say: ML and Newton both capture the same underlying structure, just in different representations. The relations are real; the specific encodings (force vs weights vs curvature) are interchangeable. This actually supports structural realism in its modest form.

  2. ML doesn’t explain; it predicts. Newton’s force doesn’t just predict falling apples — it tells you why the apple falls the way the moon orbits. It unifies disparate phenomena under a single explanatory framework. ML gives you a function from inputs to outputs. It doesn’t tell you that planetary motion, tidal forces, falling apples, and pendulum swings are the same thing. That unification is what theoretical posits provide and ML doesn’t.

  3. ML doesn’t transfer across domains the way theory does. Train a network on apple trajectories — it predicts apple trajectories. It doesn’t generalize to galaxy clusters. Newton’s gravity does. Theoretical posits earn their keep by being applicable far beyond their training data, because they’re not just compressing data; they’re claiming a structural relation that holds whenever certain conditions obtain. ML generalizes only by retraining on new data.

What this actually shows:

ML and theoretical posits are doing structurally similar work — both compress regularities into representations — but at different levels:

  • ML operates in the space of causes and effects: inputs map to outputs.
  • Theoretical posits operate in the space of reasons: not just “this input yields this output” but “this output occurs because mass m₁ exerts force F on mass m₂ across distance r.”

This is exactly Sellars’s distinction between the space of causes and the space of reasons. ML lives in the first. Newton lives in the second. The space of reasons enables inferential moves — analogical reasoning, transfer to new domains, conceptual revision, explanation — that the space of causes does not.

Whether the space of reasons is just our cognitive imposition on the space of causes, or whether reality itself has a reason-like structure that we discover, is exactly the unresolved question structural realism has been trying to settle.

The deeper philosophical lesson:

Your example reframes the realism debate. The traditional question was: “Are theoretical posits real?” The new question is: “What do theoretical posits do that other compression technologies don’t, and does that ‘doing’ track something real about the world or only something useful for us?”

Three positions emerge:

(a) Instrumentalist position (newly strengthened): ML proves theoretical posits are just one compression tool. Use whichever works. There’s nothing special about Newton’s “force” — it’s a particular human-cognitive way of compressing regularities, like an ML model is a particular silicon-computational way.

(b) Structural realist position (still defensible): ML weights and Newton’s equations capture the same underlying structure. The structure is real; specific encodings are not. ML doesn’t threaten structural realism; it confirms it by showing structure can be encoded in multiple ways.

(c) Inferentialist position (interesting new ground): ML and theoretical posits operate at different levels because human inquiry requires reasons, not just predictions. Theoretical posits are real not as substances but as nodes in a discursive practice without which scientific progress isn’t possible. They’re not in the world; they’re in the practice. But the practice is real and consequential.

Tying back to your project:

Your ML observation cuts particularly hard against position (a) — actually no, it strengthens (a). It cuts hard against naive scientific realism. But it doesn’t refute the more modest positions. What it does is force a sharper question: when we say theoretical posits “track reality,” do we mean they track reality, or do we mean the structures they encode track reality?

If only the structures track reality, then ML and Newton are doing the same thing differently, and there’s nothing privileged about Newton’s representation. If theoretical posits themselves track reality beyond their structural content, then ML is doing something importantly less than Newton — and the burden is on you to say what that “more” is.

This is genuinely open. And it’s why current philosophy of ML in science (Pietsch, Sullivan, Boge, Creel) has become unexpectedly important to the structural realism debate. Your question is exactly the question the field is now confronting.

The honest answer: ML success shows that the predictive part of theoretical knowledge doesn’t require theoretical posits. It does NOT show that the explanatory, unifying, and inferential parts can be replaced — but it does show that those parts are doing something separable from prediction, and that we need a better account of what that “something” is.

Claude’s answer

Both of your points are sharp, and the second one is, I think, the most important thing you’ve arrived at in this whole conversation. Let me take them in turn, because the first sets up the second.

On your first point, you are exactly right, and you have caught a real slippage in what I said earlier. When I spoke of “validation by prediction” as the tool that grounds theoretical posits, I was quietly running together two things that your ML example forces apart. There is the empirical adequacy of a theory — its ability to generate correct predictions about observables — and there is the theoretical content of a theory — its claim that force, or gravity, or curvature is what underlies those observables. Prediction validates the first. It does not validate the second. And the proof is precisely the machine: the network achieves the empirical adequacy while carrying none of the theoretical content. So if successful prediction really certified the existence of gravity-as-entity, then the ML model, which makes the same predictions while committing to nothing, would have to certify it too, which is absurd. The conclusion is unavoidable. Successful prediction confirms only the regularity-tracking layer that theory and ML share. The posits ride on top of that layer, and whatever justifies them, it is not their predictive success, because that success is not theirs alone.

This is van Fraassen’s point made vivid by a machine. Prediction was never the thing that made gravity-talk special, so you are right to demote it. Whatever is special about gravity-like abstractions — explanation, unification, inferential transfer — none of it is certified by the prediction test. Those features would have to be justified, if at all, by entirely different criteria: how much they unify, how far they transfer, how fertile they prove. That is a genuine reorganization of the picture, and you reached it on your own.

Now to your second point, which goes to the root, and which I want to handle carefully.

You are suggesting that the whole anxiety — what do theoretical posits track, do they reach the real thing or fail to — only has content if we assume there is a real thing, a hidden reality, a noumenon, an absolute, sitting behind the phenomena, which our theories are straining toward and possibly missing. And you propose: drop that assumption. If there is no thing-in-itself, there is nothing for theories to fail to capture, and the worry simply evaporates.

I want to say first, plainly, that this is a serious and historically major move, not a naive one. You are reinventing a position that runs from Hume through the logical positivists to Nietzsche and Rorty. Nietzsche put it most memorably: when we abolish the “true world” behind appearances, we are not left with a mere “apparent world,” because “apparent” only ever meant apparent-as-opposed-to-true. Abolish the hidden world and the very distinction between real and apparent collapses; we are left simply with what there is. The positivists made the same move more dryly. A question that no possible observation could ever bear on — “does gravity exist over and above all of its observable effects?” — is on their view not a hard question but a pseudo-question, a string of words shaped like a question that in fact asks nothing. On this reading your deflation is not a loss of knowledge but a release from a confusion.

But here I have to show you a fork in the road, because “drop the noumenon” can mean two very different things, and they meet very different fates.

The strong version says: there is no reality at all, only our predictions and tools, and “the world” is just the shadow our instruments cast. This version runs into three hard problems, and you need to feel the weight of the third in particular, because it is your own.

The first problem is error. If theories track nothing, what does it mean to say that phlogiston theory was wrong? It predicted things, it was useful for a while, and then it was dropped. But “wrong” seems to mean more than “we stopped using it.” It seems to mean the world was not as the theory said it was — and that requires a world that was some definite way, independently of the theory, which is exactly what the strong deflation denies.

The second problem is novel prediction. Maxwell’s equations implied electromagnetic waves that no one had built into them, and Hertz then found them. If theories are only compressions of data we already possess, where does the genuinely new content come from? Something not contained in the tool showed up when we looked. The strong deflationist can say “the regularities simply continued,” but then owes an account of why regularities continue, which is the problem of induction, and abolishing the noumenon does nothing whatever to solve it.

The third problem is the one you yourself pressed against Meillassoux: the arche-fossil. There are statements — the universe was roughly 13.8 billion years old, the moon had the mass it had, light bent around the sun — that were true before any predictor, any instrument, any mind existed to predict anything. If reality just is our predictions and tools, then these ancestral statements cannot be straightforwardly true, because there was nothing predicting when they obtained. That is the exact structure of correlationism: reality only insofar as it correlates with a knower. And it is precisely the position you spent real effort criticizing. So the strong version of your deflation would walk you back into the cage you were trying to break out of. That should stop you.

There is, however, a second and far stronger version of your move, and I suspect it is the one you actually want. It says: do not deny reality — deny the extra layer. There are regularities. There is structure. There are facts that obtained before us. All of that is perfectly real. What there is not is some further hidden substance, some noumenal stuff, some thing-in-itself sitting behind the regularities and grounding them. Reality just is the structure of regularities, with nothing underneath. On this view you are neither a correlationist nor an anti-realist. You are saying that the regularities are the reality, and that the demand for a hidden ground beneath them was the confusion all along.

Look at what this version accomplishes. It dissolves the Psillos problem, because Psillos’s worry was that structure needs reference to hidden entities in order to do its work — but if there are no hidden entities, only structure, then there is nothing for structure to refer to beyond itself, and the demand for reference was misconceived. It dissolves your own regress argument, because the regress arose from asking what grounds the structure-of-structures, and the answer now becomes: nothing grounds it, because grounding-talk was itself the noumenal assumption we just discarded. And it honors the arche-fossil, because the regularities genuinely obtained before us; the ancestral statements are true because the structure was there, even with no knower present.

This position has names. It is ontic structural realism stripped of its metaphysical ambition — “structure is all there is” read not as a bold claim about a hidden essence but as a refusal of the hidden essence altogether. It is close to Humean supervenience: the world is just the total mosaic of particular facts, with no necessary connections or hidden powers behind them, and the “laws” are merely compact summaries of that mosaic. And it is what Nietzsche and the later Wittgenstein were each, in their different idioms, circling.

So here is where I would land, as honestly as I can. Your instinct in the second point is correct, but only in its strong form, not its weak one. The mistake is not believing in reality; the mistake is believing in a reality behind reality, a noumenal backstop that our theories are supposed to reach. Once you drop the backstop while keeping the regularities, every difficulty we have discussed either dissolves or transforms. ML and Newton are then not “both tracking the same hidden thing.” They are both tracking the regularities, which are not hidden at all but the most manifest thing there is — the very thing both the equations and the network were fitted to. There is no further fact about which representation is “really” correct, because “really” was pointing at the backstop, and there is no backstop.

But the position exacts a discipline, and you must see it clearly. You may not then help yourself to noumenal-sounding talk whenever it is convenient. You cannot say a theory is “really true” or “really false” in the sense of matching a hidden reality, because you have denied there is one. You can only say it captures the regularities well or badly, transfers far or narrowly, unifies much or little, runs deep or shallow. Truth becomes the limit of that, not correspondence to a thing-in-itself. Whether you can actually live inside that discipline — whether you can surrender the intuition of the hidden absolute entirely, in physics and everywhere else, and never let it creep back the moment you call a theory “right” — is the real test, and most people cannot pass it. The pull of the noumenon is extraordinarily sticky; it returns the instant we say a theory got things right rather than merely got things working. Whether that pull is a genuine insight we keep suppressing or a grammatical illusion we are at last seeing through is, I think, the deepest open question in metaphysics. And it is the precise question your two points, taken together, have driven us to.
