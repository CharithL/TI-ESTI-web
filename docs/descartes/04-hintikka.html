Hintikka’s Performative Cogito

Inference or performance: what Hintikka argued in 1962, what his critics say he argued, and why the field settled on a hybrid

Chat“Research task” (Hintikka)
Date15 July 2026 · the first chat in the DEscartes project
What the chat produced“Hintikka’s Performative Cogito: A Critical Survey of Inference, Intuition, and Self-Verification in Descartes”

Abstract

The chat was set up to answer one question. Is Hintikka’s reading of the cogito as existential self-verification faithful to Descartes and philosophically better than the inferential and intuitional readings? Or does it fail, and if so, exactly how and according to whom? On that reading, “I do not exist” defeats itself in the act of being thought, so “I exist” verifies itself. The chat’s verdict: Hintikka’s insight survives and his exclusivity does not. The cogito is first-person, momentary and act-bound, as he says. But the Second Replies deny only that it is a syllogism, not that it is an inference (Frankfurt). Once made rigorous, the performance machinery either collapses into the inference “If I think, I exist” or leaves the link between thinking and existence unexplained (Wilson, Feldman, Dicker). The field has settled on an “intuitively grasped inference” (Kenny, Curley, Markie, Newman), because only that reading lets the cogito serve as the model clear and distinct perception from which the truth rule is drawn.

What this review adds. Reading the 1962 article itself changes the shape of the dispute. Hintikka did not claim exclusivity in 1962. He argued that Descartes’ cogito is ambiguous between two interpretations, an inferential one that generalises to any act of consciousness (volo, ergo sum) and a performatory one that does not, and that Descartes “does not distinguish” them (pp. 25, 31). Dicker’s first objection, that Descartes held any thought establishes existence, is Hintikka’s own interpretation (i). His second, that the performance reduces to an inference, is answered by the 1963 reply the chat quotes: there is “no incompatibility whatsoever.” The critics are right about the systematic-role problem. They are less right about what the 1962 paper claimed.

The question this chat asks, and the one it refuses

“Is Hintikka’s reconstruction exegetically faithful to Descartes and philosophically superior to inferential and intuitional readings — or does it fail, and if so, exactly how and according to whom?”

Asked: the reconstruction with article page references; its primary-text basis; a catalogue of criticisms that separates exegetical objections (faithful?) from philosophical ones (a good account regardless?); and the systematic-role question.

Refused: treating “performative” as self-explanatory. The brief required it to be defined against Austin, and asked whether Descartes’ concern with silent thought undercuts the analogy with public speech acts.

The thing that makes this chat worth reading twice

The deepest objection is not exegetical but architectural. Descartes draws the general rule, “whatever I perceive very clearly and distinctly is true,” from the cogito (CSM 2:24). If the cogito is certain for a reason unique to first-person existence claims, it is precisely not a representative clear and distinct perception, and the rule has no model. In the chat’s words, a performatively grounded cogito “looks like a sui generis fixed point that cannot underwrite a general rule about clarity and distinctness.”

That is why the field moved to the hybrid. It is also why the exegetical quarrel over the Second Replies matters less than it seems. Even a reader who grants Hintikka the text still has to explain how Meditation III gets its rule.

The whole dispute in two sentences — reconstructed
Non-syllogistic
Descartes denies only that “I exist” is deduced from a known major premise. An immediate inference, grasped in one intuition like modus ponens, remains available.
Non-inferential
“I exist” is not the conclusion of anything. It is the product of an act, as the sound of music is the product of playing.
Frankfurt and most of the field read the Second Replies as the first. The chat reads Hintikka as the second. Hintikka 1962 holds that Descartes had both in view and failed to separate them (p. 25).

About this part

The brief’s first demand: “Steelman Hintikka in his own terms before criticizing.”

In short

As logic, the syllogism is either question-begging or invalid. Hintikka’s alternative makes “I exist” the product of an act. Four features of the text then fall into place.

01Why the inference fails as logic: Hamlet

JH
Jaakko Hintikka
1929–2015 · Helsinki; Stanford; Florida State; Boston University
Logician. Founder of epistemic logic (Knowledge and Belief, 1962, the same year as the cogito paper) and of game-theoretical semantics.
The five stages
  • 1962 “Inference or Performance?”
  • 1963 “…as an Inference and a Performance”
  • 1978 “A Discourse on Descartes’s Method”
  • 1989 “The Cartesian Cogito, Epistemic Logic and Neuroscience”
  • 1996 “Cogito, ergo quis est?”
Where he matters: throughout. 1962, 1978, 1989 and 1996 are in the folder; 1963 is not.

Read the dictum as an enthymeme: whatever thinks exists; I think; therefore I exist. Hintikka shows this is a dilemma. If classical quantification theory’s existential presuppositions are kept, the step is valid but idle, because the existence of the referent of “I” was built into treating “I think” as a predication. If they are dropped, the step is invalid, and the counterexample is literary:

Hamlet did think a great many things; does it follow that he existed? Hintikka 1962 p. 8 checked

Dicker, reconstructing the same point, quotes Hintikka on the first horn: in logics where the pattern is valid, “the term which replaces a … must not be empty,” so “we in fact decided that the sentence ‘I exist’ is true when we decided that the sentence ‘I think’ is of the form B(a)” Dicker, ch. 2 n. checked.

The older objection comes from Gassendi, as Hintikka notes: “Gassendi already claimed that ambulo, ergo sum, ‘I walk, therefore I am,’ is as good an inference as cogito, ergo sum” p. 6 checked. Descartes’ reply locates the difference in awareness, not in logic:

T1 · Fifth Replies

“I may not, for example, make the inference ‘I am walking, therefore I exist’, except in so far as the awareness of walking is a thought. The inference is certain only if applied to this awareness…”

AT 7:352 · CSM 2:244 checked

02Existential inconsistency and self-verification

A sentence p is existentially inconsistent for a person if “p and that person exists” is inconsistent. “I do not exist” is the paradigm. Its negation is then existentially self-verifying:

…as existentially inconsistent sentences defeat themselves when they are uttered or thought of, their negations verify themselves when they are expressly uttered or otherwise professed. Hintikka 1962 p. 15 checked
T2 · process to product · Hintikka 1962

“Hence the indubitability of this sentence is not strictly speaking perceived by means of thinking (in the way the indubitability of a demonstrable truth may be said to be); rather, it is indubitable because and in so far as it is actively thought of. In Descartes’s argument the relation of cogito to sum is not that of a premise to a conclusion. Their relation is rather comparable with that of a process to its product. The indubitability of my own existence results from my thinking of it almost as the sound of music results from playing it…”

Hintikka 1962 p. 16 checked

The first sentence is the one the systematic-role objection seizes on (§08). If the cogito’s certainty is “not strictly speaking perceived,” it is not a clear and distinct perception at all.

And the word cogito names the act, not a premise: its function is “to express the performatory character of Descartes’s insight; it refers to the ‘performance’ (to the act of thinking) through which the sentence ‘I exist’ may be said to verify itself” p. 17 checked.

03Four consequences that fit the text

Momentariness
1962 p. 21
Descartes’ insight “emerges from his own descriptions as a curiously momentary affair. It is a consequence of the performatoriness of his insight.” Compare “for as long as I am thinking” (CSM 2:18).
First-person, not generalisable
1962 p. 21
“In a sense, therefore, Descartes’s insight is not generalizable. … Each of us can formulate ‘for himself’ a sentence in the first person singular that is true and indubitable.”
Dubito, ergo sum
1962 p. 17
The act can be called doubting; “Descartes does admit that his insight is also expressible by dubito, ergo sum” (Recherche, AT X 523).

A fourth consequence completes the picture: Descartes as his own audience. He “arrives at his first and foremost insight by playing for a moment a double role: he appears as his own audience” p. 13, n. 22 checked. The chat quotes the later, more vivid form of the same idea: “Descartes’s little skit is analogous to someone’s, say Mark Twain’s, proving his existence to a skeptic by confronting the doubter and confirming his existence to him by saying: ‘I exist.’” Hintikka & Hintikka 1989, p. 113 checked

A small correction

The chat puts non-generalisability at “1962, pp. 19–20.” The discussion begins on p. 20, but the sentence “Descartes’s insight is not generalizable” is on p. 21.

04The Second Replies: non-syllogistic or non-inferential?

T3 · Second Replies

“…he does not deduce existence from thought by means of a syllogism, but recognizes it as something self-evident by a simple intuition of the mind. This is clear from the fact that if he were deducing it by means of a syllogism, he would have to have had previous knowledge of the major premiss ‘Everything which thinks is, or exists’; yet in fact he learns it from experiencing in his own case that it is impossible that he should think without existing.”

AT 7:140 · CSM 2:100 checked

Hintikka’s reading
1
Not deduced
“Simple intuition of the mind” excludes inference: the certainty comes from the act.
Frankfurt’s rejoinder
1
Not syllogistically deduced
The text rules out only an argument needing a prior universal major. An immediate inference is untouched. Frankfurt’s own reading, as Curley quotes it: “Descartes in effect points out that a premiss from which sum can be elicited is an essential and inescapable element of every context in which the need for assurance concerning sum arises” (Curley p. 83 checked, citing “Frankfurt, 111”).

The Lexicon entry, which the chat treats as the reference consensus, adds a textual point in Frankfurt’s favour from the Meditation itself. Even the discursive version “is, after all, inferential: ‘I must finally conclude’ (enfin il faut conclure) is an explicit expression of inference to the conclusion, I exist” Cambridge Descartes Lexicon, p. 131 checked.

05Principles, Search, and the anticipations

Principles I.7 calls “I am thinking, therefore I exist” “the first and most certain of all to occur to anyone who philosophizes in an orderly way” (AT 8A:7, CSM 1:195). Markie stresses that Descartes repeatedly insists on the first person (ego cogito), which tells against Hintikka’s willingness to let the content be impersonal.

GD
Georges Dicker
SUNY Brockport
Author of the analytic introduction you have worked through most closely. His reconstruction of the cogito is the subject of Chat 6.
Key work here
  • Descartes: An Analytical and Historical Introduction (1993; 2nd ed. 2013), ch. 2 §3, pp. 48–49

Dicker gives the textbook statement of why the reading lost:

This novel interpretation of the cogito, however, has been generally rejected by Descartes scholars, for two different reasons. First, it makes the certainty that I exist depend narrowly on only one specific thought; namely the attempt to doubt my own existence. But, as we have seen, Descartes believed that any of his thoughts, regardless of its content, established his existence. Second, when one asks exactly why trying to doubt my existence causes me to be certain that I exist, the only clear answer seems to be that I accept the argument: “If I try to doubt my existence, then I exist; I am trying to doubt my existence; therefore I exist.” But then the performatory cogito reduces to an inference or argument after all. Dicker, Descartes p. 49 checked

Frankfurt (1966; Demons, Dreamers, and Madmen, 1970) supplies the textual rejoinder of §04. Markie (1992) adds the first-person point of §05. The chat reports all three as the exegetical case against Hintikka.

What the 1962 paper actually claims: two interpretations, not one

The chat’s TL;DR says Hintikka “overreaches when it denies the cogito is any inference at all,” and its recommendations tell the reader to “reject his exclusivity.” Section VIII of the 1962 paper claims something different. The cogito, Hintikka argues, admits two interpretations, and Descartes held both without distinguishing them:

(ii) Descartes realized, however, that there is more to the Cogito than interpretation (i). … On this interpretation the peculiarity of the sentence ego sum is of performatory character. The verb cogitare now has to be interpreted rather narrowly. The word cogito may still be replaced by such “verbs of intellection” as dubito (or profero) but not any longer by verbs referring to arbitrary mental acts, such as volo or sentio. Hintikka 1962 p. 25 checked — interpretation (i), the inferential one on which “the word cogito may thus be replaced by any other word which refers to one of my acts of consciousness,” is on the same page

Two consequences follow. Dicker’s first objection is Hintikka’s interpretation (i). Hintikka agrees that Descartes held any thought establishes existence, and asks why cogitare is nonetheless privileged: “If Descartes could argue volo, ergo sum and sentio, ergo sum as well as cogito, ergo sum, why did he refuse to infer that his nature consists of ‘Wille und Vorstellung’?” (p. 31 checked). Dicker’s second objection meets the 1963 reply the chat itself quotes: “there is no incompatibility whatsoever between saying that cogito, ergo sum is a performance and that it is an inference.” The critics’ target is a stronger thesis than the one on the page.

The systematic-role problem.

“This is where the performative reading is most exposed.”

08A sui generis fixed point cannot launch a general rule

T4 · the truth rule drawn from the cogito · Med. III

“…So I now seem to be able to lay it down as a general rule that whatever I perceive very clearly and distinctly is true.”

AT 7:35 · CSM 2:24 checked

The rule is extracted from “this first item of knowledge.” It needs the cogito to be a perception, and a clear and distinct one, of the same kind as “2+3=5.” T2 says the cogito’s indubitability is “not strictly speaking perceived.”

If the cogito is certain by existential self-verification…
…then its certainty is a feature of first-person existence claims only. “2+3=5” is not existentially self-verifying; its denial is not self-defeating.
…it is not a representative clear and distinct perception…
…and cannot be the exemplar from which a rule about clear and distinct perception is generalised.
So the hybrid
The “Intuitively Grasped Inference”: “It is one thing for a propositional content” to have inferential structure, another for one’s conviction to be grounded in inference (Lexicon p. 131 checked). The cogito can be self-evident and inferential, like modus ponens, and so remain a paradigm perception.

Newman’s SEP formulation, quoted by the chat, is the same point: “There is no inconsistency in claiming a self-evident grasp of a proposition that has inferential structure.” not held The chat adds that Gewirth’s reading of the Third Meditation doubt as “unrestricted and distributive” presses from the other side.

Does §07 rescue Hintikka here?

Only partly. If the cogito carries both interpretations, interpretation (i) could serve as the model for the rule and interpretation (ii) could supply the special certainty. But the truth rule is drawn from the cogito’s certainty, and on Hintikka’s account that certainty belongs to (ii). The systematic-role objection therefore stands against the part of Hintikka that is distinctive.

10“Performative” and silent thought

The brief’s caution, and the chat’s answer
“Performative” is a suggestive metaphor for existential self-verification, not a strict application of Austin.

An Austinian performative (“I promise”) is a public utterance that does something in the saying. The cogito is, for Descartes, silent thought. Hintikka accommodates this by letting a thought-act stand in for a speech-act and casting Descartes as his own audience (p. 13, n. 22). Wilson on the audience requirement and Vendler on the grammar of thinking argue that the analogy strains, because the features that make public performatives self-guaranteeing are exactly what silent introspection lacks. The same point is at the centre of Derrida’s critique in Chat 2, from the opposite side: for Derrida, the silence of hearing-oneself-think is the illusion.

12Where this leads

The line to keep

Hintikka showed that the certainty of “I exist” lives in the act. His critics showed that a certainty living only in the act cannot be the model for anything else. Both are right, and the hybrid is the price of keeping both.

The next chat takes the act and its limit together. The certainty of the occurrence (tier one) and the certainty of a self (tier two) may not be the same certainty at all. Chat 5.
