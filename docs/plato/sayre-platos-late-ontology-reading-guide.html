A Riddle Resolved

Parmenides Publishing, 2005; original text published in 1983
The question this book asks

Can Aristotle’s puzzling reports about Plato be recovered from the written dialogues?

Sayre argues that the Philebus contains a late ontology in which Forms and sensible things derive from common principles. Unity or Limit determines the Unlimited; Forms function as fixed measures through which sensible things acquire identity. The 2005 introduction qualifies the original treatment of the Good.

Part One · Close reading

Read the argument in detail

Short passages support the central claims. The surrounding explanations distinguish the author’s interpretation from the guide’s reconstruction.

01State the historical problem

Aristotle attributes doctrines to Plato about Unity, the Great and the Small, Forms, and numbers. These do not look like the familiar theory of independent Forms. Sayre asks whether the written dialogues contain the reported doctrines in a different vocabulary. He neither accepts every Aristotelian report uncritically nor assumes that the relevant teaching was confined to unwritten instruction.

SAYRE · SOURCE
“the main tenets attributed to Plato in the first book of the Metaphysics in fact are present in the Philebus.”
Printed p. 13 · PDF p. 42
What this supports. This excerpt states the book’s central interpretive hypothesis.

02Identify the philosophical problem

A theory of radically different Forms and sensible things must explain how they are related. Saying that things participate or resemble Forms can restate the dependence without explaining it. The theory must also explain the persistence and identity of sensible things and the possibility of knowledge. Sayre reads late dialogues as attempts to address this interconnected set of problems. See pp. 13–15.

03Announce the change in the status of Forms

In the reconstructed late account, Forms are constituted from Unity and the Unlimited, while sensible things depend on Forms and the same Unlimited. Forms retain explanatory priority over their instances, but cease to be ultimate principles independent of everything else. This shared basis is supposed to remove the radical separation that made participation obscure.

SAYRE · SOURCE
“Like sensible objects, Forms now are constituted from more basic principles.”
Printed p. 14 · PDF p. 43
What this supports. The thesis concerns ontological derivation, not a chronological creation of Forms.

Part One · Close reading

Read the argument in detail

Short passages support the central claims. The surrounding explanations distinguish the author’s interpretation from the guide’s reconstruction.

01Read the two halves together

The first part’s objections concern participation, likeness, regress, and the availability of Forms to knowledge. The second part is not an unrelated exercise on Sayre’s reading: it examines assumptions about unity and plurality that bear on those difficulties. A dramatic argument need not be Plato’s own final assertion. Its role in a larger examination matters. See pp. 18–37.

02Move beyond the separation thesis

Sayre’s proposed solution is stronger than correcting a single faulty inference. He treats the objections as pressure to abandon radical separation and construct a different relation between Forms and sensible things. The Philebus supplies the later positive answer, while the Parmenides prepares its conditions.

SAYRE · SOURCE
“This approach bears no sign of the separation thesis.”
Printed p. 37 · PDF p. 66
What this supports. The sentence refers to the new approach in the Philebus that Sayre contrasts with the earlier theory.

03Use the architecture of the hypotheses

Sayre pairs hypotheses I–VI, II–V, III–VII, and IV–VIII. The point is to compare corresponding consequences under differing assumptions, rather than treating every assertion as part of one contradictory doctrine. On his interpretation, strict isolation of Unity and others defeats intelligibility, whereas their appropriate relation supports determinate multiplicity. See pp. 37–62. The pairing is an interpretive proposal, not the only accepted organization.

04Connect unity and multiplicity to number and becoming

The discussion of equality, inequality, and the instant suggests a mathematical setting in which determinate limits articulate otherwise indefinite continua. Sayre explores possible connections with the ancient treatment of incommensurables. This offers a way toward understanding the Great and the Small, but the chapter deliberately stops short of a historical demonstration.

SAYRE · SOURCE
“The effect of these observations is to recommend further investigation of the possibility that such may be the case.”
Printed p. 74 · PDF p. 103
What this supports. The chapter’s ending marks the mathematical connection as a hypothesis for further inquiry.

Read the argument in detail

Short passages support the central claims. The surrounding explanations distinguish the author’s interpretation from the guide’s reconstruction.

01Separate five reports before explaining them

Sayre isolates five claims: numbers arise from the Great and the Small through Unity; sensible things are constituted from Forms and the Great and the Small; Forms are constituted from Unity and the Great and the Small; Forms are numbers; and the Good is Unity. These must be distinguished before one can judge whether the Philebus explains them. The last becomes the most qualified claim in the 2005 reassessment. See pp. 84–95.

02Treat the history as a controlled hypothesis

The chapter is designed to propose fruitful interpretations whose consequences can be tested against the Philebus. Reports of Plato’s teaching and ancient commentaries give clues, but the surviving evidence cannot settle every detail of the Academy’s history. Sayre’s criterion is whether the reconstruction clarifies otherwise puzzling passages.

SAYRE · SOURCE
“The structure of argument in this chapter is basically hypothetical.”
Printed p. 76 · PDF p. 105
What this supports. This states the methodological status of the historical reconstruction.

03Establish the terminological bridge

The Great and the Small is connected with the Indefinite Dyad, the Unlimited, and relations of more and less. Unity is connected with limitation and determination. If these connections are right, Aristotle’s unusual description can be compared with the Philebus without assuming that identical words must appear in both sources. Ancient testimony supports the bridge; the dialogue must then confirm its explanatory value. See pp. 95–103, 112–117.

04Use mathematics without importing a modern theory

A continuum of greater and lesser magnitudes can receive a determinate standard through limiting relations. Sayre explores this as a model for how measures are constituted. He explicitly distinguishes ancient magnitudes and numbers from Dedekind’s modern construction. The analogy is meant to illuminate the ancient doctrine, not identify it with modern real-number theory.

SAYRE · SOURCE
“Ascription of Dedekind’s theory to Eudoxus is an anachronism to be avoided.”
Printed p. 107 · PDF p. 136
What this supports. This is Sayre’s own warning against an overly strong historical identification.

Unlimited

Part One · Close reading

Read the argument in detail

Short passages support the central claims. The surrounding explanations distinguish the author’s interpretation from the guide’s reconstruction.

01Start with the one-and-many method

The “gift of the gods” requires inquiry to find the determinate intermediates between an initial unity and indefinite instances. In music, knowing merely that sound is one and that sounds are indefinitely many is not expertise. One must understand the structured intervals and kinds. Sayre connects this method with collection and division and with an ontology of Limit and Unlimited. See pp. 118–133.

02Interpret the fourfold division

The Philebus distinguishes Unlimited, Limit, Mixture, and Cause. Indefinite more-and-less admits no fixed amount simply as such. Limiting relations articulate it; mixtures exhibit determinate combinations, while cause accounts for their ordering. Sayre’s focus is the constitutive role of the first principles, not an assertion that the cause of every mixture has been fully explained. See pp. 133–155.

03Constitute Forms and sensible things in different stages

Limit or Unity fixes determinate reference points within indefinite ranges. These function as Forms. Sensible states then acquire identities through their relations to those standards. This is explanatory priority, not a story in which eternal Forms are manufactured at a date. The same Unlimited figures in both levels, so the relation between them no longer spans two utterly disconnected domains.

SAYRE · SOURCE
“Imposing Unity or Limit upon these continua results in fixed and unique reference points which admit quantitative comparison.”
Printed p. 175 · PDF p. 204
What this supports. The passage states how the reference points that function as Forms are constituted.

04Understand “Forms are numbers” through measures

Sayre interprets the numerical character of Forms in terms of fixed standards and proportions. The musical example is useful: an ordered scale allows performances to count as determinate notes or deviations. The claim is not that Justice is the integer three. Forms remain precisely characterizable standards, whereas sensible instances are identified by reference to them. Human discovery of a standard should not be confused with human invention of eternal reality. See pp. 161–177.

05Evaluate the bridge between Unity and Limit

Sayre combines the dialogue, Aristotle’s reports, and the commentators to argue that Unity and Limit do the same relevant ontological work. His case is cumulative: it is supported by the number of otherwise puzzling passages the interpretation makes intelligible. It is not a strict deduction from an explicit Platonic identity statement.

SAYRE · SOURCE
“Although evidence of this sort is not conclusive, and probably never could be by the nature of the case”
Printed p. 167 · PDF p. 196
What this supports. This excerpt qualifies the evidence immediately before Sayre says the equivalence is strongly supported.

06Read the original conclusion about the Good with the later qualification

The original text associates goodness with measure, proportion, and the unity of the good mixture; it uses this to interpret Aristotle’s identification of Good and Unity. The 2005 introduction calls that explanation less satisfactory and provisionally moves toward the Good as a unified field of being. Both stages belong in a guide to this edition. The detailed original argument is at pp. 168–174; the revision is at pp. xix–xx.

07Keep the generalization problem visible

Music gives a relatively clear illustration of how a determinate structure organizes variation. Extending this to Health, Beauty, or other complex Forms is harder. Sayre concedes that the dialogues do not provide the resources needed to specify all the relevant measures. Consequently, the model’s philosophical ambition exceeds its worked examples.

SAYRE · SOURCE
“For us to say just how the relevant measures in these latter cases could actually be specified, however, would require more resources than these dialogues provide.”
Printed p. 186 · PDF p. 215
What this supports. This qualifies the proposed extension to Health, Beauty, and similar Forms.

01Assign each dialogue a task

The earlier theory leaves three related questions: what sensible objects are, how knowledge is possible, and how participation works. Sayre assigns central roles to the Theaetetus, Sophist, and Timaeus respectively. This appendix is therefore part of the book’s supporting argument rather than a miscellaneous supplement.

SAYRE · SOURCE
“These three problems (the status of sensible objects, the ontological requirements of knowledge, and the nature of participation) are addressed in the Theaetetus, the Sophist, and the Timaeus, respectively.”
Printed p. 187 · PDF p. 216
What this supports. The sentence states the appendix’s organizing principle.

02Give knowledge an explanatory account

In the Sophist, collection and division disclose which kinds can combine and which exclude one another. A successful logos grounds a true judgment rather than merely being appended to a judgment already formed. Sayre regards this as progress beyond metaphorical pictures of knowledge. Truth and falsehood can also be explained through what is or is not the case with a subject.

SAYRE · SOURCE
“Philosophic knowledge distinguishes Kinds that combine and those that do not.”
Printed p. 222 · PDF p. 251
What this supports. This states the relation between dialectical knowledge and the combinations of kinds.

03Explain the alleged failure of the Timaeus

Images of imprinting, receiving, and physical motion do not, in Sayre’s assessment, explain how changeless Forms account for changing characters while remaining radically separated. He treats this failure as a motive for the revised ontology. This is a contested interpretation: Silverman gives the Timaeus a more constructive role.

SAYRE · SOURCE
“If the ontological theory of Forms and sensibles is to be rescued, it would appear, the thesis of radical separation must be relinquished.”
Printed p. 255 · PDF p. 284
What this supports. The qualification “it would appear” belongs to Sayre’s assessment of the explanatory failure.

01Distinguish motivation from logical dependence

Sayre would find it natural for the Timaeus’s attempt to preserve participation to precede the constructive alternative he reads in Parmenides II. But the first part’s criticisms and the Timaeus’s difficulties can both motivate revision, regardless of which criticism was written first. This weakens the dependence of the central case on one exact chronology.

02Assess evidence without declaring the dates settled

The appendix examines the reliability and interpretation of stylometric conclusions that place the Parmenides before the Timaeus. Sayre distrusts their use as final arbiters while conceding that stylistic evidence remains relevant. A criticism of a dating method is not positive proof of a rival sequence. Read pp. 256–267 as a defense against a potential objection, not a definitive chronology.

Read the argument in detail

Short passages support the central claims. The surrounding explanations distinguish the author’s interpretation from the guide’s reconstruction.

01Use the rival’s questions as a test

Gosling supplies criteria for interpreting the “heavenly tradition” and the determinant–indeterminate passages. Sayre asks whether his own combination of collection and division with a Pythagorean ontology answers these questions. The relevant issue is whether the method, its examples, and the later ontology belong to one coherent account. See pp. 268–272.

02Distinguish two senses of the unlimited

The interpretation differentiates indefinite multiplicity of instances from qualitative indeterminacy within relevant ranges. Without this distinction, the relation between a finite articulation of kinds and indefinitely many sensible cases becomes difficult to explain. Sayre regards that distinction as an advantage of his reading. This is his comparative claim; the appendix is not an independent neutral verdict on every alternative.

Part One · Close reading

Read the argument in detail

Short passages support the central claims. The surrounding explanations distinguish the author’s interpretation from the guide’s reconstruction.

01Retain the two roles of the Great and the Small

The new introduction revisits the music example. Fixed standards or Forms are constituted within a relevant continuum; sensible performances must also occur within that continuum to be comparable with the standards. Thus the same basic principle plays a role both in the standard and in the sensible case. The two roles do not make a Form identical with any one performance. See pp. xviii–xix.

02Qualify the explanation of the Good

Sayre does not present all five reconstructed theses as equally secure. He describes the original account of the Good’s unity through Truth, Beauty, and Proportion as less satisfactory and anticlimactic as an explanation of Plato’s lecture. The qualification should accompany any summary of chapter 3’s ending.

SAYRE · SOURCE
“The account proposed of thesis (5) is more elusive, and perhaps less satisfactory.”
Printed p. xix · PDF p. 20
What this supports. Thesis (5) is the identification of the Good with Unity.

03State the provisional alternative accurately

The revised suggestion is that the Good grounds the relations of specific Forms as a unified field of being. Sayre points to his later book Plato’s Literary Garden for its defense. This introduction reports that alternative; it does not establish it in the detail required for a new proof.

SAYRE · SOURCE
“On balance, I am now inclined to think of Plato’s Good as a unified “field of being” which grounds the relations of specific Forms with one another (as in the Sophist).”
Printed p. xx · PDF p. 21
What this supports. “Inclined” and the reference to a later work identify this as a provisional reassessment.

Part One · Close reading

Read the argument in detail

Short passages support the central claims. The surrounding explanations distinguish the author’s interpretation from the guide’s reconstruction.

01The practical problem of a discussion’s length

The Stranger has offered a lengthy account of weaving. Whether it was too long cannot be settled just by comparing it with a shorter speech. One needs an appropriate measure for the purpose of the inquiry. Sayre uses this context to explain why the text repeatedly distinguishes two kinds of measurement.

SAYRE · SOURCE
“all refer to the same distinction between types of measurement.”
Printed p. 319 · PDF p. 348
What this supports. This short excerpt states the aim of unifying the passage’s apparently different formulations.

02Distinguish comparison from adequacy

Two pieces of cloth can differ in length without either being the right length for a garment. A craft requires a relevant standard of fit or success. This illustration is the guide’s; Sayre’s essay works through the Stranger’s formulations and their relation to production. Due measure is one expression within the wider family of appropriate standards. See pp. 319–349.

03Avoid the quantity-versus-quality misreading

Both kinds of measurement can concern quantitative or qualitative dimensions. Greater and smaller can be compared merely relatively; a quantitative length can also be measured against what a task requires. Conversely, felt heat or cold can be compared without an appropriate standard.

SAYRE · SOURCE
“A mistake to be avoided in connection with this formulation is to assume that measurement of the first kind pertains to quantity exclusively.”
Printed p. 351 · PDF p. 380
What this supports. The passage explicitly blocks a tempting but inaccurate simplification.

04Relate standards to skilled production

The productive arts depend on appropriate measures; arts of measuring and producing can cooperate while remaining different skills. Sayre links excess and deficiency with the Great and the Small and uses the Philebus to clarify the standards that make successful generation possible. This does not mean human crafts invent eternal Forms. See pp. 350–351.

Compare Sayre and Silverman

This comparison is the guide’s synthesis. It locates the disagreement without deciding it by definition. For Sayre, see pp. 13–17, 174–186, and 238–255. For Silverman, see pp. 13–27 and 285–297.

QuestionSayreSilverman
What happens to Forms?Forms become derivative determinate measures constituted from more basic principles.Forms retain their unique essences as relations among them become more explicit.
ParticipationShared principles are meant to remove radical separation and make the dependence intelligible.Being, participation, and form-copies distinguish essence from the ways subjects have properties.
MathematicsMeasure and determination are central to the late constitution of Forms.Geometrical Forms complement traditional Forms in explaining physical particulars.
The TimaeusAn intermediate attempt whose account of participation remains inadequate.The fullest constructive account of spatially organized, relatively persistent particulars.
Main question to pressDoes a standard needed to identify a thing also constitute its being?Why does dependent possession of properties exclude any essence in a material particular?

A useful question for reading both

Does Plato explain dependence by revising the constitution of Forms themselves, or by clarifying how stable essences can be manifested in things whose structure and properties change?
