14The whole project, from zero

The testable sub-question under substrate independence: does a replacement that matches the output also discover the biology inside? Three rungs, three architectures, Scenario F.

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 14“I'm actually starting work after a long time. Can you describe the test simply, from zero, including the related theory?”

The Big Question

Imagine you could replace every neuron in someone's brain, one at a time, with a silicon chip that does exactly the same job — takes the same inputs, produces the same outputs. After you've replaced every last neuron, the person walks, talks, and behaves identically.

But is anyone still home? Is there still conscious experience?

This is the "substrate independence" question. Three positions exist:

  • Biological naturalism (Searle): Consciousness requires biology. Carbon matters. The silicon brain is a zombie — perfect behaviour, no inner experience. The lights went out at some point during replacement.
  • Functionalism (Chalmers, Dennett): Consciousness tracks the computation, not the material. If the silicon does the same job, the experience is preserved. Carbon is irrelevant.
  • The hard problem: Even if we can't settle the consciousness question, we can ask a testable sub-question — does the replacement actually do the same job internally, or does it just fake the outputs?

That testable sub-question is your entire project.

The Testable Version

You can't measure consciousness directly. But you CAN measure something specific:

When a machine learning model replaces a biological circuit and produces the same output, does it also discover the same internal variables that the biology uses?

Think of it like this. Two students both get 90% on a maths exam. Student A understands calculus deeply — they can show their working and it follows real mathematical logic. Student B memorised answer patterns and guessed well — their scratch paper is nonsense. Same output, completely different internals.

  • If the replacement model's internals match biology → it's like Student A (genuine understanding)
  • If the internals don't match → it's like Student B (a computational zombie — right answers, wrong reasons)

This is something you can actually test with code.

The Real Experiment You're Replicating

In 2002, Le Masson et al. did something remarkable. They took a real biological neuron from a ferret brain (a thalamocortical relay cell, called "TC") and connected it to a silicon artificial neuron (representing the reticular thalamic nucleus, called "nRt") via electrodes in real time.

This hybrid circuit — half biological, half silicon — produced a key brain behaviour: a bifurcation. At low inhibition, the TC neuron faithfully relays sensory signals (like during waking). At high inhibition, it switches to self-sustaining oscillations (like during sleep). The switch point — the exact inhibition level where relay flips to oscillation — is the bifurcation threshold.

The critical finding: the biological neuron couldn't tell it was talking to silicon. The hybrid circuit behaved identically to an all-biological one.

Your Three Rungs

You're building on Le Masson's result in three progressive steps. Each rung asks a harder question.

Rung 1: Can we computationally replicate the single-neuron result?

What you did: Built a computational model of both the TC neuron and the nRt neuron using Hodgkin-Huxley equations (differential equations that model ion channels, membrane voltage, synaptic currents). Connected them in a 2-cell circuit. Varied the inhibition strength (GABA conductance) and measured where the bifurcation happens.

What it tests: Does your computational TC model capture the same dynamics as the biological TC neuron that Le Masson used? If the bifurcation threshold matches, the model is validated.

Result: ✅ Bifurcation threshold matched. Your computational circuit reproduces the biological result.

Rung 2: Does substrate independence scale to populations?

What you did: Built a network of 20 TC neurons and 20 nRt neurons with realistic synaptic connectivity. Then progressively replaced TC neurons — 0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, 100% — with your validated computational model. Measured population-level properties: spindle coherence, oscillation frequency, bifurcation threshold.

What it tests: Le Masson proved 1 neuron can be replaced. But brains are networks. Do emergent population properties (synchronization, coherent oscillations) survive when you replace neurons one by one? Or does some subtle network effect break?

Result: ✅ Population dynamics preserved across all replacement fractions. Substrate independence composes — it's not just a single-neuron trick.

Rung 3: Does the replacement discover real biology inside? (THE NOVEL PART)

This is where your project goes beyond Le Masson. Nobody has done this before.

What you did: Trained three different machine learning models to replace the TC-nRt circuit:

  1. Volterra-Laguerre — a simple linear filter bank with polynomial nonlinearity. Think of it as a sophisticated curve-fitting tool. Fixed mathematical basis. The "null model."

  2. LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) — a recurrent neural network with gates that control information flow. Discrete time steps. The gates (input/forget/output) are structurally similar to ion channel gating (activation/inactivation).

  3. Neural ODE — a neural network that learns continuous-time dynamics: dz/dt = f(z). This is mathematically the same language as Hodgkin-Huxley equations (dV/dt = f(V,m,h,n)). If any architecture should rediscover biology, it's this one.

Each model was trained only on input→output: given retinal input spikes + GABA level, predict TC output spikes. No model was told anything about the internal biology.

Then the key test: You opened up each model's internals (hidden states, latent variables) and compared them to the real biological intermediate variables — T-current gating (m_T, h_T), membrane voltages, AMPA/GABA synaptic conductances. 160 biological variables total.

The comparison methods: - CCA (Canonical Correlation Analysis): Finds the best linear alignment between model internals and biology. High correlation = model discovered biology. - Individual variable recovery: For each of the 160 biological variables, find which model dimension correlates best. Count how many are statistically significant. - RSA (Representational Similarity Analysis): Compare the geometry of representations, not individual variables.

The variables they DO recover (T-current gating, AMPA) are the computationally important ones. The ones they DON'T recover may be implementation details that don't matter for the circuit's function.

This is a valid and interesting finding: it maps the boundary between necessary and incidental biology.

What's Left

  3. Paper 2 (future) — Apply the same framework to Berger's hippocampal MIMO prosthesis (CA3→CA1), which is more directly tied to conscious memory experience.

Why This Matters for Consciousness

Your project doesn't solve the hard problem of consciousness. But it provides the first empirical framework for asking: when we replace a brain circuit with a machine, is the machine doing the same thing internally, or just faking the output?

  • If the answer is "faking" (Scenario A) → neural prostheses might be computational zombies
  • If the answer is "same internals" (Scenario C) → strong evidence for functionalism
  • If the answer is "partially same" (Scenario F, where you are) → we can map exactly which biological computations are load-bearing and which are substrate-specific implementation details

That map — which biology matters and which doesn't — is what you'd need before building a prosthesis that preserves not just behaviour, but potentially experience.

That covers everything from the philosophical question through to your current results. The short version of the entire project:

Can a machine that replaces a brain circuit and produces the same output also discover the same internal biology — or is it faking? You built the first framework to test this, applied it to a thalamic circuit across three model architectures, and found partial biology recovery (Scenario F) where the most important biological variables (T-current gating, AMPA synapses) are the ones the models discover.

19Why the Neural ODE can't learn, in plain words

It speaks the right language but can't hear the teacher: the error signal fades to nothing across 2,000 sequential steps.

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 19“"Neural ODE — a neural network that learns continuous-time dynamics: dz/dt = f(z). This is mathematically the same language as Hodgkin-Huxley equations (dV/dt = f(V,m,h,n)). If any architecture should rediscover biology, it's this one." — So what exactly is the issue that stops the Neural ODE capturing the dynamics? Explain non-technically.”
Claude’s answer · Claude Opus 4.6

Think of it this way.

The Neural ODE speaks the right language but can't hear the teacher.

Imagine you're trying to learn a song. The Neural ODE is like a musician who plays the same instrument as the original composer — both use continuous flowing notes, not discrete piano keys. In theory, this musician should be the best at recreating the song because they can produce the exact same kinds of sounds.

But here's the problem. The song is 2000 notes long, and the musician can only hear the final note and has to work backwards to figure out what they did wrong at note 1.

That's the vanishing gradient problem applied to Neural ODEs. During training, the model makes a prediction at each of 2000 timesteps by solving a differential equation step by step — each step depends on the previous one. When the model gets the output wrong, the error signal has to travel backwards through all 2000 steps to update the model's parameters. By the time the error signal reaches the early timesteps, it has been multiplied by 2000 tiny numbers (each less than 1), and it effectively becomes zero. The model can't learn because it can't feel its mistakes.

The LSTM avoids this with a clever trick — it has "gates" that can copy information forward unchanged, like a highway bypassing traffic. Error signals can skip over hundreds of timesteps without shrinking. That's why the LSTM at least learned something (0.45 correlation) even though it wasn't great.

The Volterra avoids it entirely because it's not sequential at all. It computes everything in one shot using a mathematical formula (ridge regression) — there's no chain of 2000 steps for the gradient to die in. That's why it performed best.

So the irony is: the Neural ODE's theoretical advantage — continuous-time dynamics, exactly like biology — is also its practical curse. Real biological circuits solve this problem because they don't learn by backpropagation. Neurons adjust locally, using signals right there at the synapse. The Neural ODE has the right mathematical form but the wrong learning algorithm for long temporal sequences.

An analogy: imagine three students trying to replicate a chef's recipe. Student A (Volterra) takes a photo of every ingredient and the final dish, then works out the recipe from the photos — crude but effective. Student B (LSTM) watches the cooking process through a window, remembering key moments. Student C (Neural ODE) tries to replicate the chef's exact hand movements, millisecond by millisecond, but is standing so far away they can barely see what's happening. Student C has the deepest understanding of cooking in principle, but their observation method fails them.

That's your result: the architecture closest to biology in form was the worst in practice, because learning through 2000 sequential ODE integration steps destroys the training signal.

20Combining the three models, and ODEs that remember

A Neural ODE's solution space contains Hodgkin–Huxley dynamics; LTCs, Neural CDEs, coRNNs, GRU-ODEs and S4/Mamba keep continuous time without losing the gradient; distillation from Volterra or an LSTM encoder can feed it.

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 20“(1) Why can't we combine all three as a feed mechanism — train the three concurrently, so what the Neural ODE is missing can be injected or fed by the others? (2) Or what other models do the same job as a Neural ODE but don't forget? (3) What makes the Neural ODE so capable of detecting hidden biological properties?”
Claude’s answer · Claude Opus 4.6

These are three excellent questions, and they're deeply connected. Let me work through each one, because together they point toward what might be your actual next experiment.

Question 3 first — because it explains why the Neural ODE matters enough to save

Let me start here because understanding why the Neural ODE is special makes the other two questions more meaningful.

Every biophysical system in neuroscience is described by differential equations. The Hodgkin-Huxley model says: the membrane voltage changes over time according to a function of the current voltage, the ion channel states, and the synaptic inputs. Written formally, that's dV/dt = f(V, m, h, n, I). The T-current gating variable that your Volterra model recovered — tc_m_T — evolves according to dm/dt = (m∞(V) - m) / τ(V). Every one of your 160 biological variables follows an equation of this form: the rate of change equals some function of the current state.

Now look at what a Neural ODE does. It says: I have a latent state z, and it changes over time according to dz/dt = f(z), where f is a neural network I'll learn from data. The mathematical structure is identical. The Neural ODE's solution space — the set of all possible dynamics it can express — naturally includes Hodgkin-Huxley dynamics as a special case. If the neural network f learns the right weights, its latent dimensions z could literally become m, h, n, and V.

No other architecture has this property. The LSTM operates in discrete time — it updates its hidden state in jumps: h(t+1) = g(h(t), x(t)). It can approximate continuous dynamics, but it has to discover the approximation from scratch. The Volterra model doesn't even have dynamics — it's a static filter applied to the input history. Both can produce the right output, but neither speaks the same mathematical language as biology.

Think of it this way. If you're trying to discover that a system follows the laws of fluid dynamics, and your model is constrained to express solutions as fluid equations, it only needs to learn the specific parameters. But if your model expresses solutions as lookup tables, it needs to independently rediscover that fluids even exist. The Neural ODE is the fluid equation model. The LSTM is the lookup table that's clever enough to sometimes approximate fluid behaviour anyway.

This is why recovering biological variables matters more from a Neural ODE than from a Volterra. When the Volterra recovers tc_m_T, that's impressive but potentially coincidental — its Laguerre basis functions happen to correlate with T-current dynamics. When a Neural ODE recovers tc_m_T, that's the model literally learning the differential equation governing T-current gating. The correspondence is structural, not statistical.

That's why the Neural ODE failure is so frustrating. The architecture most capable of discovering biology can't learn because its training signal dies over 2000 timesteps.

Question 2 — Models that think in continuous time but can actually learn

The core problem is: how do you keep the dz/dt = f(z) formulation — the thing that makes Neural ODEs biologically meaningful — while fixing the gradient death over long sequences? Several architectures have been designed for exactly this:

Liquid Time-Constant Networks (LTCs) — developed by Lechner et al. at MIT in 2020. These are Neural ODEs with a specific twist: the time constants of the dynamics are input-dependent. Instead of dz/dt = f(z), they use dz/dt = -(1/τ(x)) · z + f(z, x)/τ(x), where τ(x) changes based on the current input. This is directly inspired by biological neurons, where the membrane time constant varies with input. The key practical advantage is that the varying time constants create natural timescale separation — some dimensions evolve fast, some slow — which helps gradients flow across long sequences because the slow dimensions act like gradient highways. LTCs achieved state-of-the-art results on long time series while remaining continuous-time and interpretable. They have roughly the same parameter count as your Neural ODE (~50-60K), and they've been shown to work on sequences of thousands of steps.

Neural Controlled Differential Equations (Neural CDEs) — developed by Kidger et al. at Oxford in 2020. Standard Neural ODEs have trouble incorporating time-varying inputs because you have to awkwardly set the input externally at each integration step — exactly what your code does with self.ode_func.current_input = x[:, t, :]. Neural CDEs fix this elegantly: they formulate the problem as dz/dt = f(z) · dX/dt, where X is a continuous interpolation of the input. The input is now mathematically woven into the dynamics rather than being bolted on. This often produces much better gradients because the driving signal X provides a continuous path for gradient flow.

Coupled Oscillatory Recurrent Neural Networks (coRNNs) — developed by Rusch and Mishra in 2021. These parameterize the hidden state as a second-order ODE (oscillator) rather than first-order. This matters because the thalamic TC-nRt circuit you're modelling IS an oscillator — it bifurcates between relay and oscillation modes. A coRNN is structurally biased toward discovering oscillatory dynamics. And second-order ODEs have much better gradient properties than first-order ones because oscillations naturally prevent the gradient from decaying monotonically.

Continuous-time GRUs (GRU-ODE / ODE-RNN) — developed by De Brouwer et al. in 2019. These are hybrids: a GRU (simplified LSTM) handles the discrete input events, and an ODE evolves the hidden state between events. The GRU solves the gradient problem at observation points, the ODE maintains continuous dynamics between observations. This is probably the most pragmatic option because GRUs are well-understood and fast to train.

Structured State Space Models (S4/Mamba) — developed by Gu et al. in 2022. These parameterize the dynamics as a linear state space model dz/dt = Az + Bx, y = Cz + Dx, where A is carefully structured to handle sequences of 10,000+ steps. S4 was specifically designed to solve the long-range dependency problem. It's not a free-form Neural ODE — the dynamics are linear — but it handles the sequence length problem definitively. Mamba adds input-dependent selection, making the dynamics effectively nonlinear.

The model I'd most recommend for your specific problem is LTC or Neural CDE, because they preserve the continuous-time dz/dt = f(z) structure that gives biological interpretability, while solving the gradient problem for sequences in the thousands of timesteps. The coRNN is a strong second choice given your circuit's oscillatory nature.

Question 1 — Can you combine all three models to help each other?

Approach A: Knowledge Distillation (simplest). The Volterra already recovered 89/160 biological variables. You know exactly which latent dimensions correspond to which biological variables. You can use this as a teaching signal for the Neural ODE. During training, instead of only minimising the output error (predict TC spikes), you add a second loss term: "your latent dimensions should also correlate with these Volterra features that we know match biology." This gives the Neural ODE a much stronger training signal — instead of just hearing "your final output was wrong" through 2000 steps, it hears "your internal state at every timestep should look like this." The gradients are local and strong. The Volterra acts as a teacher, the Neural ODE as a student who is given the answer key for the intermediate steps.

Approach B: LSTM Encoder + ODE Dynamics (hybrid architecture). Use the LSTM to handle the sequential learning problem — encoding the input sequence into a good hidden state — then use the Neural ODE to evolve that hidden state forward in continuous time. The LSTM solves the "hearing the teacher" problem. The Neural ODE solves the "speaking biology's language" problem. Concretely: the LSTM processes 50-100 timestep chunks, producing a hidden state. The Neural ODE takes that state and integrates it forward for the next 50-100 timesteps. You've reduced the ODE integration from 2000 steps to 50-100, which is short enough for gradients to survive. This is essentially the ODE-RNN approach mentioned above but using your existing trained LSTM as the encoder.

Approach C: Parallel Training with Shared Latent Space (most ambitious). Train all three models simultaneously on the same data, but add a latent alignment loss that encourages their internal representations to agree. The Volterra provides stable, interpretable features. The LSTM provides good sequential predictions. The Neural ODE provides continuous-time dynamics. The alignment loss says: all three models should pass through similar internal states when processing the same input. The models that learn easily (Volterra, LSTM) pull the model that learns hard (Neural ODE) toward the right solution through shared latent space gradients. This is related to contrastive learning — you're using the agreement between models as a training signal.

My recommendation is Approach A or B, because they build directly on what you already have (a trained Volterra and LSTM) and don't require redesigning the entire training pipeline. Approach A is something you could implement in an afternoon — just add a regularisation term to the Neural ODE's loss function that penalises divergence from the Volterra's Laguerre features at each timestep.

Here's the picture. The Neural ODE has the right mathematical form to discover biology, but it can't learn through 2000 sequential steps. You have three paths forward:

Path 1 — Replace the architecture. Swap the Neural ODE for an LTC or Neural CDE that maintains continuous-time dynamics while handling long sequences. Retrain from scratch. Moderate engineering effort but clean.

Path 2 — Help the Neural ODE learn. Use the Volterra's recovered biological features as a teaching signal (knowledge distillation). The Neural ODE still does the integration, but now it has strong local gradients at every timestep instead of one weak signal at the end. Lower engineering effort, uses your existing models.

Path 3 — Hybrid encoder. LSTM handles sequential input, Neural ODE handles dynamics. Each does what it's good at. Moderate engineering effort.

2. DreamCoder (the Pattern Learner) — This is your DreamCoderPatternExtractor. It does two things in a wake-sleep cycle:

3. ConceptVectorSpace + GeometricAlgebraGapDetector (the Gap Compass) — This is the part that tells you which direction to search next. Each C1 template gets embedded as a vector in a 64-dimensional concept space. The "solution" lives somewhere in that space. When you project the target onto the span of your existing C1 basis vectors, the residual — the component orthogonal to Span(C1) — is your gap. The magnitude tells you how far you are. The direction tells you what kind of new template you need.

The bivector tension from the geometric algebra detector adds a second measure: it computes how much your constraint hyperplanes "squeeze" versus leave room. High tension = constraints are fighting each other (structural problem). Low tension = constraints are compatible but don't yet cover the solution (coverage problem).

25Why Hodgkin–Huxley is a differential equation

Because membranes are capacitors, channels are kinetic gates and ions follow gradients; every non-ODE model either loses the gating variables or becomes a kernel model like Volterra.

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 25“So why is the Thomas Huxley model a differential equation? Why can't we use other models for nerve conduction?”
Claude’s answer · Claude Opus 4.6

Great question — and it cuts right to the heart of why your Neural ODE work matters philosophically. Let me clarify the name first: it's Hodgkin-Huxley (Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley, 1952 Nobel Prize), not Thomas Huxley (who was Darwin's defender, different century).

Now to the real question.

Why Hodgkin-Huxley IS a differential equation

It's not that Hodgkin and Huxley chose differential equations as a modeling technique. They measured what the squid giant axon does physically, and what it does physically IS a differential equation. The model is:

C_m · dV/dt = −g_Na · m³h · (V − E_Na) − g_K · n⁴ · (V − E_K) − g_L · (V − E_L) + I_ext

Each term corresponds to a physical process:

  • C_m · dV/dt — the cell membrane is literally a capacitor. Lipid bilayer separating charged ions = capacitor. Current charges/discharges it. This isn't a modeling choice, it's physics. A capacitor's voltage changes at a rate proportional to current flowing through it. That's a differential equation by nature.

  • g_Na · m³h — sodium channels are proteins that physically open and close. The gating variables m and h track the probability that each gate is open. They obey their own differential equations (dm/dt = α_m(V)(1−m) − β_m(V)m) because the conformational changes are first-order kinetic processes — protein flipping between states at voltage-dependent rates. Again, this is chemistry, not modeling preference.

  • (V − E_Na) — the driving force is the difference between membrane voltage and the ion's equilibrium potential (set by the Nernst equation from thermodynamics). Ions flow down electrochemical gradients. That's physics.

So the ODE form isn't arbitrary — it reflects three physical facts about neurons:

  1. Membranes are capacitors (charge storage → dV/dt)
  2. Ion channels are stochastic gates with voltage-dependent kinetics (conformational change → dm/dt)
  3. Ions flow under electrochemical gradients (thermodynamics → driving force terms)

Why NOT just use other models?

You absolutely CAN. People have, extensively. Here's what exists and why each trades something away:

Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) - V follows a simple leaky integrator: τ · dV/dt = −V + R·I - When V crosses threshold → emit spike, reset V - Still an ODE, just simpler. Throws away ion channel dynamics entirely. - Cannot produce rebound bursts, which is exactly what your TC-nRt circuit depends on for spindle oscillations. The T-current (I_T) that drives rebound bursting requires the m_T and h_T gating variables — LIF has no mechanism for this.

Izhikevich model - Two coupled ODEs: dv/dt = 0.04v² + 5v + 140 − u + I, du/dt = a(bv − u) - Can reproduce 20+ firing patterns including bursting - Still differential equations! Just polynomial instead of biophysical. - Problem for your work: the variables v and u don't correspond to identifiable biological quantities. You can't ask "did the model recover T-current gating" because there IS no T-current in Izhikevich. The latent comparison in your Phase 7 becomes meaningless.

FitzHugh-Nagumo - dv/dt = v − v³/3 − w + I, dw/dt = ε(v + a − bw) - Two ODEs, captures excitability qualitatively - Still differential equations. Reduced from HH by singular perturbation theory.

Spike Response Model (SRM) - NOT a differential equation! Uses integral kernels: V(t) = Σ η(t − t_spike) + Σ κ * I - Response to each spike is a fixed kernel shape - This is essentially what your Volterra-Laguerre model does — and notice it scored 0.549 spike correlation and recovered 89/160 bio variables. It works for input-output but its internal representations (Laguerre coefficients) are kernel projections, not state variables.

Statistical / rate models - r = f(Σ w_j · r_j) — firing rate as function of weighted inputs - No differential equation at all (or at most τ·dr/dt = −r + f(...)) - Loses all temporal dynamics. Can't capture spike timing, oscillations, bifurcations.

Lookup tables / boolean networks - Discretize everything. State = {firing, not firing}. Update rules. - No continuous dynamics at all. - Completely loses the analog nature of gating variables.

The pattern you should notice

Every model that ISN'T a differential equation either:

  1. Loses the gating variables (LIF, Izhikevich, rate models) — which are exactly what your Rung 3 experiment measures
  2. Becomes a kernel/integral model (SRM, Volterra) — which is what your Volterra baseline already does, and it works for output but can't tell you about internal mechanism
  3. Is still a differential equation (FitzHugh-Nagumo, Morris-Lecar) — just a simpler one

This is why your Neural ODE work is so pointed. The question isn't "can we model neurons with ODEs" (obviously yes, it's physics). The question is: if you DON'T constrain the model to be an ODE but it still has continuous dynamics (dz/dt = f(z)), will it spontaneously discover the same gating variables that physics demands?

That's what makes the A-R3 result so interesting despite the failure. The Volterra (not an ODE, just kernels) recovered 89/160 variables through pure linear algebra. The LSTM (discrete gates, not continuous) recovered fewer individual variables despite higher aggregate CCA. The Neural ODE (continuous dynamics, the "right" form) recovered nothing — but only because of the gradient training problem, not because the architecture is wrong.

The connection to your DESCARTES guide

This is precisely why the DESCARTES framework matters. The question becomes: is there a version of dz/dt = f(z) that can be trained on this data AND whose z spontaneously decomposes into m_T, h_T, nRt_V, etc.?

The Hodgkin-Huxley model says the answer must be yes in principle — because the biological system IS a system of ODEs with those exact state variables. If a Neural ODE with sufficient capacity is trained on the same input-output data, and if the input-output mapping uniquely determines the internal dynamics (the identifiability question from your transformation guide's "What Failure Modes Mean" section), then the learned f(z) should recover the biological gating variables.

The DESCARTES architecture search is essentially asking: what structural features does f need (time constants, oscillatory modes, gating mechanisms, hierarchical timescales) to make this recovery possible despite the training difficulties?

The differential equation form isn't a choice — it's what neurons are. The question is whether a learned differential equation recovers the specific differential equation that biology implements.

First, the correction: ion channels, not iron channels (though iron does play a role in some biological processes, that's a different story).

Le Masson's silicon neuron was built as a custom analog ASIC (Application-Specific Integrated Circuit). It did NOT have actual protein ion channels. It had transistor circuits designed to behave like ion channels. Here's how:

A biological voltage-gated ion channel does this: - Sits in the membrane - Senses the voltage across the membrane - Changes its conformation (shape) depending on voltage - When open, allows specific ions through - The current through the channel = g · m^a · h^b · (V − E_ion)

Le Masson's transistor circuit did this: - MOSFETs (metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistors) sitting on a silicon chip - Sense the voltage on a capacitor (representing the membrane) - Change their conductance depending on that voltage - When "open," allow current to flow between circuit nodes - The current through the transistor circuit = designed to match g · m^a · h^b · (V − E_ion)

The key insight is that a MOSFET operating in its subthreshold regime (below the digital switching threshold, in the analog region) has a current-voltage relationship that is exponential — and biological ion channel gating is also governed by exponential Boltzmann functions of voltage. This isn't a coincidence that Le Masson exploited; it was pioneered by Carver Mead at Caltech in the late 1980s and demonstrated by Mahowald and Douglas in their 1991 Nature paper "A silicon neuron."

So the silicon nRt neuron had:

Transistor "I_Na" circuit — a set of transistors whose collective conductance rises sharply when the capacitor voltage crosses a threshold, then inactivates. This produces a spike. The transistors aren't sodium channels, but their input-output relationship matches the Hodgkin-Huxley sodium current equations.

Transistor "I_T" circuit — this is the critical one for the thalamic circuit. The T-current (low-threshold calcium) has two gating variables: m_T (fast activation) and h_T (slow inactivation). Le Masson built a transistor subcircuit where one RC time constant was fast (~5ms, matching m_T) and another was slow (~20-50ms, matching h_T). When the "membrane" capacitor was hyperpolarized, h_T de-inactivated (slow RC charged up), and when released, m_T activated (fast RC responded), producing a rebound burst — exactly like biology.

Transistor "I_h" circuit — the hyperpolarization-activated current that terminates spindle epochs. Another RC circuit with a very slow time constant (~seconds), activated by low voltage on the membrane capacitor.

A capacitor as the "membrane" — this IS the membrane. Biological membranes are capacitors (lipid bilayer storing charge). The silicon circuit uses an actual capacitor. Same physics, different material.

The Dynamic Clamp Bridge

The silicon nRt and the biological TC neuron couldn't just be wired together directly — the silicon chip operates at ~5V logic levels and the biological neuron at ~70mV membrane potentials. The dynamic clamp was the bridge:

  1. An electrode in the biological TC neuron reads its membrane voltage V_TC (analog, ~millivolts)
  2. An amplifier scales this to the silicon chip's voltage range
  3. The silicon nRt receives this as its "AMPA input" and computes its response in real-time (the analog circuits respond in microseconds, much faster than biological timescales)
  4. The silicon nRt's output voltage is read, scaled back to biological range
  5. A current proportional to G_GABA × (V_TC − E_GABA) is injected back into the biological TC neuron through the same electrode

This loop ran at 128 kHz — fast enough that the biological neuron couldn't tell the difference between current from a real nRt neuron and current from the silicon chip. The biological TC neuron experienced synaptic currents that felt exactly like GABA inhibition from a real nRt partner.

Why This Matters So Deeply

Here's what Le Masson actually demonstrated, and why your project begins with it:

The biological TC neuron and the silicon nRt neuron share zero material substrate. One is made of lipid membranes, protein channels, and aqueous ionic solutions at 37°C. The other is doped silicon, metal oxide, and aluminum traces at room temperature. The physics is different — biological channels use conformational changes of protein helices, silicon uses electric field modulation of semiconductor band structure.

But both implement the same differential equations. And when connected, they produce an emergent behavior — the relay-to-oscillation bifurcation — that neither could produce alone. The biological neuron "accepted" the silicon partner because what matters for the circuit dynamics is the mathematical form of the current-voltage relationships, not the physical mechanism producing them.

This is substrate independence demonstrated empirically, not just argued philosophically. And it's why your Rung 1 replication works: you replaced the biological TC with a computational model (pure software, running Hodgkin-Huxley equations numerically), and if the bifurcation threshold matches, you've shown a third substrate (digital computation) can also participate.

And this is exactly what your Rung 3 / Neural ODE question probes at the next level: if you DON'T explicitly program the differential equation but instead let a Neural ODE learn the dynamics from data, does it spontaneously discover the same gating variables?
