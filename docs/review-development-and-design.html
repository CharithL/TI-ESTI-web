Development and Design

What changes across the dialogues — and what the change is evidence of

The question — and the answer this review reaches

Suppose Plato wrote the whole corpus in a single year. Which developmental claims would survive? Seven volumes, published across twenty-eight years, are read against that question, because it is the one that makes each of them declare what it has been leaning on.

They divide by the instrument each trusts. Brandwood’s chapter in the second Cambridge Companion trusts the word-counts, and reports with unusual candour what they will and will not bear. Nails and Thesleff take that instrument apart — Nails, in the Bloomsbury Handbook, on the circularity buried in its criteria; Thesleff, in Platonic Patterns, on the assumption that one dialogue has one date and that style tracks time rather than function. Vlastos trusts the arguments instead, and finds them defective in a way that needs explaining. Kahn distrusts the arguments as evidence of anything but design, and reads the corpus as a single proleptic programme. Fine, introducing the Oxford Handbook, holds that the chronology as such is neutral between the readings and settles nothing either way. Wolfsdorf, surveying rather than advocating, reports where the responses to Vlastos have come to rest.

Run the test and almost everything dies, on both sides. Every temporally indexed claim goes; so does Kahn’s prolepsis, which needs “withholding” and therefore needs time. What is left is a logical asymmetry: you can state the Gorgias’s difficulty without any of the Meno’s apparatus, and you cannot state the Meno’s solution without a difficulty for it to solve. That is not yet a development. Converting it into one takes a temporal premise — and the whole question is how much, and from where.

The answer reached here: one ordered pair, and the texts supply it. Not a sequence of periods, not a hinge dialogue, not fifty years of growth — one defect and one repair, too closely fitted to each other to be independent. That matters because it is far less than the instrument the field has just declared bankrupt was built to deliver. What Nails and Thesleff destroy is chronological developmentalism, the inference from an order of composition to a change of mind; the claim that survives never used that inference. And the decisive witness for it is Kahn — the leading unitarian, in the volume least disposed to concede anything: he poses the failure himself, states that Plato saw it, and names the Meno’s method of hypothesis as the repair. He is a unitarian about doctrine and a developmentalist about argumentative equipment. Once those two axes are pulled apart, most of the apparent disagreement between these seven volumes dissolves. The position this leaves standing — logical-first developmentalism, on which development is an order of dependence among arguments before it is an order of dates — is stated on its own account at §30, together with the scholars already holding it: R. M. Dancy since 2004, Brickhouse and Smith since 2010, and Kahn — who retracted his own chronology in 1996 and replaced it with an order that is “spatial rather than temporal.”

The move this review makes

And one internal fact discriminates. Convergence of style and doctrine does not — prolepsis predicts it too, and Kahn claims the same pattern as his own evidence (§28). What design does not predict is a method abandoned and then reinstated. That is the discarded patch, and it is the sharpest thing in the file.

Axis D has been invisible because it was never separated from Axis C. Once separated, the structural-fit argument — a defect appears, then machinery shaped to repair it — runs on D, where the leading unitarian agrees with it, rather than on C, where he does not. That is what converts an argument the field has been having for forty years into one with a determinate answer.

The whole dispute in two sentences
The developmental premise
A dialogue is a snapshot of its author’s mind at the moment of writing. What is absent was not yet thought.
The design premise
A dialogue is a designed artefact. What is absent may be withheld, for the reader’s sake or the argument’s.
Both premises fit the corpus we have. Neither is provable from chronology. The question is where they make different predictions — and §25 is the place.

Read §01 first, and §03 carefully.

The debate is regularly misdescribed — by its participants as well as its readers — as a disagreement about whether Plato changed. It is not. No unitarian holds that a man wrote for fifty years without learning anything, and Kahn says so more forcefully than his opponents do.

What is actually in dispute is an inferential licence: whether the difference between two texts entitles you to infer a difference in their author. §01 fixes the price of that licence by removing the chronology altogether and seeing what is left. §§02–04 then set out the three positions bundled under one label, and the very different burdens they carry.

Read §03 carefully. Almost every crossed wire in this literature comes from treating the weak evidential claim and Gerson’s strong metaphysical claim as the same position.

01The question: zero the chronology

Suppose we obtained solid proof that Plato wrote the whole corpus inside one year at the end of his life. Which developmental claims could still be derived from textual evidence alone?

None of the seven volumes under review asks this. It is asked here first because it is the question that sorts them. Every position in this literature carries a temporal commitment somewhere, and almost none of them declares it; forcing the chronology to zero makes each one declare. The hypothesis is absurd, and that is what makes it useful. A thought experiment does not have to be possible to be diagnostic — it has to be clean, and this one is. It removes exactly one thing and leaves everything else standing.

“Plato changed his mind.” “The Meno marks a transition.” “Plato later realised the arguments were unsatisfactory.” All of them are about a sequence of mental states, and there is no sequence left.

The answer, and it is a negative one

Internal evidence yields a dependency order, not a composition order. And a dependency order is exactly what both readings predict: the developmentalist reads it as a mind working forward, the proleptic reader as an author organising an exposition. Under the one-year hypothesis the second reading wins by default, because there is nothing left for the first to describe.

So: no. Developmentalism is not derivable from internal evidence alone. It needs two further things — a composition order, and the premise that composition order tracks doctrinal order. The first is what stylometry was built to supply (§05); the second is precisely what unitarians deny (§02).

That is the honest answer, and most of this review is an inquiry into how much of it can be recovered, and from where. The position this review ends by adopting is stated separately at §30, where it turns out several scholars have been running it — one at book length since 2004, and the leading unitarian since 1996.

The exception — and the second stress test

One class of internal evidence does discriminate, and it is not dependency. It is a method being dropped and then reinstated. The elenchus is used and defended in the Gorgias; it thins out at exactly the point where the defence fails; and it returns in the Meno once recollection has supplied the ground it lacked. Design predicts convergence of style and doctrine — prolepsis predicts that as readily as development does. What design does not predict is a working procedure being abandoned and later restored. That is this review’s one candidate, and it is developed at §§25 and 28.

Honesty requires running the test on the exception as well. It does not pass cleanly either.

THE EXCEPTION, STRESSED “Dropped and reinstated” is itself temporal. Under the one-year hypothesis it flattens into absent here, present there — a distribution, not a history. So the exception does not survive zero chronology. What it survives is something narrower, and more useful: it survives the loss of stylometry.

What the exception actually buys

Not a corpus without dates. A minimal order fixed by the content of the arguments rather than read off the word-counts. You can state the Gorgias’s difficulty without any of the Meno’s apparatus; you cannot state the Meno’s solution without a difficulty for it to solve. That asymmetry fixes which text is answering which, and it does so without a single stylometric datum.

Which means the question worth asking is not the one the field has been asking. It is not is developmentalism over? It is this:

How much chronology does a developmental claim actually need — and where does that much come from?

The answer reached here: one ordered pair, and the texts supply it. Not a sequence of periods, not a hinge dialogue, not fifty years of growth — one defect and one repair, too closely fitted to each other to be independent. Everything demolished in Part One is machinery built to deliver far more than that, which is why its demolition costs so much less than it appears to.

What the test also corrects, about Kahn

It is tempting to treat unitarianism as “fifty years without a change of mind.” Kahn does not hold that. He accepts a chronology: the seven threshold dialogues in “the middle or late 380s, when Plato was about forty-five,” and the Republic “in the decade 380–370, when Plato was in his fifties” p. 59.

His actual assumption is narrow and he states it plainly: “I assume that Plato did not change his mind in any fundamental way between the Laches and Protagoras, on the one hand, and the Phaedo and Republic on the other” p. 59. That is a span of perhaps ten to fifteen years and one authorial project — not a frozen half-century. Prolepsis is a live position, and the absurdity objection does not touch it.

That is the shape of what follows. Part One establishes that the first payer is insolvent — which is the news the Bloomsbury Handbook is reporting. Part Two removes the second. Parts Three and Four establish that the third is solvent, which is the news nobody is reporting.

02The two readings — and the premise that separates them

The question as posed: are unitarians saying Plato did not change his view in fifty years — which would be absurd — or are they saying the developmentalists’ evidence is insufficient?

The second, with a qualification that matters. Nobody defends the first. What separates the camps is not a claim about Plato’s biography but a premise about what a dialogue is, and therefore about what its silences mean.

The inference under attack
From order of composition to order of doctrine

Stylometry delivers a sequence of texts. To get from that to a sequence of beliefs you need an extra premise — that a dialogue’s contents index its author’s mind at the date of writing. That premise is what unitarians deny. They do not deny the sequence, and they do not deny that the texts differ.

So the absence of the Forms from the Laches admits of several explanations: the interlocutor’s capacity, the question at issue, the dramatic date, deliberate reserve — and “Plato had not yet thought of them” is one candidate among these, not the default.

a fuller, subtler, and more satisfying account of the differences is available using the resources internal to each dialogue Ebrey & Kraut, Introduction, Cambridge Companion to Plato, 2nd edn (2022) — on the “growing reluctance” to read the dialogues developmentally

Note what that sentence does and does not say. It does not say nothing changed. It says the changes have a better explanation than a changing mind. The target is the inference, not the phenomenon.

03Three claims bundled as “unitarianism”

Ranked from weakest and most widely held to strongest and most exposed.

1 · The evidential claim — mainstream
The developmental inference is underdetermined

Order of composition is not order of doctrine; textual absence has many available explanations; therefore the developmental reading is not licensed by the evidence usually offered for it. This leaves the developmental conclusion possible. It attacks the route.

This is the Ebrey & Kraut position, and it is where most working scholars now sit. It is also why the labels have fallen out of use: on this claim you are not committed to any thesis about Plato’s mind at all.

2 · Kahn · the proleptic-ingressive reading
Plato had the doctrine and withheld it

The aporetic dialogues are not records of a Socratic phase but literary preparation: they raise, in the reader, the questions the middle dialogues answer. Ch. 2 §5 sketches the ingressive interpretation; §6 is titled for Plato’s motive for holding back.

He accepts a chronology. The seven threshold dialogues belong to “the middle or late 380s, when Plato was about forty-five”; the Republic to “the decade 380–370” p. 59. His assumption is narrow and stated plainly: “I assume that Plato did not change his mind in any fundamental way between the Laches and Protagoras, on the one hand, and the Phaedo and Republic on the other” p. 59. Ten to fifteen years and one authorial project — not a frozen half-century. See §01.

Scope note, easily missed: Kahn’s book runs from the Socratic dialogues through the Republic. He is perfectly content that the late dialogues move to new problems and revise old apparatus. His target is narrow — the claim that there was an early period in which Plato held, as his own, a philosophy inherited from his teacher.

3 · Gerson · the strong version
The position preceded the writing

Plato “was probably in some sense a proponent of Pythagoreanism and a ‘two-world metaphysics’ well before he wrote anything” (AGPh 96/4, 2014). This is not a claim about texts at all; it is a claim about a man who had a philosophy before he had a pen.

Strongest and most exposed: it is unfalsifiable by the corpus, since on it the corpus was never a record of the position in the first place.

Where a fourth position sits

Stable core, revised under challenge. A settled set of commitments, repeatedly reformulated as objections land — the Parmenides being the obvious case of Plato pressing his own view. This is neither claim 1 nor claim 2, it fits the corpus, and it is roughly where most current scholarship actually operates while still using the old vocabulary.

The parsimony point, stated fairly

Development requires only that a man’s views changed — something that happens to everyone. Prolepsis requires an additional posit: a sustained authorial plan with a motive for withholding. The instrument

§01 left a debt: a developmental claim needs a temporal premise, however minimal. Stylometry is the first candidate payer, and the one the field has relied on for a century — establish the order, then trace the growth. Part One reviews five volumes on that instrument and finds it cannot pay: the order is secure precisely where nobody’s thesis needs it, and unavailable precisely where every thesis needs it.

The source is Leonard Brandwood, “Stylometry and Chronology,” ch. 3 of the Cambridge Companion to Plato, 2nd edn (2022), pp. 82–112 — a chapter carried over essentially unchanged from the 1992 first edition, which produces a visible seam (§06).

Bottom line: chronology gives coarse bands, not a sequence. And even a perfect sequence would not yield development — that requires the extra premise of §02.

Two further findings
Republic I, and the poverty of external evidence

Republic I was very likely a separate, earlier work — a conclusion Siebeck, Ritter, and Arnim reached independently. That matters: the dialogue most often used to characterise “early Socratic method” may be a detached piece later annexed.

External evidence is nearly nil. Brandwood: “the only information likely to be reliable is Aristotle’s statement that the Laws was written after the Republic.” One datum, at the far end of the corpus, about two works whose relative position nobody disputes.

The consequence for developmentalism

You cannot trace a development through a group you cannot order. Every interesting developmental claim — that the Laches precedes the Meno, that the Gorgias follows the aporetic dialogues, that the elenctic method has a career — lives inside or at the edge of the group Brandwood declares unorderable. Chronology delivers coarse bands: early < middle < late. Within the band that carries the argumentative weight, it delivers nothing.

06The seam — a 1992 chapter inside a 2022 volume

Worth noticing because it shows how the field moved while the chapter stood still.

Brandwood’s chapter opens with a flat developmentalist premise, stated as motivation:

his philosophical activity spanned some fifty years, during which time certain doctrines underwent considerable changes. To trace this development … it is essential to know in what order the dialogues were written. Brandwood, opening of ch. 3

That sits roughly three pages after an Introduction, written thirty years later by Ebrey and Kraut, reporting a “growing reluctance” to read the dialogues that way and recommending resources internal to each dialogue instead. The volume contains both, unreconciled.

What the seam is evidence of

Not incoherence on the editors’ part — they are reprinting a standard technical survey. It is evidence that the developmental premise was once so unremarkable that it served as a chapter’s opening motivation, and that this is no longer true. The chapter is a fossil of the consensus that Kahn 1996 and its successors displaced, preserved intact inside the volume that reports the displacement.

The methodological point that keeps Axis A from settling Axis C.

Fine’s position — stated in the Introduction to the Oxford Handbook of Plato, and present verbatim in the 2008 first edition as well as the 2019 second — is that chronology as such is neutral between developmentalism and unitarianism. Both parties can accept the same order of composition; they disagree about what the order licenses.

NEUTRALITY Let O be the true order of composition and D the claim that Plato’s doctrines changed. O does not entail D, and D does not follow from any purely stylometric fact. Establishing O is a precondition for stating D precisely; it is not evidence for it.

This is why the argument has to move somewhere else — and why Part Three goes to a case where the two readings make different predictions.

08The Handbook’s verdict — and the volume that issues it

In 2022 a reference work announced that the developmental consensus was dead. The announcement is worth reading closely, because the same volume disagrees with it twice.

The Bloomsbury Handbook of Plato, edited by Gerald A. Press and Mateo Duque, opens with an editorial survey of what has changed in the field. Its first “major development” is the collapse of the three-period division, and the verdict is unambiguous:

In Plato scholarship of the last thirty to thirty-five years, however, four major developments have undermined and resolved these divisions. First, through the work of Holger Thesleff, Debra Nails and others, it has come to be widely recognized that despite the general developmental consensus, there was never, in fact, agreement about exactly which dialogues went into the three groups. More significantly, it was shown that neither thematic nor stylistic evidence unambiguously supports the division of Plato’s dialogues into three groups. Evidence of revision and school accumulation has provided further evidence that assuming the dialogues to have been written in the way modern books are, at one time and by the author alone, is misleading. This has led to the demise of the developmental consensus that existed fifty years ago and a considerable weakening of the developmental-unitarian opposition. They also serve to undermine the project of finding an historically significant Socratic philosophy in Plato’s ‘early’ dialogues, and, in fact, those who pursue ‘Socratic philosophy’ now do so without making historical claims. Press & Duque, Introduction, The Bloomsbury Handbook of Plato (2022) p. 5

That paragraph is doing a great deal of work for this review, and it is the natural terminus of Part One as it stood: §05 showed a gradient rather than three groups, §06 showed a 1992 chapter reprinted unchanged in 2022, §07 showed that chronology is neutral anyway. Here is a reference volume saying the whole apparatus has gone. Three things should be said before that is accepted.

Do not read this as a survey finding. It is an editorial statement, in an introduction, about a field in which one of the editors has a thirty-year position. The four “major developments” are, in substance, the programme Gerald Press has advocated since Who Speaks for Plato? (2000). That is not an objection to the position — it is a reason to read the passage as advocacy rather than as a report.

The volume contradicts itself, and not subtly

Seventeen pages of the same book contain William Prior’s entry on developmentalism. It defines the position, traces it to Aristotle, surveys its varieties, and ends with a paragraph that does not report a demise:

Developmentalism has been the dominant interpretive paradigm for Platonic scholarship, particularly in the Anglo-American tradition, in the last century. It has, however, had its critics, beginning with unitarianism. W. J. Prior, ‘Developmentalism’, in Press & Duque (eds.) p. 402

Prior then lists the critics — Kahn, Nails and Thesleff on the stylometric side, Press on the doctrinal side — and stops. More significantly, the body of his entry describes live varieties of the position, and one of them is explicitly built to survive the stylometric collapse:

Penner (1992, 2002) argued that the relevant distinction between Socratic and Platonic dialogues is concerned with the soul: Socrates views the soul as simple, whereas Plato has a tripartite conception of the soul… This version of developmentalism has the advantage that it does not require modification of the stylometric distinction between the early and middle dialogues. Differing conceptions of dialectic provide the basis for yet another version of developmentalism, the Socratic method of elenchus being succeeded by the Platonic method of hypothesis and finally by the method of collection and division (Robinson 1953). Prior, same entry p. 402

That second variety — elenchus, then hypothesis, then division — is precisely the thesis Part Four of this review will find Kahn himself asserting about the Gorgias and the Meno. It is not a fossil. It is the live centre of the dispute.

And the volume’s own technical entry is more careful than its introduction

Debra Nails writes the Handbook’s entry on compositional chronology — the place where the claim would have to be substantiated. Her opening sentence is measured, and the scope of its verb matters:

It was once hoped that determining the order in which Plato composed his dialogues would permit the mapping of his philosophical development, but that approach, dominant for some hundred and fifty years, now creaks unreliably. D. Nails, ‘Compositional Chronology’, in Press & Duque (eds.) p. 402

What creaks is the inference from composition order to philosophical development. Not “has collapsed”; and the entry closes by sending the reader to Ryle’s “comprehensive, common-sense discussion” of composition order and to Irwin’s “case for the Anglo-American ‘standard view’ of the order of composition, distinguishing it from simple developmentalism” p. 406. An entry that ends by naming a defence is not an obituary.

Ausland on where the three periods came from

The Handbook also supplies the historical diagnosis, in Hayden Ausland’s account of the reception. Schleiermacher (1804) held that the dialogues form a series deliberately designed as such by Plato; K. F. Hermann converted that into an unfolding over which Plato had no authorial control; and the three periods we use are neither of those but a nineteenth-century British modification:

…in his 1867 edition of the Sophist and Politicus, Lewis Campbell found affinities in the diction of the presumably late Laws with that found in five other dialogues (Sph., Plt., Philebus, Timaeus, Critias). With this observation he paired a surmise that Plato had later in life re-considered both the political and the metaphysical idealism of the R. in favour of a compromise with the real world in both regards… But the conjectural three-period ordering and explanation proposed by Campbell in 1867 still remain those habitually assumed by Platonic scholars today, if usually without a sufficiently critical appreciation of their historical genesis or scientific fragility. H. W. Ausland, in Press & Duque (eds.) pp. 399–400

Two things in that are worth separating. The observation was linguistic and it has held: there is a late group. The surmise paired with it was doctrinal — that Plato softened in old age — and it was a conjecture about content dressed in the authority of a finding about diction. What the twentieth century inherited was the pair, and it has been using the second on the credit of the first ever since.

So the Handbook does not deliver a verdict; it delivers a disagreement. The rest of Part One tests the two scholars its introduction names.

09Nails: six criteria, and a circularity

The entry is a demolition conducted criterion by criterion. The cumulative shape is the argument.

What the ancient evidence actually gives

Two data bear directly on order. Aristotle at Politics 1264b26 says the Republic antedates the Laws. Diogenes Laertius (3.37) says the Laws were “in the wax” at Plato’s death and were transcribed by Philip of Opus. To that Nails adds the linguistic evidence catalogued by Thesleff — “notable synchysis, plummeting rate of hiatus, absence of certain clausulae” — which marks out a group: Timaeus, Critias, Politicus, Sophist, Philebus and Epistles 7 and 8, “all as late and edited with the same stylistic principles in view, perhaps those of Philip.”

THE WHOLE SECURE BASIS One relation (R. before Lg.) and one group, at one end. Every ordering ever proposed is inference from that.

And then the sentence that is the entry’s central negative finding — which should be read for exactly what it says and no more:

Not unreasonably, scholars sought definitive style-markers of R. that would permit the identification of a set of R.-like dialogues, leaving a third and earlier set, yet more remote from Lg. Although that effort has failed to distinguish the ‘early’ and ‘middle’ groups reliably, the bulk of modern scholarship has nevertheless followed Campbell’s nineteenth-century identification of three periods of productivity. Nails pp. 402–3

The late group stands. The early/middle boundary does not. That is the same result §05 reached from Brandwood’s numbers, arrived at independently.

The four criteria

1 · literary
Founders on the exceptions

The proposal: predominance of Socratic questioning marks a work early, of constructive speeches, middle; and dialogues in direct speech postdate the Theaetetus. Nails’s reply is by counterexample: the criterion “founders on the exceptions: for example, Laches and Ion would be post-Tht.; Ti. and Parmenides would be pre-Tht., defying considerations of content.” Several dialogues mix the modes.

2 · stylometric
The method returns its inputs

This is the sharpest paragraph in the entry, and it is a finding about method rather than about results:

The advent of computers allowed measurement and correlation of very large numbers of stylistic features and heralded a new interest in stylometry, but the problem of how to programme the computer reiterated Campbell’s original problem: the only invulnerable datum was the relationship between R. and Lg., insufficient for generating secure results. Ledger (1989) marked an advance with new programmes that did not require prior assumptions about what to count as early style; but his preliminary results did not confirm scholars’ preconceptions about the order of the dialogues. Brandwood (1990), less concerned about what he had to assume to produce his results, delivered more palatable fare, that is, confirming expectations. Nails pp. 403–4

The Ledger/Brandwood contrast is the case in two sentences. The study that minimised prior assumptions failed to reproduce the consensus; the study that did not, reproduced it. That is the signature of a technique returning what was fed into it. Nails adds the detail that damages the three-period picture from inside stylometry: “both R. bk 1 and the first part of Prm. usually cluster with the Socratic dialogues considered early.” The clustering cuts across dialogues, not between them — a fact §10 will explain.

3 · thematic
Two difficulties, and Nails calls them insurmountable

(a) “the view considered most highly evolved depended entirely on the existing views of the scholars who performed the investigations”;

(b) “when dialogues addressing more than one subject were compared, a dialogue might be ‘highly developed’ on one subject and introductory on another, while another dialogue would have the reverse configuration, leaving no obvious way to determine which had been written earlier.”

(a) is circularity at the level of the criterion. (b) is the more serious, and it does not depend on anyone’s bias: if development is multi-dimensional, no single ordering can be read off it even in principle. This is the formal version of what §07 says about neutrality, and it is stronger, because it makes the thematic route unavailable rather than merely uninformative.

4 · historical
As various as the events chosen

Absolute dates inferred from allusions to events, rivals or persons. Nails’s treatment is brief and the brevity is itself the verdict: the death of Theaetetus gives 392 on one reconstruction (Kirchner 1901) and 369 on another (Vogt 1909–10); Theban politics, Antisthenes, Isocrates, the Cynics and the Cyrenaics have each been used, with the results one would expect.

The circularity result

Then the paragraph that turns the four criteria against each other, and which is the strongest thing in the entry:

Consensus about the order of composition has stayed firmly out of reach for several reasons. Despite the wondrously exact results achieved by many of these scholars, their results contradict one another within and across the methods used. Moreover, a circularity problem confounds (1)-(4): each suggested order depends on first positing a pre-R. exemplar for which no independent confirmation has ever been credible, though the Apology has been an unfortunate favourite — unfortunate because court speeches are not reliably compared to dialogues. Nails p. 404, citing Nails 1995: 53–63

Stated generally: every method of ordering needs a fixed point at the early end to measure distance from. The late end has one, supplied by Aristotle and Diogenes. The early end has none. So an exemplar must be posited; the ordering is generated relative to it; and the ordering cannot then confirm the exemplar without circularity. The Apology is the usual choice and it is a bad one, because it is not a dialogue.

The two structural findings

Criteria (5) and (6) are different in kind. They do not compete with the first four — they undermine the question the first four were trying to answer.

5 · revision
A work may have no single date

“Textual evidence and testimony that Plato edited or rewrote dialogues in his lifetime confound any attempt to set a single date of composition for any dialogue.” The dossier: Gorgias (Tarrant 1982; Thesleff 2003, 2007), Protagoras (Frede 1986), Cratylus (Sedley 2003), Alcibiades I (Thesleff 2017), Theaetetus (Bastianini & Sedley 1995; Tarrant 2010, 2016, 2020); and the Laws, where Tarrant has distinguished three styles inside the single work. The testimony is Dionysius of Halicarnassus, who reports that Plato “combed and curled in every direction” the opening line of Republic I.

The logical force is easy to understate. If a work has no single date, the object the enterprise sought — a linear ordering of dated works — does not exist to be found. That is not a difficulty about evidence. It is a difficulty about the target.

6 · overlap
Short dialogues written alongside long ones

“Short dialogues may have been written during the periods when longer ones — Grg., R., Lg. — were being conceived and executed, resisting any linear chronology.” Nails develops the Republic case at length: a freestanding Republic I, and a proto-Republic of two scrolls comprising much of II, most of III and the start of V.

Her best argument here is not stylistic or doctrinal but bibliographical. Aristophanes’ Ecclesiazusae of 392/1 contains “more than a score of exact parallels to the language and proposals of R. bk 2-3 and 5”; Aulus Gellius mentions a two-scroll version; and the summary at the opening of the Timaeus, the summary at Republic VIII, and Aristotle’s summary in Politics II all summarise those same parts and no others. Three independent ancient summaries agreeing on which parts existed is evidence of a different order from any style count.

What Nails does not claim

She does not say Plato’s thought failed to develop. She does not say the three periods are false. She says the project of recovering development from composition order creaks — and ends by naming two treatments the reader should consult, one of them a defence of the standard view. The distance between that and “the demise of the developmental consensus” is the distance between a method in serious trouble and a thesis refuted.

10Thesleff: dispersion, function, revision, audience

Four planks across three books. Three are negative; the fourth is positive, and it is what makes the other three bite.

Thesleff’s case runs across Studies in the Styles of Plato (1967), Studies in Platonic Chronology (1982) and Studies in Plato’s Two-Level Model (1999), collected in 2009 as Platonic Patterns. It is the source Nails cites for the Laws mannerisms, for the summary of stylometry, and for the proto-Republic; and it is the deeper half of the demolition, because it supplies a rival explanation rather than only a set of objections.

Plank one · the conspectus

Pages 153–65 of the Chronology print every proposed ordering from Tennemann (1792) onward, each with the criteria its author used. Read consecutively it is a remarkable document, and Thesleff’s note on why he prints it is characteristically dry:

…in general the lack of cumulative linearity in the progress of research of this kind, perhaps will also justify the printing of the list here: a later study is not necessarily closer to the truth than an earlier one. Thesleff, Platonic Patterns p. 153

A reviewer counts one hundred and thirty-two distinct chronologies in it. The inference Thesleff intends is the one Nails reaches by another route at Agora, Academy 53–63, and the two are best read as a single finding:

DISPERSION IS THE EVIDENCE A method that tracked a real signal would converge. Where that many scholars derive that many different results from the same evidence, the fault is not in any one of them — it is in the supposition that the evidence determines an answer.

Plank two · style tracks function, not date

This is the plank the Handbook’s introduction does not mention, and it does the most work. Studies in the Styles of Plato is not primarily a book about chronology. It is an attempt to describe what Plato’s styles do. Thesleff distinguishes ten classes — colloquial, semi-literary conversational, rhetorical, pathetic, intellectual, mythic narrative, historical, ceremonious, legal, and the onkos style — and then asks what governs their distribution. The answer is dramatic and rhetorical function:

3. Rhetorical style. … It is commonly employed for play and parody in the early and middle works… But commonly also, and indeed more often, it serves Platonic persuasion above the mimetic level. Then it naturally occurs mainly in monologues or speeches…

4. Pathetic style. … Its primary function is to add to the liveliness of the style, and hence it is particularly common in interludes. It is largely mimetic…

5. Intellectual style. … on the whole, it seems that it serves a serious purpose in Platonic argument, and also in visions… It is significant that the intellectual style is particularly manifest in those works which develop the methods of Platonic philosophy, Men., Phd., Prm., Tht., R. VII–IX…

8. Ceremonious style. A solemn style, mostly used … to add loftiness to myths and visions and (in the late works) speeches. Thesleff, Studies in the Styles of Plato pp. 136–8

Consider what that does to stylometry. If the incidence of a stylistic feature is set by whether a passage is a myth, an interlude, a parody, a stretch of formal rhetoric or a stretch of dialectic, then measuring its frequency across a whole dialogue measures the mix of passage types in that dialogue. Two works will resemble each other stylometrically when they contain similar proportions of similar kinds of writing. That is a fact about their contents and their form, and there is no reason it should be a fact about their dates.

This explains what would otherwise be a coincidence. §09 recorded Nails’s observation that Republic I and the first part of the Parmenides cluster with the “early” dialogues. On the chronological reading those are anomalies needing separate hypotheses — and the literature has duly supplied them. On Thesleff’s they are predictions: both are stretches of elenctic conversation, and the “early” dialogues are largely made of that. The feature the counting detects is present.

His verdict is blunt:

Traditional chronological stylometry cannot be expected to yield useful results. Thesleff p. 227

And he is careful to distinguish that from a rejection of statistical method. Three pages later he recommends “a very broad ‘cluster stylometry’” on large bodies of material by computer, and a 1981 postscript reports his own tests, run with K. Loimaranta on Thesaurus Linguae Graecae tapes of the Burnet text, with Xenophon as a control. The results are a stylometrist’s refutation of stylometry, conducted with the tools:

(d) The differences in the use of particles are on the whole greater between the three types of exposition studied … than between the various works in the Corpus.
(e) The differences … are greater between ‘obviously’ authentic works such as Chrm., Grg., Phd. and Smp., than between the authentic works en bloc and various dubia or spuria.
(f) No statistically reliable dialogue clusters were found apart from [the Xenophon control, the late group, and a group around Phdr., Prm. I, R. II–X and Tht.]… Thesleff p. 230 n. 202

Result (d) is the empirical form of the functional thesis: passage type is a larger source of variance than date of composition. Result (e) is a warning that the technique cannot separate the certainly genuine from the doubtful, which is a low bar it does not clear.

Plank three · revision and semi-authenticity

Nails’s criterion (5) is Thesleff’s, and he states its target precisely — not a claim about the texts, but about an unexamined assumption concerning publication:

The modern view of Platonic chronology rests to some extent upon premises concerning methods of publication which are probably anachronistic. It is taken for granted or implied that the finishing of a manuscript for a dialogue was in most cases the result of a single effort within a relatively short space of time. Thesleff p. 230

To that he adds “semi-authenticity”: composition in cooperation with younger members of the Academy, with the institution functioning in a role “analogous to that of a modern copyright-supervising body of publishers” p. 231. The consequence is that “Platonic dialogue” may name a text with a compositional history rather than a date — and on his account a great deal of the corpus does.

Plank four · different audiences

The fourth plank is the positive one, and it is the alternative explanation of everything developmentalism explains by date. Thesleff sets it out in the 2009 Introduction, as a list of complications which “taken together, exclude the possibility that the dialogues reflect a steady line of linguistic change”:

In spite of considerable opposition by some authorities, it seemed in the 1980s that the stylometrists had won the game. I tried to show that they had not. … I point out the numerous complications that, taken together, exclude the possibility that the dialogues reflect a steady line of linguistic change. Such complications include the revision of the texts; Plato’s cooperation with younger friends (‘semiauthenticity’); the oral discussion behind the texts, which may have affected even the choice of style; and, above all, the different audiences to whom the written dialogues were originally addressed. Thesleff, Introduction to Platonic Patterns pp. xiv–xv

Applied to the group that matters most for this review:

It seems to me easier to argue (as I did) that most of the so-called ‘early’ dialogues were written for different audiences and different purposes after the Academy had been ‘founded.’ Thesleff p. 390 n.
THE RIVAL HYPOTHESIS Developmentalism explains stylistic and doctrinal variation by when. Thesleff explains it by for whom and for what. Both fit the data. Neither is entailed by it. This is exactly the position in which a criterion is needed to decide — and §09 is the report that no such criterion has survived.

Plank five · the confrontation with Brandwood

The four planks above are Thesleff’s general case. There is also a specific one, and it is aimed at the source §05 relies on. Thesleff reviewed Brandwood’s 1958 dissertation closely — he calls it, in the same breath, “well-informed and prudent” and “in most respects of great value” — and then says it does not go nearly far enough:

Consequently, any attempt to determine stylometrically the internal chronological order of the dialogues in the ‘late group,’ not to mention the ‘middle’ and ‘early’ groups, rests methodologically upon precarious premises. It is regrettable that this fact was not clearly envisaged by Brandwood (1958) … Brandwood rejected all attempts so far to establish the internal chronology of the ‘early’ and ‘middle’ groups on linguistic grounds; but he did not find the method inapplicable in principle. And for the ‘late group,’ certain apparent linguistic trends, notably in the use of clausulae and the avoidance of hiatus, led him to accept the order Timaeus—Critias—Sophist—Politicus—Philebus—Laws… Whatever one thinks of the reliability of clausulae and hiatus as Platonic style markers, the method is not basically sound. Thesleff, Platonic Patterns p. 218

Two distinct disagreements are packed into that paragraph, and they matter differently.

Disagreement one · how far the result reaches
i
The scope of the negative result
Brandwood stops at the early and middle groups. Thesleff carries it into the late group as well — not the membership of that group, which he grants as stylometry’s “only indisputable achievement” p. 246, but its sequence. He notes that “neither Campbell nor Dittenberger attempted to ascertain the internal order within the ‘late group’ on linguistic grounds” p. 217 n. 188 — the founders of the method were more cautious than their heirs.
Disagreement two · what kind of result it is
ii
The kind of negative result
Brandwood rejects the attempts so far; Thesleff rejects the instrument. That is the difference between a verdict of insufficient evidence — repairable by a better dataset, which is exactly what Ledger 1989 and Brandwood 1990 set out to supply — and a verdict that the object being measured does not have the shape the measurement presupposes. Only the second is immune to a stylometric revival — which is why §01 does not rely on either.

The ground of (ii) is stated a page earlier, and it is a claim about authorship in general, not about Plato:

It does not seem to me likely, even in theory, that the direction of the change in individual style normally follows a distinct trend which could be characterized quantitatively as a steady increase in certain features … and a steady decrease in others. And it is even less probable that the characteristics of such a trend, if it does exist, could be somehow derived by interpolation or extrapolation from two separate works of an author for which the chronological order is known — say, the Republic and the Laws … Do such consistent and manifestly traceable trends ever occur in literature? The burden of proof rests with those who assert this. Thesleff pp. 217–18

And the coarse bands go too

§05 concedes Brandwood a residue: whatever happens inside the early group, the three bands themselves — early < middle < late — survive. Thesleff denies exactly that residue, and he does it by catching Brandwood in a change of confidence between 1958 and 1976.

The critical assessment of earlier work made by Brandwood 1958 conveys less confidence than what would appear from the preface of his Word Index 1976, especially XVI f.: “It can be taken as reasonably established on the basis of stylistic investigations that all works of Group I. were written before those of Group II with the possible exception of R. I, and that these in turn were written before those of Group III… As regards the chronological sequence of individual works, nothing definite can be said. . . .” As for the order of the ‘early’ groups, this is very far from certain. Thesleff p. 219 n. 196

This is the same shape as the seam in §06 — a scholar’s summary statement standing at a higher confidence than his own technical work supports — found independently, in a different scholar, by a different reader, thirty years earlier.

Where Thesleff is harder on the early group than Brandwood

Brandwood’s verdict is that the early group cannot be ordered. That presupposes there is a determinate order which the instrument fails to recover. Thesleff denies the presupposition — and then, having denied it, produces an ordering anyway, on entirely non-stylometric grounds. His Concluding Chronological Sketch pp. 381–2 lays the corpus out in three columns which are not dates at all:

Exoteric · Esoteric · Dubious and spurious
The columns are audiences, not dates
The organising axis is audience. Several dialogues appear twice, in two versions (Gorgias1 c. 390 and Gorgias2 c. 380; likewise Symposium, Phaedrus, Theaetetus, Protagoras, Menexenus), which is the revision thesis cashed out: a text with two versions has no date.
What the sketch does to the early group
The dialogues on which the Socratic-period reading depends are scattered across thirty years, and half of them sit in the third column: Laches c. 373 and Euthyphro c. 370 among the dubia, Hippias Major as late as c. 360, Charmides and Lysis c. 375, Protagoras1 c. 385, Gorgias1 and Menexenus1 c. 390 — and Republic I1 c. 390, a decade before Republic II onwards is begun.
Brandwood: the early group cannot be ordered. Thesleff: there is no early group to order, and much of it is not early.

What this review may and may not take from him

Usable: the negative arguments — dispersion, function, revision, semi-authenticity, audience, and the two disagreements with Brandwood above. Each is an argument, and each stands or falls on its own.

Not usable as evidence: the sketch itself. Thesleff disclaims it in terms that cut hard against anyone who would cite it as a result: “The chronology deducible from the considerations made in the above chapters is, as has been repeatedly stated, a theory. It requires much additional testing, and confirmation or refutation as to detail, before it can be taken as a basis for a fresh interpretation of Plato’s work. Even so, I trust it is no less reliable than any other theory of Platonic chronology so far produced” p. 381. Having argued that no such theory is reliable, “no less reliable than any other” is a very small claim, and he means it to be. It also places Brandwood 1958 as entry no. 67 in his own conspectus of published chronologies — which is the dispersion argument of plank one applied to the source of §05.

His own verdict — and his own caution

Part II of the Chronology opens with the summary of where the negative case leaves things:

The results arrived at so far in the present study are for the most part negative. The sad and perhaps irritating truth is that the strict, reliable and elegantly linear Platonic chronology dreamed of by earlier generations of scholars, seems to be today even more remote than ever.

Should we give up? Thesleff p. 245

He does not give up. Part II builds a new model and ends with “a tentative sketch of a new chronology, open to further debate.” Thesleff is not an anti-chronologist; he is a chronologist who thinks the existing methods worthless and proposes better ones, and his own reconstruction is itself a developmental story of a kind — a proto-Republic preceding the text we have, the dramatic dialogue form arising in the Academy.

And on the question this review is asking, he is explicit that it remains open:

Much more careful study and pondering is needed to make sure what in the dialogues reflects a real development in thought (and style), and what are suggestions and experiments for different readers. Thesleff p. 390 n.

Set that beside the Handbook’s “demise.” Thesleff’s position is that we cannot presently tell development from audience-variation — not that there was no development.

11What the demolition establishes — and the three developmentalisms

Two lists, kept apart. Most of the confusion in this area comes from running them together.

established
What Nails and Thesleff have shown

1 · A late group exists and is stylistically distinct — Ti., Criti., Sph., Plt., Phlb., Lg., with Epistles 7–8. Anchored externally by Aristotle and Diogenes. Not in dispute, and both authors rely on it.

2 · The early/middle boundary has no secure evidential basis. Nails: “that effort has failed to distinguish the ‘early’ and ‘middle’ groups reliably.” Independently confirmed by Brandwood’s own gradient at §05.

3 · No stylometric result is independent of its assumptions. Ledger vs Brandwood; Thesleff’s functional account of style; the Loimaranta cluster tests.

4 · Every proposed order is circular at the early end — it presupposes an exemplar it cannot independently confirm.

5 · Thematic development cannot in principle yield a single ordering, because development is multi-dimensional (Nails’s objection (b)).

6 · Several dialogues were revised and some compiled; a work may have no single date, so the object sought may not exist.

7 · Stylistic variation in Plato is largely governed by passage function — and variance between exposition types exceeds variance between works.

8 · There is a rival explanation of the same data: audience and purpose rather than date.

not established
What does not follow

That Plato’s thought did not develop. Nobody argues this. Thesleff explicitly leaves it open; Nails does not address it.

That the three-period division is false. The arguments show it is unsupported, which is a different result. An unsupported division may still be true — and this one may well be, for all that has been shown.

That the late group is doubtful. It is the one secure result in the field.

That unitarianism wins by default. Unitarianism is a positive thesis about doctrinal unity (see §03) and needs its own argument. The collapse of a dating method supplies none.

That there is a consensus that developmentalism is finished. Prior’s entry in the same volume; the Stanford Encyclopedia’s treatment; Irwin’s defence of the standard view, to which Nails herself directs the reader.

Three claims travel under one name

The question “is developmentalism over?” cannot be answered until they are separated. It is the same discipline §03 applied to unitarianism, applied now to the other side.

D1 · CHRONOLOGICAL The dialogues fall into three compositionally distinct groups, and the groups can be identified by stylistic and thematic evidence.
D2 · PHILOSOPHICAL Plato’s thought changed over his life, and the changes can be read off the dialogues taken in order.
D3 · THE SOCRATIC THESIS A subset of the dialogues represents the philosophy of the historical Socrates rather than Plato’s own.

D1 is in serious trouble at the early/middle boundary and secure at the late one. That is not a nuance; it is the actual state of the evidence, and both Nails and Thesleff say so. A scholar who writes “the late dialogues” stands on firm ground. A scholar who writes “the early dialogues” is using a category whose boundary has never been fixed by a method that does not assume it.

D2 has lost its instrument but not its subject. Everything in §§09–10 attacks the inference from composition order to development; none of it touches the antecedent likelihood that a man writing for fifty years changed his mind. What has gone is the claim to trace the change by ordering the texts. This is why the Stanford Encyclopedia can still record the question as open rather than closed — noting that “it remains a matter of dispute whether the division of Plato’s works into three periods … does correctly indicate the order of composition, and whether it is a useful tool,” and that even for the secure late six “there is, as yet, no agreement among students of Plato that these six form a distinctive stage in his philosophical development.” The same finding at both ends: the compositional group is secure, the philosophical stage is not.

D3 is the casualty — and it is the one the Handbook’s introduction is right about. It has been abandoned, and those who now pursue “Socratic philosophy” do so, as the editors say, “without making historical claims.” The reason is structural. D3 requires D1 at exactly the boundary where D1 fails, and requires it to carry a further weight besides: that the works on one side of the line report someone else’s views. With the line unsecured, the historical thesis has nothing to stand on. This is the same conclusion Part Two reaches by an entirely different route — through Aristotle, Irwin and the four gates — and the convergence of a stylometric argument and a source-critical one on the same casualty is the strongest single result in this review.

So: is developmentalism over? No — but the strong version is, and the strong version is what Anglophone Socratic scholarship has mostly been relying on. What is finished is the Vlastosian package: three groups fixed by stylometry, mapped onto three philosophical stages, with the first stage attributed to the historical Socrates. Each of its three joints has been shown unsupported and one has been generally given up. What is not finished is the modest claim that Plato wrote the Laws late and changed his mind about some things — nor the versions Prior describes, Penner’s and Robinson’s, which never depended on stylometry and survive its collapse intact.

One observation about the sociology, because it explains why the answer feels less clear-cut than it is. The three-period division is enormously convenient: it supplies a way of citing, a way of building a syllabus, and a way of quarantining inconvenient passages. Ausland’s phrase at §08 is the exact description — “habitually assumed… without a sufficiently critical appreciation of their historical genesis or scientific fragility.” A framework can be simultaneously without evidential support and in universal use, and that is the actual condition of Platonic chronology. Announcing its demise is premature; relying on it without argument is no longer respectable.

Which is why the argument cannot stop here. If chronology cannot settle the question — and §07 already said it would not, even if it were secure — the two readings have to be taken somewhere they make different predictions. Part Two removes the external prop; Part Three finds the test case.

Socrates

The historical Socrates has functioned as the main external prop for the developmental inference. If the early dialogues report a real man’s philosophy, you have a non-textual reason to think that material is not Plato’s own, and therefore a reason to read the later divergence as Plato coming into his own.

Part Two does two things: it shows that the prop is weaker than it looks, and then shows that the developmental argument does not need it — which is the more important result.

The payoff of §16: once developmentalism is indexed to textual stages rather than to biography, Kahn’s best-known objection has nothing to bite on, and the whole Aristotle question becomes irrelevant to the philosophy.

12Kahn’s creativity objection — target and backfire

The most quoted sentence in the anti-developmental case, and the least load-bearing.

it is highly implausible to assume that a philosopher as creative as Plato should remain fixed in the position of his master for a dozen years or more Kahn, first objection to Vlastos
What it targets
Vlastos specifically, not development

Read the emphasised phrase. The objection is to remaining fixed in someone else’s position — which is Vlastos’s ventriloquist version, where SocratesE is the historical man’s philosophy that Plato held and expounded before finding his own.

Substitute the neutral formulation — the early dialogues present position P₁, whoever first held it; the Meno marks a transition; the middle dialogues present P₂ — and the objection has no purchase. A creative philosopher holding his own view at forty and a better one at fifty is the default case, not an implausible one.

And it cuts the other way
The premise favours development

If Plato was too creative to stand still for a dozen years, why is he creative enough to possess the whole system by thirty and then spend twenty years withholding it? The proleptic Plato is the more static of the two. “Too restless to stand still” cannot be run as an argument for “he stood still and staged the reveal.”

It is also, as an argument, a biographical premise about a man whose biography we do not have — not textual evidence.

Kahn’s actual evidence, in fairness

The creativity line is a crowbar to loosen Vlastos, not the foundation. What Kahn rests on is (i) the literary premise — a dialogue is fiction, and we do not infer a novelist’s beliefs from what chapter 3 omits; and (ii) proleptic textual data — anticipations in the early dialogues aimed at later material. Point (ii) is genuine textual argument and has to be met on its merits.

13What Aristotle actually says

Before asking how much weight the testimony bears, it is worth being exact about what it consists of. It is not what the developmental tradition’s shorthand suggests.

The thing he never says

Aristotle nowhere names a dialogue as a record of Socrates. There is no sentence in the corpus of the form “the Laches gives us Socrates.” What he does is two quite different things, and the tension between them is the whole problem.

(a) His default is that the dialogues are evidence for Plato — Socrates included

He mentions the Phaedo (Met. 991b3–4) and “Socrates in the Phaedo” (GC 335b9–17); alludes under Plato’s name to the Phaedrus, Theaetetus, Sophist and Philebus; and contrasts the Timaeus with the unwritten doctrines (Phys. 209b11–16).

The datum that settles the naivety question the other way
The Laws as a “Socratic discourse”

“Aristotle’s criticism of the Republic and the Laws in Politics II provides a striking example of his identification of Plato with the Platonic Socrates. After speaking of the Socrates in the Republic, Aristotle goes on to speak of the Laws as another ‘Socratic discourse’ (1265a10–13), even though Socrates is not a speaker in the Laws.” He then treats both dialogues as evidence of Plato’s views (1266b5, 1271b1, 1274b9–10).

The argument from silence around this is strong. Aristotle does all of it while attacking Plato, before an audience that included Plato’s own school, during or just after Plato’s lifetime. If the character Socrates were not understood as Plato’s mouthpiece, “a defender of Plato would only have had to remind Aristotle that the Platonic Socrates was not meant to represent Plato’s views.” Nobody appears to have done so.

(b) Separately, he ascribes specific doctrines to a Socrates who is not Plato

(c) There is a grammatical marker, but it is a rule of thumb

Fitzgerald’s canon, stated honestly
A philological tendency, not a declaration of sources

When Aristotle discusses the dialogue character he usually writes “the Socrates”; for the historical man, “Socrates” without the article (Fitzgerald 1853: 163; defended by Ross 1924, I xxxix–xli). Irwin’s own note concedes the difficulties, citing Deman 1942 and Nehamas 1992: 169–71, and adds: “The exceptions to the general rule do not undermine its general reliability. In any case, our decision on this point does not affect the main evidence.”

The fair verdict

The honest charge against Aristotle is not dishonesty. It is that his interest is doxographical, in service of his own metaphysics; that he never states a source; and that on the Laws he is demonstrably loose. His categories are doctrinal, not documentary. That is enough to make him worth taking seriously, and not enough to make him decisive.

It also shows the sceptical worry has been mis-stated. The question is not whether Aristotle naively took every Platonic Socrates for the historical man — the Laws passage proves he did not. The question is why he carves out exceptions at all, which is where §14 begins.

Irwin’s argument — stronger than a tally, and the reason he needs it

T. H. Irwin, “The Platonic Corpus,” ch. 3 of the Oxford Handbook of Plato (2019), pp. 76–77, answers Kahn by name (n. 24 cites Kahn 1996: 79–87, which “discounts Aristotle’s [testimony]”). His argument is not a tally. It is an argument from selectivity.

The argument from discrimination
A criterion is needed, and no internal criterion supplies it

Aristotle assigns some views in the dialogues to the historical Socrates and marks others as Plato’s. So he discriminates among dialogues — and therefore “cannot … have naively supposed that the character Socrates always puts forward the views of the historical Socrates. He must have supposed that he had some reason.”

Irwin then eliminates the internal candidates. Doctrinal conflict would show only that the dialogues disagree, not which side is Socrates’. Biographical fullness is refuted by a counterexample: “The Phaedo has more to say than any other dialogue about Socrates’ life and philosophical development, but Aristotle takes it to present a Platonic, not a Socratic, theory of forms.”

Conclusion: “The internal evidence of the dialogues could not have persuaded Aristotle that some, but not others, are about Socrates. Probably, then, Aristotle ascribes some of the views of the Platonic Socrates to the historical Socrates because he has external evidence.” And he had access: the Academy from 367, thirty-two years after Socrates’ death; people who had known him; and a motive, since Plato was not the only writer of Socratic dialogues.

Correction to the framing above
“Minority” understates the position

Smith’s 7-of-26 tally is a minority argument. But Irwin reaches the same conclusion by a different and stronger route, in the same handbook, against Kahn by name. The honest description of the state of play is contested between heavyweights, not fringe versus consensus. What remains true is that the sceptical reading has held the recent decades, and that the Stanford Encyclopedia’s Socrates entry omits Aristotle from its sources altogether.

Where Irwin’s argument is exposed

1 · The gap between “not from Plato” and “about the historical Socrates.” The other Sōkratikoi logoi — Antisthenes, Aeschines, Xenophon, Eucleides, Aristippus — sit in that gap. Irwin invokes them as Aristotle’s motive for seeking external evidence, but they are also an available source. If the criterion came from that literature, Aristotle’s independence from Plato is real while his independence from fiction is not. That is precisely the reception point: the genre was invention, not report.

2 · The Phaedo counterexample is weaker than it looks. Aristotle’s own discriminating mark is separation (Met. M.4 1078b30–32; M.9 1086b3–5) — a doctrinal criterion he could apply from the inside, and one that cleanly separates the Phaedo from the definitional dialogues regardless of how much biography it contains. Irwin’s reply must be that a prior fix on which side is Socrates is still needed; but that fix could come from the Apology plus diffuse Athenian memory rather than privileged testimony.

3 · He concedes one of his own props. n. 17: “In (1) I take a disputed position on the legitimacy of ‘Fitzgerald’s canon.’”

15Irwin’s three options — and what Aristotle cannot decide

The most useful thing in Irwin’s treatment is his own concession about how little the testimony is needed, and how little it settles.

study of the dialogues reveals the sorts of differences that Aristotle describes; and even without his help we ought to be able to see the differences for ourselves. Without his evidence we would not know that the differences have anything to do with the historical Socrates, but we would have to take account of them somehow in our account of Plato’s development. Irwin, Plato’s Ethics p. 10

Strip Aristotle out entirely and the doctrinal differences remain and still demand explanation. What the testimony contributes is one thing only: the claim that the difference is a difference between two men rather than between two stages or two authorial purposes. Irwin then sets out the three surviving options.

The doctrines are genuinely Socrates’, as Aristotle says
Plato develops from agreement with his teacher toward independence. developmental
Not Socrates’, but Plato’s own earlier views
Still development — just without the biography. This is the decoupled position of §16. developmental
Neither
Plato “does not introduce them into the dialogues because he believes them, but because he wants to discuss them.” Kahn’s option
The choice between the first and the second explanation depends on our estimate of Aristotle’s credibility. Irwin, Plato’s Ethics p. 10
The consequence, and it is decisive for this review
Aristotle adjudicates 1 versus 2. He does not touch 1–or–2 versus 3.

Options 1 and 2 are both developmental. The testimony picks between them. Option 3 is prolepsis — a claim about Plato’s compositional intent — and Aristotle never says a word about compositional intent, in either direction.

Aristotle is evidence about whose view a doctrine was, not about why a dialogue was written. Those are different questions, and only the second is load-bearing for the argument this review runs.

Two things this settles

1 · It vindicates the decoupling (§16) on the philosophical axis. Moving from option 1 to option 2 costs nothing philosophically, because both are developmental and Aristotle is the only thing that separates them. So the whole Aristotle question really is idle for the interpretation — which was your instinct several steps back.

2 · And it exposes a circularity in the ordering use. Irwin’s handbook chapter uses Aristotle’s testimony, with doctrinal considerations, to place Group 3 after Groups 1–2 (§16). But that use presupposes we are already inside options 1–2. Aristotle can order the groups only within the developmental family; he cannot license the family itself. So the testimony cannot be both the evidence for development and the instrument that fixes the order development is read off.

Gate 1 · The sources are the wrong kind of text

This is the strongest finding in the literature, and it is not a modern imposition: the classification is Aristotle’s own.

The genre, as antiquity classified it
Sōkratikoi logoi as mimēsis

“It was Aristotle who invented the term Sōkratikoi logoi (Poetics 1447b1–3), and the context in which he introduced it is revealing. He described them as the prose equivalents of the verse form mime… He also described both mimes and Sōkratikoi logoi as ‘imitative’, which is his term for ‘fictional’.” Waterfield p. 9

“The main difference between mimes and Sōkratikoi logoi was that the latter showed historical characters in fictional settings, whereas in mimes even the characters were fictional.” p. 9

Dorion draws the methodological consequence, and it is the sharpest formulation of the whole problem — that the question has been mis-posed from the start:

The Socratic problem has all the makings of a false problem because it rests on a misunderstanding… For the Socratic problem as it had been debated since the start of the nineteenth century to have meaning, the principal direct witnesses (Xenophon and Plato) must have intended to faithfully reconstruct Socrates’ ideas… Yet everything seems to indicate that neither Xenophon nor Plato set out with the intention of faithfully reporting Socrates’ ideas. Dorion, “The Rise and Fall of the Socratic Problem,” Cambridge Companion to Socrates pp. 7–8
Xenophon’s and Plato’s Socratic writings belong to a literary genre — that of the logos sokratikos, which Aristotle explicitly recognized and which authorizes by its very nature a certain degree of fiction and a great freedom of invention as far as the setting and content are concerned, most notably with the ideas expressed by the different characters. Dorion p. 8

And Waterfield reports the consensus rather than claiming novelty:

It is in fact generally acknowledged that the Sōkratikoi logoi are a species of fiction, and that the differences between the views of Socrates we find in the Socratics tell us more about the authors than they do about the historical Socrates… each of them used Socrates as an ideal and a cipher, for his own purposes. Waterfield pp. 9–10

Verdict on Gate 1

Demanding historical fidelity from a logos sokratikos is a category mistake about the genre. The texts were not built to bear it, and the ancient critic who named the genre said so.

Where he matters: §16 (gate 2) · and the reception axis in the residue box

If the Sōkratikoi logoi were the first literary use of Socrates, the fiction finding would still leave open that each author invented from life. Bromberg’s two chapters close that door: a public “Socrates” existed on the Athenian stage before the prose tradition, and it shaped what followed.

The scale of the comic tradition
Seven comedies, five poets, one decade

“the evidence for (at least) seven comedies by five comedians satirizing intellectuals in similar ways within a decade of one another seems sufficient to argue for a subgenre of Old Comedy, akin to ‘demagogue comedies’ and ‘mythological burlesques.’” Stavru & Moore p. 34

“423 bce saw two comedies at the Dionysia featuring Socrates as a character (Ameipsias’ Connus, and Aristophanes’ Clouds)” p. 47; and “we know that Socrates himself was specifically singled out for ridicule by several comedians besides Aristophanes, especially by Ameipsias and Eupolis” p. 49.

What matters is not the count but what the count shows: the comic “Socrates” is assembled from a transferable repertoire applied indifferently to Hippon, Protagoras, Damon and Prodicus:

intellectuals and their students in Old Comedy are neither all-seeing nor provident: they are, on the contrary, ‘forgetful’… and ‘gullible’… ‘pretentious’… ‘beggars’… ‘braggarts’… ‘supple, dissembling, slippery, pretentious’… ‘thieves’… and sloppy dancers. Bromberg pp. 46–47 — each predicate attested across several poets and several targets
The direction of fit — against Dover
Comedy did not report a famous Socrates. It made him famous.

“Dover argues that Socrates’ behavior at Potidaea and Delium… had made him the subject of conversations… But it seems more likely that these two plays helped thrust Socrates fully, and perhaps unexpectedly, out of anonymity.” p. 55

“his appearance in two plays in a single Dionysia, in front of as many as 17,000 spectators, surely helped to make ‘Socrates’ a household name, and by the time of Birds (414 bce) Socrates was well known enough for Aristophanes to coin the verb ‘socratize’.” p. 55

If that is right, the public figure is substantially a theatrical product rather than a theatrical record — and the details that look like biography are the least trustworthy part of it.

The comic tradition then fed the prose one, in both directions of the corpus:

just as Kolakes has provided material and inspiration for Plato’s Protagoras, Autolykos will have given Xenophon material for his symposium, including the presence of Socrates. Storey 2003: 324, quoted approvingly by Bromberg p. 44; cf. p. 45 on “the monopoly of influence that Clouds has held over the Socratic tradition since antiquity”

And his companion chapter states the chronological result that matters most here — a literary Socrates was taking shape before Plato chose to write one:

Documenting engagements with Socrates and Socratic ideas in pre-Platonic texts allows us to understand what some of the earliest Socratic receptions might have looked like, and how a literary tradition about Socrates began to take shape in the final decades of his life — and, importantly, in the years leading to Plato’s decision to represent him in literary (dramatic) form. Bromberg, “Greek Tragedy and the Socratic Tradition,” Brill’s Companion to the Reception of Socrates p. 42

He also states the problem of identification at the outset, which is worth keeping because it names the difficulty precisely: “there is the question of establishing what we mean by ‘Socrates’. The earliest mentions of his name are in Athenian Old Comedy, where he is identified as an unwashed, chattering charlatan… But this is not the Socrates familiar to readers of Plato and Xenophon” p. 41. And: “some of our earliest descriptions of the man (i.e., in Aristophanes’ Clouds and Plato’s Apology) not only disagree, but in several places explicitly contradict one another” p. 42.

Verdict on Gate 2

A shared literary inheritance stands between every Socratic author and the man. What the sources have in common may be what they inherited, not what they witnessed.

Gate 3 · Convergence between sources is intertextuality, not corroboration

This is the gate that kills the eclectic method — the practice, running from Schleiermacher through Vlastos, of triangulating the historical Socrates from points where the sources agree. Waterfield gives three reasons it fails; the third is the decisive one:

since the Socratics were writing fiction, when one of them echoes the other (and, as far as we can tell, Plato’s texts were generally primary), it is more appropriate to talk of intertextuality than of historical veracity. Waterfield p. 11

His test case is the Delphic oracle: he argues Plato invented the Chaerephon story and Xenophon knowingly reworked it, substituting “free, upright, and prudent” for “wise.” “This is a good demonstration of how intertextuality rather than biographical veracity was the name of the game for the writers of Sōkratikoi logoi” p. 13. And the aggregate:

there is no conclusive reason why we should privilege one of our authors over another, and there is no conclusive reason why we should think that any of them was writing anything close to what we understand as history or biography. Waterfield p. 11

Dorion reaches the same place from the other side — the sources do not converge in the first place. “It is quite surprising that there is no consensus regarding the number and identity of Plato’s dialogues that would allow for the reconstruction of the historical Socrates’ ideas, but, in another way, this disagreement among interpreters is inevitable because of the doctrinal heterogeneity of Socrates’ character in the corpus platonicum” pp. 6–7.

Verdict on Gate 3

Agreement among the Socratics is a literary fact about a genre, not a corroborating fact about a man.

Gate 4 · And it would not change the philosophy anyway

The three gates above are about whether historicity is recoverable. This one is about whether it would matter — and it is the gate that settles the question for this review.

The decisive sentence
Two portraits are not a likeness

“The critical weakness of Vlastos’s position will be evident to any clear-thinking reader from my summary: even if we accept Vlastos’s Ten Theses, we are left with no more than differences between two portraits of Socrates, one composed by early Plato and the other later; we have no reason for going on to say that the earlier portrait is a likeness of the historical Socrates.” Waterfield p. 6

Read that against what this review actually argues. The claim in §25 is that the Gorgias’s arguments fail and the Meno’s machinery repairs the failure. Grant Waterfield’s point in full and nothing in that claim moves. The defect is in the argument; the repair is in the later text; the relation between them is a relation between two pieces of writing. Whether the earlier position was ever held by a man in the Agora is a further question, and answering it in either direction leaves the relation exactly where it was.

Waterfield’s note on Fitzgerald’s canon makes the same point about the strongest philological instrument available: even where the grammatical criterion holds, “all this proves is that Aristotle thought he was referring to the historical Socrates, not that he actually was, as a result of access to privileged information” n. 23, p. 330.

Verdict on Gate 4

Historicity is an extra claim laid on top of the textual findings, not a premise they rest on. It can be removed without loss.

The reformulation

What to say instead
Index the stages to texts, not to biography

Do not say: the early dialogues give the historical Socrates’ philosophy, which Plato later outgrew.

Say: the early dialogues present a determinate position P₁, and its author is Plato in every case. The Meno marks a transition. The middle dialogues present P₂. No claim is made about who first held P₁, or whether anyone did.

In Dorion’s vocabulary, what the corpus gives is a “diffraction” — “the different and often conflicting interpretations that his disciples have given of his life and ideas” pp. 20–21. A diffraction pattern is still a determinate object of study. It is simply not a portrait.

consequences
Kahn’s creativity objection lapses
It was aimed at remaining fixed in a master’s position (§12). There is no master in the reformulation.
The Aristotle question becomes irrelevant to the philosophy
Whether Socrates lived, whether Aristotle read him independently, whether he is a construct — none of it changes what P₁ and P₂ are, or that they differ. Gate 4 is the warrant for this, and the Aristotle standoff above is the demonstration.
And the external evidence is no longer needed
This is the repair. Developmentalism looked fragile because it was historically formulated with Socrates inside it. Reformulated, it stands on textual evidence alone — which is where Part Three takes it.

The limit of the decoupling — and it is a real one

The reformulation frees you from needing Socrates as a philosophical figure. It does not free you from needing an order: P₁ must precede P₂. And within the early band, the order is not delivered by statistics.

Irwin is explicit. His Group 2 is Protagoras, Gorgias, Euthydemus; his Group 3 is Meno, Cratylus, Hippias Major, Phaedo, Symposium. And: “Linguistic and stylistic evidence … places Groups 1–3 before the others without fixing any definite order of groups or within groups. Doctrinal considerations (mentioned in points (3) and (4) above) justify us in placing Group 3 after Groups 1 and 2” Oxford Handbook p. 74 — where point (4) is Aristotle’s testimony.

So on Irwin’s own account, Aristotle is part of what puts the Meno after the Gorgias. That is exactly the pair §25 and §29 run on. See §32 for the over-determination that saves it.

The honest residue

Decoupling is not denial. Waterfield states the remainder exactly: “There must be traces of the historical Socrates in them, otherwise Socrates would not have been chosen as their protagonist, but we lack grounds for identifying such traces, and in any case any authentic, historical data that they might preserve about Socrates are ‘only incidental’” p. 11. And: “The Socratics’ works may be fictional, but they are fictional accounts of a person they knew and loved… The historical Socrates is at least the focus of his followers’ myth-making” p. 14.

Two things are also given up. The biographical narrative that made classic developmentalism attractive — the young Plato in his teacher’s shadow, then emerging — goes. And the question does come back on a different axis: ask what the name did in the culture rather than what the man thought, and the comic, tragic and biographical material becomes admissible evidence again. That is Bromberg’s programme, and it is a different enquiry, not a rival one.

The decoupling, stated

“Socrates” in Plato is a philosophical position bearing a name, not a witness being quoted. Index claims to texts; treat the identity of whoever first held them as a separate question with a separate evidence base.

Four gates, four failures: the sources are the wrong genre; the figure was circulating before them; their agreement is inheritance rather than testimony; and a successful historical result would leave the arguments untouched. What survives is a corpus of positions in a determinate relation — which is all the rest of this review needs.

The elenchus

Parts One and Two cleared ground. Part Three is where the question gets a test case, because here the two readings make different predictions about what we should find in the text.

The case: elenctic arguments establish inconsistency in a belief-set. In the Gorgias, Socrates describes what they have achieved using the vocabulary of demonstration and truth. That mismatch is what Vlastos named “the problem of the elenchus,” and it is the single most-worked question in Socratic studies of the last forty years.

Why this and not something else: a defect in an argument is a public, checkable fact. Unlike the absence of a doctrine, it cannot be explained away by dramatic propriety or an interlocutor’s capacity. If a later text supplies machinery shaped to that defect, that relation is between texts — not between a reader and an author’s mind.

17The gap: Style A machinery, Style B talk

Stated without any theory about what Socrates privately believed.

The elenchus is a procedure. You assert p. Socrates obtains your assent to q and r. He shows that q and r entail not-p. You retract.

What that establishes, in point of logic, is that {p, q, r} cannot all be true — an inconsistent set. It does not identify the false member. Perhaps p. Perhaps q.

Two vocabularies for describing a result
Style A — about you
“You have contradicted yourself.” “We agreed on these premises and they clash with your claim.” Nothing here says the claim is false; it says you cannot hold your beliefs together.
Style B — about the world
“It has been proved.” “This is true.” “What is true is never refuted.” Claims about the thesis, not about the man.
If the elenchus were purely ad hominem — a test of whether one man’s beliefs cohere — Socrates should speak only in Style A. That expectation is the contrast class. In the Gorgias he speaks in Style B.
how is it that Socrates claims to have proved a thesis false when, in point of logic, all he has proved is that the thesis is inconsistent with the conjunction of agreed-upon premises for which no reason has been given in that argument? Vlastos, Socratic Studies p. 25 — his own statement of the problem
Vlastos’s analogy
The courtroom, and why the witness is not compelled

“a witness gives testimony p on his own initiative and then, under prodding from the prosecuting attorney, concedes q and r, whereupon the attorney points out to him that q and r entail not-p … Has he then been compelled to testify that p is false? He has not. Confronted with the conflict in his testimony, it is still up to him to decide which of” his statements to withdraw. SS 25

The courtroom scene is Style A machinery. The next section is Style B talk, in the same dialogue.

18The Gorgias proof-language

Vlastos’s texts T19–T24, Socratic Studies ch. 1. This is the evidential base — assembled, and certified as localised, by the very scholar whose reconstruction is under suspicion.

T19

“Has it not been proved (apodedeiktai) that what was asserted [by myself] is true?”

Gorgias 479e — SS p. 19

The plainest one. The perfect passive of apodeiknŭmi, the standard word for demonstration. Vlastos: “Here Socrates says in so many words that he has done what Grote and I had maintained he never did in an elenctic argument: ‘proved’ his thesis ‘true.’”

T20

“But I, a single man, do not agree, for you do not compel me, but produce a multitude of false witnesses against me, trying to drive me out from my property, the truth.”

Gorgias 472b — SS p. 20

Seven Stephanus pages before T19. Vlastos: “How do you ‘compel’ your adversary to affirm what he denies? In an argument your only means of compulsion are logical.” So the intention to prove is announced in advance.

T21

“But I know how to produce one witness to my assertions: the man against whom I am arguing.”

Gorgias 474a5–6 — SS p. 20

The trap for the deflationist. This line is often cited as evidence that the elenchus is ad hominem by design. In context the claim is perfectly general: whenever he argues elenctically, Socrates “knows” how to make the opponent witness against himself — and maintains it is “impossible” for the opponent to do the same to him. It is a claim about the method, not a modest confession.

T22

“Not just difficult, Polus, but impossible: for what is true is never refuted.”

Gorgias 473b10–11 — SS p. 20

The strongest single item. A claim that refutation and truth track one another — about the world and about the method, with nothing about this interlocutor’s beliefs. Vlastos writes immediately after quoting it: “This brings us smack up against what I had called earlier on ‘the problem of the elenchus.’”

T23

“I believe that I and you and the rest of mankind believe that committing injustice is worse [for the agent] than is suffering it.”

Gorgias 474b — SS p. 20

The weakest, and the source of the trouble. Socrates attributes to Polus a belief Polus denies. This is where Vlastos derives covert vs overt belief, and hence assumption (A). It needs machinery to work — which is why it is the item to leave with Vlastos rather than build on. See §29.

T24

[a] “philosophy always says the same thing.”  [b] “if you leave this unrefuted, then, by the dog, god of Egypt, Callicles will not agree with you, Callicles, but will dissent from you your whole life long.”  [c] “I would rather … that a whole multitude should dissent from me and contradict me, than that I, a single man, should be out of tune with myself.”

Gorgias 482a–c — SS pp. 20, 27

Together with Grg. 486e–487e (Callicles as the ideal touchstone: knowledge, goodwill, frankness), this completes Vlastos’s cluster.

The distributional fact — the most valuable line in the chapter

Vlastos, Postscript p. 36, on the whole cluster: “where he is made to say those very strong things — T19, T20, T21, T22, T23, T24[a,b], and G. 486e–487e — of which there is no sign earlier on.”

This is a claim about the corpus, not about Socrates’ mind: the vocabulary switches on in the Gorgias and is absent before. Checkable, falsifiable, and exactly the shape of evidence a structural-fit argument needs. Vlastos assembled the sample and supplied the negative control, then explained it with a premise (§19) that nobody had to accept.

And the answer to “is this just Vlastos?”

SS p. 19: “Grote had certainly gone over that sentence many times, and so had I, and so had scores of others. But it had not hit anyone between the eyes.”

The text was there for two millennia. What was new in 1983 was noticing it. That distinction — datum versus reconstruction — is formalised in §21.

Where he matters: §17 (the gap) · §18 (T19–T24) · §19 (A) · §28 (the discarded patch)

The hypothesis that closes the gap, and the developmental story that follows from it.

(A) Anyone who holds a false belief also holds true beliefs entailing its negation. Therefore the premises Socrates extracts are not merely agreed but true, the inconsistency lands on p rather than on q, and “proved false” becomes legitimate.
So [A] is Plato’s present to his teacher, bestowed on him in the Gorgias, where he is made to say those very strong things … of which there is no sign earlier on. Vlastos, Socratic Studies p. 36

Note what that sentence concedes: (A) is not the historical Socrates’ premise, and not the early dialogues’ premise. It is an intrusion, at one point, by Plato — because Plato, not Socrates, is the one who feels the epistemological worry. Vlastos p. 36: “It must be Plato, then, who comes to feel the force of that question when he writes the Gorgias.”

The 1983b concession, and the chain it produces

Replying to Kraut in the same volume of Oxford Studies, Vlastos conceded that outside the Gorgias there is no evidence for (A) at all — that in the pre-Gorgias dialogues Socrates argues as any philosopher does:

he picks premises which he considers so eminently reasonable in themselves and so well-entrenched in his interlocutors’s system of belief, that when he faces them with the fact that these premises entail the negation of their thesis he feels no serious risk that they will renege on the premises to save their thesis — as in fact, they never do. Vlastos 1983b: 73, quoted at Wolfsdorf pp. 39–40
Before the Gorgias: no problem is felt
Socrates does not question, as an epistemologist would, the justification of his own method.
In the Gorgias: Plato feels the force of the question
“Plato’s own epistemological concerns lead him to question the justification of Socrates’ elenctic method. Plato’s answer, (A), is his ‘gift’ to Socrates.”
The gift fails
Plato “quickly comes to recognize ‘how hopeless it would be to justify [(A)] by the inductive evidence, which is all Socrates could have offered for it.’”
The elenchus is abandoned
“Plato loses faith in the elenchus and proceeds to extricate Socrates from it” — in Euthydemus, Lysis, Hippias Major. 1983b: 74
The Meno supplies a new justification
“Plato’s introduction of the theory of recollection in Meno provides a new means of justifying the elenchus. Thus, in Meno, a dialogue transitional between the early and middle periods, the elenchus returns.” Wolfsdorf pp. 39–40

What this establishes

The structural-fit argument — defect appears, machinery shaped to repair it follows — is not a novelty. It is Vlastos’s own, published in 1983 and still standing in Socratic Studies in 1994. That is a pedigree. It is also a warning: the argument has been available for forty years, and unitarianism grew anyway. §29 explains why, and what to change.

21Is the problem a reconstruction? Three layers

The distinction that dissolves the “it’s just Vlastos” objection. People collapse these three and then attack the wrong one.

The logic
An elenctic argument yields inconsistency in a set, not the falsity of a member.
Nobody disputes this
The words
Socrates nonetheless says “proved,” “true,” “impossible,” “what is true is never refuted.”
Nobody disputes these are on the page
Assumption (A)
The hidden premise that would license the words: everyone holds true beliefs entailing the negation of their false ones.
Heavily disputed

The result

L1 and L2 are observation. L3 is theory. “The problem of the elenchus is Vlastos’s reconstruction” points at L3 and pretends it is L1 and L2. The reconstruction is (A). The problem is the distance between L1 and L2 — public, checkable, and older than the reconstruction by two thousand years.

What the gap does not establish is that the mismatch is a defect. That is a fourth step, and it is where the interpretive fight properly lives — loose speech? dramatic bravado? Plato drawing an overconfident character? Part Four settles that step from an unexpected direction.

Everything to this point has been preparation for one chapter: Charles Kahn, Plato and the Socratic Dialogue (CUP 1996), ch. 5, “Gorgias: Plato’s manifesto for philosophy,” pp. 125–147 — specifically §3 “Elenchus in the Gorgias” (133), §4 “The positive function of the elenchus” (137), §5 “The limits of the Gorgias” (142).

Not dissolves. Poses.

§4 opens by conceding the negative result: “The results of all three refutations are essentially negative: to reveal the incoherence between the life and the doctrine of the interlocutor, an incoherence reflected in the inconsistency between different views held by the same man” p. 137. He then sets the strong claims beside it — Grg. 508e–509a, where the conclusions are “bound and fastened with iron and adamantine arguments” — and asks:

But how can such highly personalized refutations and, in the central case against Polus, such a complex and dubious argument, justify such strong and universal claims? Kahn p. 138

That is the problem of the elenchus in Kahn’s own words. And §5 goes further, conceding both halves of §21 and the fourth step:

We know that the argument against Polus is not strong enough to establish Socrates’ conclusion; and it is hard to believe that Plato was entirely unaware of this fact. Kahn p. 143

What has just been conceded

Kahn grants (i) the arguments do not establish what Socrates says they establish; (ii) Socrates nonetheless says it; and (iii) Plato knew. Point (iii) is the step that the “we cannot know what was in Plato’s mind” framing always struggles with — and it is granted by the opponent, not asserted by the proponent.

He has held this since 1983: n. 11 at p. 135 cites Kahn (1983) 90–2, “the argument is strong enough to defeat Polus but not strong enough to establish Socrates’ positive thesis that doing injustice is worse for the doer.”

24Kahn rejects (A) — and fills the same slot

His answer is not Vlastos’s. But it occupies the identical logical position, which is itself informative.

It is not that these claims follow from premises that are actually believed by his interlocutors (as Vlastos once suggested). It is rather that these claims are protreptic: this is what everyone will believe and prefer if they understand what is at stake. So at 495e 1 Callicles will change his mind “when he has come to see himself correctly.” Kahn p. 140 n. 15

Kahn’s own filler: shame, and the unconscious desire for the good

Shame is the recurring mechanism in all three refutations
Gorgias is refuted because he is ashamed to deny moral responsibility; Polus “because he was ashamed (aischuntheis) to say what he thinks” (482e 2); Callicles’ defeat is “precipitated by his sense of shame” pp. 135–136.
Shame is evidence of an innate moral sense
“Shame reflects a Platonic conception corresponding to our own notion of an innate moral sense, but which Plato describes as a universal desire for what is good” p. 138.
And that is what Socrates is relying on
“Shame operates in this dialogue as an obscure intuition of the good on the part of Socrates’ interlocutors. It must be on an intuition of this sort that Socrates is relying in his claim that Polus or Callicles will inevitably agree with him or be in disagreement with themselves” p. 138.
So the arguments are protreptic, not deductive
“Perhaps we should construe Plato’s use of such an argument as protreptic rather than deductive … the motive force is not provided by Socrates’ dialectical skill alone but by his ability to draw upon that deep desire for the good that motivates every rational agent, even when the agent himself is ignorant of the true nature and object of this desire” p. 140.
And the faith needs underwriting — Kahn says so
“As the dialogue represents him, it is Socrates’ confidence in such a desire that underlies his avowed faith in the elenchus, his conviction that, unless the ethical doctrine can be refuted, ‘Callicles himself will disagree with you, Callicles’” p. 142.
The structural observation
This is (A) with a different filler in the same slot

Vlastos: everyone holds true beliefs entailing the negation of their false ones. Kahn: everyone has a rational desire for the good, partly unconscious. Both are universal psychological premises, supplied by the interpreter, doing the job of underwriting Socrates’ confidence.

Kahn’s has one real advantage: it is visible in the text, in the shame passages, rather than reconstructed from T23. But the shape of the manoeuvre is identical — and the phrase “his avowed faith in the elenchus” is an admission that there is something in need of underwriting.

Rules out the “off-stage Forms” solution

A tempting move is to say: Socrates’ proof-language is licensed because Plato already has the Forms and is withholding them — the warrant is real but off-stage. Kahn denies this for the Gorgias, explicitly.

“To get from the technikos of the Gorgias to the philosopher-king we will need the doctrine of the Forms” p. 145. “In the Gorgias things are simpler, since our rational desire for the good is directed not towards some transcendent Form but towards the very adornment of the soul that makes a life admirable and beloved” p. 146. And p. 133: “if the Gorgias is earlier, Plato had not yet decided to develop the Socratic link between virtue and wisdom into an explicit definition or identity.”

Two independent reasons to drop the move even without Kahn: (i) it explains confidence in the theses but not the claims about the method (T21, T22 are claims about what elenctic argument can always do — private knowledge licenses “p is true,” not “my procedure is reliable”); and (ii) it cannot explain localisation — if the off-stage warrant was there all along, the confidence should be there all along, but Vlastos certifies “no sign earlier on.”

Read that last clause again

Defective arguments → the author recognises the defect → new machinery built to repair it → and the machinery is named: the Meno’s method of hypothesis.

That is the structural-fit argument, complete, with each link supplied by the leading unitarian. It is not “compatible with” the developmental reading — on the equipment axis, it is the developmental reading.

The two things, side by side

28The corrected reconstruction, in four steps

The natural way to state the whole argument is in three steps. The shape is right; two of the three steps are wrong in instructive ways, and fixing them produces a fourth step that is the only one doing real work.

The natural version

1. Stylometry groups the dialogues. 2. One group predominantly contains what we formalise as the elenchus, and with it the problem of the elenchus. 3. The other groups still contain elenchus, but they also contain the Theory of Forms, which prevents the problem from arising.

Step 1 — correct, with three caveats worth keeping visible

Stylometry is doctrine-blind, and that is its whole value here
It measures particles, clausulae, response formulae — never content. Which is precisely why using it to group the corpus is not circular. Keep that virtue explicit; it is the reason step 1 earns its place at all.
It gives grouping and relative order, not direction
Direction comes from outside: the Laws unfinished at Plato’s death. So even here you are not operating on internal evidence alone (§01).
Resolution is uneven
Strong at isolating the late group, weak inside the early group (§05). “The early dialogues” is a cluster, not a sequence.

Step 2 — “predominantly” is carrying too much

The exceptions are load-bearing rather than marginal:

CaseWhy it breaks the clean grouping
Republic IElenctic throughout — and it is Book I of the paradigm middle dialogue.
MenoIts first third is elenctic. Vlastos says so himself (Vlastos 1988: 144, at Prior p. 82).
TheaetetusStylometrically late, thoroughly elenctic, and it ends in aporia.

And the sharper problem: the strong form of the difficulty is concentrated in one dialogue, not a group. Vlastos conceded in 1983b that outside the Gorgias there is no evidence for (A) at all (§19).

Step 2, restated
Distribution and concentration, not membership

Arguments of the standard elenctic form are distributed across the corpus in a describable way; and the language of proof that makes the form problematic is concentrated in the Gorgias, at the early cluster’s edge.

Weaker as a slogan, stronger as a claim — because it is checkable, and because it is what the census (§31) would actually establish.

Step 3 — the Forms are the wrong machinery

Forms say nothing about the problem. The problem is: why drop p rather than q? Three pieces of middle-period apparatus bear on that, and Forms are the last of the three, not the first.

M. 81d–e

“We must, therefore, not believe that debater’s argument, for it would make us idle, and fainthearted men like to hear it, whereas my argument makes them energetic and keen on the search.”

Meno 81d–e — recollection introduced explicitly to license inquiry

Recollection grounds the claim that the interlocutor already holds latent true beliefs — that is (A) with an epistemology underneath it. It is the answer to why is q true and not merely agreed?

Phd. 101d–e

“when you must give an account of your hypothesis itself you will proceed in the same way: you will assume another hypothesis, the one which seems to you best of the higher ones until you come to something acceptable.”

Phaedo 101d–e — the upward hypothetical method

This answers Vlastos’s actual complaint, which was that the premises are “logically unsecured within the argument: no reason has been given to compel agreement to them” (1983a: 40, at Wolfsdorf p. 37). A procedure for securing q is exactly the missing step — and here it is, named.

Rep. VI 510b–511b

One path “investigate[s] from hypotheses, proceeding not to a first principle but to a conclusion”; the other “makes its way to a first principle that is not a hypothesis… using forms themselves.”

Republic VI 510b–511b — Forms as terminus

Forms stop the regress the hypothetical method starts. They are downstream of the repair, not the repair itself.

The corrected mechanism

problem → recollection (grounds the latent true beliefs) + upward hypothesis (secures the premises) → Forms (terminate the upward path). Saying “the Forms prevent the problem” skips the two steps that actually do the work.

Step 3(ii) — the elenchus does not stop where the Forms start; it comes back

On Vlastos’s own account (§19), Plato “loses faith in the elenchus and proceeds to extricate Socrates from it” in the Euthydemus, Lysis and Hippias Major — dialogues containing no theory of Forms. Then recollection arrives and “in Meno… the elenchus returns.”

Note the sequence
Abandonment precedes the Forms. Reinstatement comes with recollection.

So the natural step 3 has the mechanism backwards — and the correct version is better for the argument, for the reason set out below.

What the argument actually is — and where it stops working

Say this part out loud; it is currently implicit
An argument from the convergence of two independent measures

Stylometry is blind to doctrine. The doctrinal grouping is blind to style. That they line up is the entire force of the argument. Framed that way, step 1 earns its place. Framed as “stylometry proves the order, therefore development,” it collapses on contact with §07.

But convergence gives correlation, not the direction of explanation

Kahn accepts every step and reads the same convergence the other way: the preparatory dialogues are stylistically earlier because they were written earlier, as preparation. Prolepsis predicts exactly the same alignment. Stylometry measures when, never why, so it cannot break the tie.

And this is not a reconstruction of what Kahn might say. It is what he says. His evidence for the ingressive reading is “of two kinds” p. 60: first, “passages in the threshold dialogues that are enigmatic, puzzling, or somehow problematic, for which the solution or clarification will be provided by a text or a doctrine in the middle dialogues”; second, middle dialogues that “deliberately emphasize their continuity with ideas and formulations familiar from the earlier works.”

The first kind is the structural-fit pattern — earlier problem, later solution — claimed as evidence for design. The same data, on the page, serving both hypotheses. Kahn even anticipates the objection: “This proposal may be regarded as begging the question against the developmental reading… But since in any case we do not have access to Plato’s mind, the issue is: which assumptions provide us with the best interpretation of the texts?” p. 59

The discriminator

The only element in the reconstruction that design does not predict is the discarded patch — a method dropped, and then reinstated once the missing ground arrives.

An author executing a plan has no reason to write three dialogues that abandon his central method and then restore it. A philosopher who found a repair does. This is why step 3(ii) matters far more than it looks, and it is the second mark of stumbling alongside the imperfect fit of the Meno’s repair (§29).

The corrected version, in four steps

Stylometry, blind to doctrine, clusters the corpus
And, with the Laws anchor, orders the clusters. Grouping, not sequence, inside the early band.
The problematic language concentrates in one dialogue
Arguments of the standard elenctic form are distributed across the corpus; the language of proof that makes the form problematic concentrates in the Gorgias, at that cluster’s edge — “of which there is no sign earlier on” (§18).
What follows supplies precisely what the form lacked
A ground for the interlocutor’s latent true beliefs (recollection, M. 81d–e) and a procedure for securing the premises (upward hypothesis, Phd. 101d–e), terminating in Forms (Rep. 511b).
And in between, the method is dropped and then reinstated
Abandoned in the Euthydemus, Lysis and Hippias Major — before the Forms arrive — and restored in the Meno with recollection. Design does not predict this. Discovery does.

Where step 4 is itself vulnerable

It leans on Vlastos’s reading of those three dialogues as an abandonment rather than as three works with different aims. A unitarian can deny the description without denying any text. So step 4 is the strongest element and the one most in need of the census (§31) — specifically, of a form-by-form count showing that elenctic argument really does thin out in the Euthydemus, Lysis and Hippias Major and thicken again in the Meno. That is a countable claim, and nobody has counted.

29The decoupled thesis

Why the argument has to be stated without (A) — and why that is the contribution rather than a concession.

The question the pedigree raises

§19 showed the structural-fit argument is Vlastos’s, from 1983, still standing in 1994. So why did it not settle the matter? Because every link in his chain runs through (A): Plato feels the worry → gives (A) → sees (A) is unjustifiable → abandons the elenchus → the Meno restores it. Brickhouse & Smith 1984a attack (A) directly; Kahn rejects it by name at n. 15. Kill (A) and the whole chain goes with it.

The restatement
Not “the elenchus has a problem” but this

Socrates’ language of proof in the Gorgias outruns what his arguments establish; the Gorgias is the dialogue where that gap becomes visible; and the machinery introduced downstream is shaped to it.

This needs no premise about what Socrates secretly holds. It reports L1 and L2 of §21 — the logic of the argument form, and the words on the page. (A) is Vlastos’s explanation of the gap. The argument needs the gap, not the explanation.

The offensive version
Marks of stumbling rather than design

A designed reveal and a genuine repair both produce problem-then-solution. To discriminate, look for a repair that does not fit tightly — because design produces tight fits and discovery does not.

And there is one. If the Meno were the planned payoff, it should land square on the difficulty. It does not quite. Recollection answers a paradox about inquiry (80d) — how can you look for what you do not know — generated by the priority of definition at 71b, not by the elenchus’s probative weakness. And the methodological deliverable at 86d–e is the method of hypothesis, introduced as a concession: if you must, let us proceed from a hypothesis. Twenty years of withholding, and the payoff is a grudging second-best procedure.

And there is a second mark, which is stronger. The elenchus is not merely repaired — it is abandoned and then reinstated: dropped in the Euthydemus, Lysis and Hippias Major, before any Forms arrive, and restored in the Meno once recollection supplies the missing ground (§28). An author executing a plan has no reason to discard his central method mid-project and then take it back. A philosopher who found a repair does.

State the case that way — the repair is imperfect, and the method was discarded before it was recovered, in ways design would not produce — rather than defensively, as “we cannot know what was in Plato’s mind.” The defensive version concedes the frame.

One caution on framing

Do not write “even Vlastos said this in 1983.” That borrows authority from the version that lost. The framing is: the developmental structural-fit reading is old; running it without (A) is what is new — and Kahn, who rejected (A), supplies the equipment premise independently.

The debt, discharged
How much chronology this actually costs

§01 set the price: a developmental claim needs a temporal premise, however minimal, and the whole question is how much and from where. The restatement above pays it exactly once. It needs one ordered pair — a text in which a procedure is used and defended without adequate ground, and a text in which the ground arrives — and it takes the order from the content of the two arguments rather than from a word-count.

Nothing else is bought. No sequence of periods, no early/middle boundary, no hinge dialogue, no fifty-year narrative, no claim about which decade anything was written in. Which is exactly why the collapse documented in Part One leaves this standing: what collapsed was an instrument for ordering the whole corpus, and this claim never ordered the corpus. It orders two arguments, on grounds internal to them.

And the price is symmetrical. Prolepsis needs the same pair in the same order — Kahn places the Gorgias before the seven threshold dialogues and the Meno among them, on ingressive and literary grounds p. 148, and dates them independently at p. 59 (§§01, 03). Neither side is buying its chronology from stylometry any more. What remains in dispute is what the ordered pair is evidence of — which is where the disagreement belonged from the start.

30The position adopted here — and the scholars who already hold it

Marked as mine, not a reviewed author’s. Everything before this section reports; this section commits.

A review that only reports leaves its reader without the one thing the reporting was for. So: the seven volumes have been described; here is the position I hold, stated so that it can be attacked separately from the reporting, and so that a reader who rejects it can still use the rest.

The position
Logical-first developmentalism

Plato’s thought develops, and the development is in the first instance an order of dependence among arguments rather than a sequence of dates. A composition order is neither the evidence for the development nor a precondition of stating it. Where an ordering is needed, it is needed for one pair of texts at a time, and it comes from the content of the two arguments — a defect, and a repair too closely fitted to it to be independent — not from a word-count.

Chronology is not denied. It is demoted: from premise to optional corroboration. If a secure stylometry of the early group were somehow recovered tomorrow, this position would gain nothing it needs. That it has been lost (§§05–11) costs nothing it uses.

Why this is not a compromise between the two camps

It looks like a hedge and is not one. It concedes more to the unitarians than developmentalists usually do, keeps less than developmentalists usually want, and refuses something both sides have taken for granted.

CONCEDED TO THE UNITARIANS Order of composition is not order of doctrine; textual absence has many available explanations; and the inference from a date to a state of mind is not licensed by anything the evidence supplies. This is the Ebrey & Kraut position of §03, accepted in full — not grudgingly, but because it is correct.
KEPT FROM THE DEVELOPMENTALISTS That Plato’s equipment changed, that the change has a direction, and that the direction is recoverable. The Gorgias’s arguments fail to establish what Socrates says they establish; the Meno’s machinery supplies what they lack. That is a fact about two arguments, and it is orderable without a date.
REFUSED TO BOTH The assumption that the dispute has to be settled corpus-wide. Both camps argue as though the question were what kind of thing the whole corpus is. It is not. It is a question about particular pairs of arguments, and it will have different answers for different pairs. On the elenchus the answer is discovery; on the Forms (§§03, 32.1) there is as yet no answer at all.

Dancy holds this already, and has since 2004

This is the part I did not expect to find. R. M. Dancy’s Plato’s Introduction of Forms is a three-hundred-page developmental argument — the Theory of Forms grown out of the Socratic demand for definitions — and it opens by declining the chronology outright:

This book is concerned with a development in Plato’s thought. But it does not depend on a particular chronological scheme. The development in question is in the first instance a logical one. The most natural way of thinking of this development is as a chronological one as well, and I know of nothing against this, but if Plato’s biography turns out to be more complex, so be it. R. M. Dancy, Plato’s Introduction of Forms (Cambridge, 2004) p. 4

A page later he throws out the chronological labels along with the scheme, and replaces them with argumentative ones:

The dialogues I am going to consider fall into two groups: they are those frequently taken to be the “early” and “middle” dialogues, but, since I am emphasizing argument rather than date, I have preferred the labels “Socratic” and “doctrinal.” Dancy p. 5

The substitution is not cosmetic. His “Socratic” group is defined by four features — Socrates is the main speaker; the task is defining something for a practical purpose; the task fails; and Socrates professes no doctrine beyond the Socratic paradoxes pp. 5–6. Membership is decided by reading the dialogue, not by dating it. And he adds, closing the door on the very move Part One shows cannot be made: “I am not going to be trying to discern development, whether logical or chronological, within the group of Socratic dialogues” p. 5.

THE TEST Read his conclusion and count the dates. There are none. “That theory has now turned into the Theory of Forms. Substitutivity is obvious enough … The Paradigm Requirement is now the claim that the separate Form, The Pious, is through and through pious … And the Explanatory Requirement has become a nearly full-blown theory of causality” p. 314. Every step is a requirement generating a doctrine. Three hundred pages of development, summarised without a single chronological premise.

So the position is not new, and it should not be advertised as new. What is new is generalising it. Dancy ran it once, on one axis, for reasons of his own; Brickhouse and Smith ran it on another; Kahn conceded it about his own thesis. Nobody drew the conclusion that this is how the whole dispute has to be conducted.

Where I depart from him — two points, and the first is the useful one

1 · He keeps a prop he does not need
Aristotle is still in the frame

Dancy’s ch. 1.2 is titled “The genesis of the Theory of Forms: Aristotle’s account,” and what he takes from Metaphysics A 6, M 4 and M 9 includes the historical claim that Plato “made them ‘separate’ … where Socrates had not” p. 12. That is external testimony about a real sequence — and it is exactly the prop Part Two removes (§§13–16): Aristotle never names a dialogue as a record of Socrates, and on Irwin’s own account adjudicates only between two options that are both developmental.

This is not an objection to Dancy; it is an improvement of him. Strip Aristotle out and his argument is stronger, because it stops depending on the one witness the last thirty years of source criticism has dismantled. He took the right step — logical order first — and then kept a crutch his own method had made unnecessary.

2 · He declines the chronology but names no replacement
Refusing a premise is not the same as supplying one

Dancy never asks what would happen if the chronology were forced to zero, and never identifies a feature that the design reading and the discovery reading would predict differently. His logical order is a reconstruction of what the arguments require; it does not by itself show that Plato worked them out in that order rather than expounding them in it. That gap is what §01 exists to close, and the discriminator it proposes — a method abandoned and then reinstated, which design does not predict and discovery does — is the thing Dancy’s position lacks.

And Brickhouse & Smith arrive at the same place from the other side

Dancy is not alone, and the second precedent is more useful than the first, because it comes from scholars this review otherwise treats as opponents. Thomas Brickhouse and Nicholas D. Smith are the source of the heterogeneity objection at §20 and the standing threat at §32.2 — the claim that there is no such thing as the Socratic elenchus. On the substance they are adversaries. On the method they are allies, and they say so at length.

Their developmentalism is deflated to almost exactly the size proposed here:

This book provides further evidence for developmentalism — which, for the sake of convenience, we have characterized as making a distinction between Socrates and Plato, although, as we said earlier, we are not actually committed to the view that the ‘Socrates’ of whom we speak is the historical philosopher by that name. T. Brickhouse and N. D. Smith, Socratic Moral Psychology (Cambridge, 2010) p. 193

No biography, no dates — a distinction between two bodies of argument. And their §1.3.1, titled flatly “contra chronology,” argues that content analysis alone does the work. Their demonstration is the interesting part: they take the three loudest opponents of chronological ordering and show that each of them, having demolished the dating, quietly rebuilds the same grouping on other grounds.

I once believed that my arrangement of the dialogues in Group I was a chronological sequence, but I now think it is a mistake to make any claims about a matter on which we have so little evidence. Even if they are taken as chronological, however, my six stages do not pretend to represent the development of Plato’s thought. They represent different moments in his literary presentation of Socrates and different approaches to the philosophical position of the Republic. Kahn p. 48
Since the dialogues of Group I are earlier, I have described their relation to the Republic as proleptic. But this term “proleptic” may seem too chronological. It does not greatly matter in what order these dialogues were actually composed or in what sequence they are in fact read. My six stages may be thought of as the proposal for an ideal reading order. Perhaps the better metaphor will be spatial rather than temporal: instead of before and after we can speak of exoteric and esoteric, of relative distance from the center as defined by the Republic. As a variant on the notion of prolepsis, this mode of interpretation might equally well be called ingressive. Kahn p. 48

And he keeps the grouping while dropping the dating, in as many words: the division into three groups is “the kind of solid philological result, intersubjectively confirmable” p. 47, whereas “the order of dialogues within each group is a matter of personal conjecture” p. 47.

What this does to §01
Kahn saw the stress test coming — and conceded its result

§01 predicted that under zero chronology prolepsis “becomes mere order of exposition within one work — which is a much weaker and less interesting thesis.” That prediction was made against Kahn. It turns out he had already made the retreat himself, thirty years earlier: an ideal reading order, spatial rather than temporal, ingressive rather than proleptic.

So the stress test does not defeat Kahn. It describes a move he had already made. Which is the strongest available confirmation that the move is forced: the leading unitarian de-temporalised his own central thesis before anyone asked him to.

Brickhouse and Smith draw the general conclusion twice, and it is the conclusion adopted here:

If Cooper’s position is consistent — and we think it is — then it follows that one can jettison the entire apparatus and the methodologies of chronological ordering and nonetheless advocate some version of both the first and the second foundational principles of Socratic studies. Brickhouse and Smith p. 21
Kahn’s project shows that content analysis — even if it has been extolled as an instrument of chronology — requires no commitment to chronology in order to sustain the field of Socratic studies. Brickhouse and Smith p. 22
Three anti-chronologists, one pattern. Each dismantles the dating and then rebuilds the same grouping on content. Cooper calls it “thematic affinities”; Nails calls it agora and Academy; Kahn calls it ingressive distance from the Republic. Nobody presents this as a discovery. It is simply what the field does when the instrument fails — which is the observation Part One was working towards, arrived at here from four directions at once.

What it costs, and what would refute it

The cost. It gives up the narrative. There is no philosopher’s growth to tell, no hinge dialogue, no early-middle-late arc — the things that made developmentalism worth holding for a century. What is left is smaller and duller: one ordered pair, defensible.

What would refute it. Three things, each checkable. (i) A demonstration that the Meno’s machinery is not shaped to the Gorgias’s failure but to a neighbouring problem — the sceptical route at §32.4. (ii) A census showing that Socratic argument-forms scatter rather than cluster, dissolving the explanandum — §31. (iii) Evidence that the Meno precedes the Gorgias, which Irwin lists as a live dispute p. 74 and which would invert the pair. Any of the three sinks it. None of them is about stylometry, which is the point.

The claim, in one sentence. Development is real, it is an order of dependence before it is an order of dates, it costs exactly one ordered pair per claim, and the texts pay for it — which is why the collapse of the chronology documented in Part One is not the end of developmentalism but the end of the only version that could not survive without it.

Two things to verify before this is published

1 · How load-bearing is Aristotle for Dancy? Ch. 1.2 uses him for the genesis story; whether the book’s argument would stand without him is not settled by the passages read here. Dancy’s n. 42 p. 11 says his treatment is “in many respects similar to that in Fine (1993) 44–65,” diverging on whether Plato’s Socrates has a Theory of Forms — so Fine 1993 is the place to test it.

2 · Nails 1995. The agora/Academy grouping is taken from Brickhouse and Smith’s report at p. 21, citing Nails 1995: 203. Agora, Academy, and the Conduct of Philosophy is not held locally; the claim that her agora group coincides with the Socratic list has not been checked against her own text.

3 · Fronterotta’s ordering. Prior reports that Fronterotta, an avowed anti-developmentalist, nonetheless places Laches and Charmides before Hippias Major and Euthyphro — “that is where the theory of Ideas is introduced” (Prior, Bloomsbury Companion p. 90). If that ordering is fixed by his hierarchy of levels of demand, it is independent confirmation of the position above, from a hostile witness. If he took it from the standard chronology, it confirms nothing. Fronterotta 2007 is not held locally and has not been checked.

The programme — the position I intend to work from next

Stated as an intention, not a result
Dancy’s method, generalised; Dancy’s thesis, taken up where he left it

Everything above is what this review argues. This last part is different in kind: it is a declaration of where I intend to go, and it should be read as a research programme rather than as a conclusion the seven volumes support. R. M. Dancy’s Plato’s Introduction of Forms is the position I hold and hope to adopt as the frame of my own future work — both the way it argues and the thing it argues for.

§30 has already used Dancy as a precedent: he is the proof that a full-length developmental argument can be run without a chronology. What follows says what I want to take from him beyond that, and why.

Four sentences that constitute the charter

One — the order is logical before it is temporal. This is the sentence the whole programme turns on, and it is the first thing he says about method:

This book is concerned with a development in Plato’s thought. But it does not depend on a particular chronological scheme. The development in question is in the first instance a logical one. The most natural way of thinking of this development is as a chronological one as well, and I know of nothing against this, but if Plato’s biography turns out to be more complex, so be it. R. M. Dancy, Plato’s Introduction of Forms (Cambridge, 2004) p. 4

Two — the groupings are argumentative, so they are readable off the texts. Where the field names its groups by date, he names them by what Socrates is doing in them:

… since I am emphasizing argument rather than date, I have preferred the labels “Socratic” and “doctrinal.” Both labels could be misleading: the first because it suggests that the historical Socrates is in view and the second because it suggests that things are written in stone. Neither implication is intended here. Dancy p. 5

Three — the substantive thesis, in his own compressed form. This is what the programme is about, and it is a claim about two states of one philosophical practice, not about two decades:

Socrates in certain dialogues produces arguments to defeat proposed definitions without committing himself to the idea that the things being defined are to be found in an eternal, unchanging, and ontologically pure realm. In other dialogues definition takes more of a back seat, and Socrates does commit himself to that metaphysical view. The metaphysical view is the Theory of Forms. Dancy p. 4

Four — the discipline about what one may not order. He refuses, in advance, exactly the ordering Part One shows cannot be had — and he loses nothing by refusing it:

I am not going to be trying to discern development, whether logical or chronological, within the group of Socratic dialogues. Dancy p. 5
WHY THIS IS THE RIGHT FRAME TO INHERIT

Brandwood’s verdict and Thesleff’s dissent (§§05, 10) between them make the early group unorderable and possibly not a group. A method that must order it is finished. Dancy’s method never asks. His Socratic group is a set defined by four readable features — Socrates leads, a definition is sought for a practical purpose, the search fails, no doctrine is professed beyond the Socratic paradoxes pp. 5–6 — and his argument runs between that set and the doctrinal one, not inside either. The demolition of Part One does not touch it. That is not a lucky escape; it is what choosing a logical order buys.

He also says, plainly, the thing the unitarians treat as a scandal

Sometimes it is made to sound as if Plato would be inferior as a philosopher if he ever altered his views about anything. I think, on the contrary, that changing one’s mind often is something any self- and other-respecting philosopher can be expected to do: the questions are far too difficult. Dancy p. 4

Set beside §03: no unitarian actually denies this, and Dancy is not attacking a position anyone holds. But it states the working attitude the programme adopts — that development is the normal condition of a philosopher, and that the burden falls on whoever claims a fifty-year fixed point, not on whoever claims a change.

What I mean to add to it

Repairs — §30’s two departures, as work
1
Remove Aristotle from the genesis story
Ch. 1.2 takes from Metaphysics A 6, M 4 and M 9 the historical claim that Plato “set the ‘universal’ … ‘apart from’ the things we perceive … he made them ‘separate’ where Socrates had not” p. 12. Part Two (§§13–16) is the case that this witness cannot carry that weight. The task is to show that the book’s argument survives the removal — and, if it does, that it is stronger for it, because separation would then be established from the Phaedo’s own text rather than from a report about Plato.
2
Supply the discriminator he never names
Dancy shows what the arguments require; he does not show that Plato worked them out in that order rather than expounding them in it. The design reading fits his data as well as the discovery reading does. §01’s proposal — a method abandoned and then reinstated, which design does not predict — is offered as the missing test, and it needs to be run on Dancy’s material and not only on Vlastos’s.
Extensions — where it should go next
3
The Parmenides, which he explicitly leaves open
The book ends on an invitation: “When Plato does come to focus on that relationship, the Theory of Forms gets into deep trouble. This happens in the Parmenides. Anyway, so I think. But that is another story” p. 315. That story is the obvious next application: a defect exposed, and later machinery shaped to it — the same structural-fit form this review runs on the elenchus, applied to participation.
4
Separation as the threshold, tested against Prior
Dancy’s denial that the Euthyphro passages give a theory of Forms rests on one condition — separation — being necessary. Prior, Allen and Fine read the same lines and say the remaining conjuncts suffice. That dispute is a definitional threshold, not a disagreement about the Greek, and it is worth settling on its own terms rather than by counting authorities (§32.1).

What adopting this frame costs, and the honest risk

It gives up corpus-wide verdicts. A logical order is established for one pair of arguments at a time. There is no move from “the Meno’s machinery repairs the Gorgias’s defect” to “Plato is a developmentalist’s Plato,” and no move from the Euthyphro–Phaedo case to any thesis about the corpus. That is a real loss of reach, and it should be stated rather than concealed.

The risk that has to be watched. A logical order can be a reconstruction of the subject matter rather than a history of anyone’s thinking — requirements generate doctrines whether or not a mind ever traversed them in that direction. Dancy concedes the possibility in the mildest possible way (“if Plato’s biography turns out to be more complex, so be it”) and moves on. It cannot be left there. Either the discriminator at item 2 works, or the programme delivers a rational reconstruction and must say so.

The intention, in one sentence. Take Dancy’s logical-order method, strip the Aristotelian prop out of it, give it the discriminator it lacks, and run it forward from the point where he stopped — the Parmenides, and the trouble participation makes for the theory the Phaedo introduced.

Where they matter: §20 (heterogeneity) · §29 (the objection table) · §31 (job 1)

One outstanding objection, one piece of work that answers it, and a second job the same work does.

Job 1 · against heterogeneity
Is there a unit to have a problem?

Brickhouse & Smith 2002b: there is no such thing as “the Socratic elenchus.” A form-by-form classification of Socratic arguments, using Wolfsdorf’s six categories — reductio, analogy, epagogic, dialectical expedients, deliberate fallacy, and variants — either shows the arguments cluster, in which case there is a unit and the objection fails, or shows they scatter, in which case the objection succeeds and the explanandum dissolves.

Wolfsdorf issues the invitation himself: “a good deal of middle ground lies open for investigation of details and specifics” p. 66. This is not a side-quest; it is the decision procedure for the standing objection.

Job 2 · against (A)
A countable universal, from the person under suspicion

Vlastos 1983b: 73 — interlocutors never “renege on the premises to save their thesis — as in fact, they never do.” That is a universal quantifier over the corpus, and the inductive base on which (A) rests. Fine’s footnote already names two counter-instances: Laches 197a–c and the Crito. If a census finds more, the empirical basis for (A) goes.

Job 3 · for the discriminator
Does the elenchus actually thin out and thicken again?

Step 4 of §28 is the only element design does not predict — and it rests on Vlastos’s description of the Euthydemus, Lysis and Hippias Major as an abandonment. A form-by-form count would convert that description into a measurement: elenctic arguments per dialogue, across the early band and into the Meno. It is a countable claim, and nobody has counted.

Why the census wins either way for the decoupled version

If (A) falls, Vlastos’s chain falls — but the decoupled thesis does not use (A), and it gains, because the leading rival explanation of the same gap is removed. If (A) stands, the gap is explained but still present, and the downstream machinery is still shaped to it.

Win–win for the decoupled version; lose–lose for Vlastos’s. That asymmetry is the reason to run the census rather than argue about it.

32What is not settled

Recorded so the review does not overclaim.

Open

1 · The doctrinal axis. Nothing here decides whether Plato had the Forms while writing the Laches. The equipment result does not transfer: an author can build better arguments for a position he has held throughout. Kahn is living proof that the two are separable.

2 · Heterogeneity. Brickhouse & Smith 2002b stands until the census is run. Everything in Part Three and Part Four presupposes there is a determinate argumentative practice with a determinate defect.

3 · The fourth step. §21 establishes a mismatch, not a defect. Kahn supplies the defect verdict for the Gorgias specifically (“not strong enough,” “unsatisfactory”) — but that is one dialogue, and the general claim about elenctic argument as such is not thereby established.

5 · The Gorgias–Meno order itself. Irwin, having defended Aristotle’s testimony as hard as anyone does, still concedes: the cumulative argument “does not settle, for instance, the relation of the Gorgias to the Meno” p. 77. He also lists as a live dispute that “Some place one or both of the Protagoras and Gorgias after the Meno” p. 74. Since the structural-fit argument is a claim about that pair — and since §29 shows the argument buys nothing but that pair — the entire debt set in §01 rests here. This is the sharpest outstanding gap, and the review does not pretend otherwise. And there is a circularity risk to name: if doctrinal difference fixes the order, the order cannot then be cited as independent evidence of doctrinal development.

4 · Whether the Meno’s repair is aimed at the elenchus at all. §29 uses the mis-fit as evidence for discovery. But a sceptic can run the same fact the other way: if recollection answers the inquiry paradox and not the elenchus’s probative weakness, perhaps it is not a repair of the elenchus at all, and the sequence is a coincidence of neighbouring problems. This needs argument, not assertion.

Where two proposals land

Decoupling (§16): holds. It dissolves Kahn’s creativity objection, makes the Aristotle question irrelevant to the philosophy, and repairs rather than weakens the developmental case.

The order (§32.5): partly repaired by over-determination. Kahn is the scholar Irwin names as discounting Aristotle’s testimony — and Kahn still places the Gorgias before the seven threshold dialogues, the Meno among them, on ingressive and literary grounds p. 148. The two scholars who most disagree about Aristotle agree about this order, each resting it on a different prop. So the sequence survives the loss of either one. What it does not have is a prop that is independent of doctrinal judgement altogether.

Off-stage Forms (§24): does not hold. It is a solution to the problem, not a dissolution of it — so it concedes the gap; it is structurally (A) with Plato rather than Socrates as bearer, and inherits (A)’s liability of being unattested; it explains confidence in the theses but not the claims about the method; it cannot explain localisation; and Kahn contradicts it for the Gorgias at pp. 145–146. Its one virtue is that it is the strongest unitarian counter available — which is worth knowing.
