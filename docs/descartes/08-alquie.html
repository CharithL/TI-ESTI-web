The Missing Half of the Quarrel

What of Ferdinand Alquié’s Descartes reached English, and what the Royaumont exchange with Gueroult actually said

Abstract

Chat 7 ended on a gap. Its strongest evidence that “method makes the object” was the Gueroult–Alquié exchange at Royaumont, and it could report only one side from English sources. This chat went after the other side. The brief’s motivating fact: Gueroult’s Descartes selon l’ordre des raisons was translated by Roger Ariew in 1984–85, and Alquié’s La découverte métaphysique de l’homme chez Descartes (1950) never was. “One side of the most important methodological quarrel in Descartes studies is in English; the other is not.”

The chat’s answer. Nothing of Alquié on Descartes exists in English. The quarrel reaches English readers through Knox Peden (2011; 2014), and earlier, it guessed, through J. F. Bannan (1960) and L. J. Beck (1965). Alquié is cited “overwhelmingly as an editor,” for the Garnier Œuvres, and not as an interpreter. His position survives, uncredited, in Michel Henry, Jean-Luc Marion and Paul Ricoeur. Nobody in English has answered Gueroult’s charge that Alquié’s surplus of being over thought is an “occult quality.” And Elliot Samuel Paul’s anti-transparency thesis (2018) rediscovers Alquié without citing him.

What this review adds. The three sources the chat could not open are all in your folder, and none says what the chat expected. Bannan is mainly about Roger Lefèvre; the quarrel gets one sentence (p. 436). Beck mentions Royaumont once, for Gouhier’s paper (p. 231), and not at “pp. 28–38.” He does record, though, that he owes “a debt … which no words can total and express” to Alquié, Gouhier and Gueroult (p. vii). Ariew’s introduction names Alquié as “Gueroult’s great ‘psychologist’ opponent” and quotes him praising Gueroult (p. xv). So an English reader first meets the quarrel as a truce.

Four of the chat’s eight answers need revising.

  • Something of Alquié is in English, in sentences rather than books: Peden translates his Royaumont thesis (p. 77), and his spoken objections to Deleuze are printed in Desert Islands (pp. 104–106).
  • He is cited as an interpreter in the Oxford Handbook (Ragland, pp. 180, 182) and by Marion.
  • The Alquié–Henry lineage is stated in English, by Marion himself (On the Ego and on God, p. 197 n. 21), and by Peden (pp. 67, 90).
  • The Paul parallel is weaker than claimed: Alquié holds that “no reflection, no doubt” can prevail against the cogito’s self-presence (p. 189). Paul holds that radical doubt is needed even to know a thought exists.

Finally, a reply to the “occult quality” charge is available in the texts. Alquié grounds his surplus in the same two passages Dicker used in Chat 6.

The question the chat was built around

“What of Ferdinand Alquié’s reading of Descartes has reached English — in translation, in commentary, in citation, or in philosophical descent — and what has been said in English about his quarrel with Martial Gueroult?”

Two test cases from the brief. Anti-transparency: Alquié denies in 1950 that the cogito is “une pensée transparente à soi.” Does the current transparency literature know it? The primacy of sum: “l’évidence du sum est première, et dépasse l’idée de pensée.” Does anyone in English hold it? The Anglophone critics argue that the cogito yields less than a self. Alquié argues that it yields more than the concept can contain.

The standard the brief set: “Distinguish citing Alquié from engaging him. A footnote to the Garnier edition is not engagement.” And: “Report negative findings explicitly.”

The thing that makes this chat worth reading twice

It turns a methodological quarrel into a question about the archive. Chat 7 argued that the choice of method is the choice of object. This chat asks a prior question: which methods an English reader can even see. The answer is that the structural reading comes with a translator and front matter, and the existential reading comes as rumour. That asymmetry shapes what the Anglophone literature thinks the cogito is.

It also shows the chat’s own method in use. Three of its load-bearing sources were behind paywalls or logins, and it said so plainly rather than guessing. That is the right practice. It also means those three are where this review can add most.

Prologue

About this part

The French texts behind the English reception, which the chat could only reach second-hand.

In short

Alquié calls the primacy of thought “un faux primat.” The cogito leads to a being it does not contain, and it is certain only “in the instant.” At Royaumont Gueroult calls that being an occult quality. Beck, the one Anglophone at the table, sides with Gueroult.

The quarrel in French.

“Quote in the original with translation where a French text must be adduced.” The brief’s standard, applied to the two texts it most needed.

01What Alquié argued in 1950

The brief attributes two formulas to Alquié 1950. Neither is verbatim in the book. The first is close: the phrase “une pensée transparente à soi” is on p. 181. The second, “l’évidence du sum est première,” is from Royaumont (p. 42), not from the book. What the book does say is stronger and more precise.

T1 · the false primacy of thought

“Aussi ne saurait-on croire, avec les idéalistes, que le cogito soit une pensée transparente à soi, ni, avec M. Gilson, que le substantialisme de Descartes ne soit qu’un réalisme de la pensée, la chose qui pense étant la pensée elle-même. C’est là assimiler le sum au cogito, prétendre qu’il ne lui ajoute rien…”
“Aussi le primat de la pensée ne sera-t-il jamais pour Descartes qu’un faux primat, un primat pour nous, un primat de connaissance. Il faut partir de la pensée, mais pour aller à l’Être… De la sorte, l’idée d’un cogito se suffisant et transparent à soi perd son sens…”

Alquié, La découverte métaphysique, ch. 9, “Existence du moi” pp. 181–182 checked

“So one cannot believe, with the idealists, that the cogito is a thought transparent to itself, nor, with M. Gilson, that Descartes’ substantialism is only a realism of thought, the thing that thinks being thought itself. That is to assimilate the sum to the cogito, to claim it adds nothing to it…” — “So the primacy of thought will never be, for Descartes, anything but a false primacy, a primacy for us, a primacy of knowledge. One must start from thought, but in order to go to Being… In this way the idea of a cogito sufficient to itself and transparent to itself loses its meaning.” (This review’s translation.) The targets are named: the idealists and Gilson. The anti-transparency thesis is a thesis about being. Thought refers beyond itself. It is not a thesis about the reliability of introspection.

Two further details matter for what follows. The first concerns the argument Alquié gives on p. 182. The sum exceeds the cogito “au nom du principe selon lequel « le néant n’a aucunes qualités ni propriétés »” (Principles I.11), and “l’âme n’est pas cogitatio, mais bien res cogitans,” on which “les réponses de Descartes à Hobbes sont sans équivoque” p. 182 checked. The second concerns p. 150. Alquié allows that the Discours cogito is transparent: “point de dépassement … mais une conscience transparente à soi” p. 150 checked. His anti-transparency is a claim about the Meditations alone, which is Chat 7’s Rule 1, “name the text,” practised in 1950.

T2 · a presence doubt cannot touch, limited to the instant

“L’évidence du cogito repose donc sur une présence si intime de la conscience à elle-même que nulle réflexion, nul doute, nulle séparation, nulle subtilité logique ne sauraient, contre elle, prévaloir. La certitude du cogito n’en demeure pas moins limitée à l’instant.”

Alquié, La découverte métaphysique p. 189 checked

“The evidence of the cogito therefore rests on a presence of consciousness to itself so intimate that no reflection, no doubt, no separation, no logical subtlety could prevail against it. The certainty of the cogito nonetheless remains limited to the instant.” (This review’s translation.) This is the sentence Marion quotes in English (§07). Its second half is the problem of Chat 1 and Chat 2: a certainty that holds only “as long as I am thinking” (CSM 2:18), with memory unprotected. Alquié takes the instant as given and puts the surplus in being, not in duration.

02Royaumont 1955: the occult quality, and Beck at the table

The chat reports the occult-quality charge through Peden, Baugh and a 2020 article. The transcript has it in full, and it has more than one form. At its first appearance Gueroult is reading Principles I.63 aloud, in the French version. Alquié seizes on its phrase “la chose même qui pense”: “Il y a donc une chose qui pense … sur laquelle on peut, ou non, faire réflexion” p. 36. Gueroult answers with the charge in the prologue above.

T3 · the charge in full

“…je ne vois pas du tout de textes dans lesquels Descartes oppose un support, une qualité occulte, un être qui ne pourrait pas être atteint par la pensée puisqu’il ne serait pas la pensée, ce qui fait qu’au fond de nous-même nous aurions un être opaque qui nous échapperait… Cela me paraît être la négation même de Descartes qui a combattu toute sa vie ces gens qui mettaient de la qualité occulte soit dans les choses extérieures, soit en moi-même.”

Gueroult, Cahiers de Royaumont p. 39 checked · Peden translates it, p. 77 checked

“I see no texts at all in which Descartes sets up a support, an occult quality, a being that could not be reached by thought since it would not be thought, so that at the bottom of ourselves we would have an opaque being that escaped us… That seems to me the very negation of Descartes, who fought all his life against those who put an occult quality either in external things or in myself.” (This review’s translation.) The charge and transparency are the same argument. In the elided middle, Gueroult rests it on Descartes’ claim that “il n’y a rien en nous que nous ne pourrions rendre conscient si nous le voulions.” At p. 50 he states it outright in Descartes’ voice: “Tout ce qu’il y a dans ma pensée, cela peut devenir instantanément — si je le veux — conscient en moi. Il n’y a rien qui m’échappe.” Alquié: “Pardon, je suis, ici encore, en désaccord.” The text Gueroult is paraphrasing is the Fourth Replies: “there can be nothing in the mind, in so far as it is a thinking thing, of which it is not aware” (CSM 2:171 checked).

Beck at the table. The brief called Beck “the most promising untapped lead: a first-hand English-language witness.” The transcript shows he was more than a witness. He intervenes in the discussion of Alquié’s paper, on whether substantia and natura are synonyms p. 44. He then takes Gueroult’s side on the point at issue:

Je suis de votre avis. Je veux dire que la distinction entre la chose pensante et la pensée est une distinction de raison uniquement. … C’est nous qui distinguons la chose de la pensée. Leslie Beck, to Gueroult, Cahiers de Royaumont p. 45 checked

“I agree with you. I mean that the distinction between the thinking thing and thought is only a distinction of reason. … It is we who distinguish the thing from the thought.” This is exactly what CSM 1:215 says of Principles I.63: the distinction “is merely a conceptual distinction” checked. Alquié concedes the point and keeps his thesis: “Que la distinction … de la pensée et de la chose qui pense soit une distinction de raison, je l’accorde. Mais cela veut dire … qu’il n’y a pas de pensée sans chose qui pense” p. 45. Beck speaks again in the discussion of Gouhier’s paper, pressing Gueroult on whether there are one or two doubts pp. 95–97. In Beth’s session he argues for putting “Locke immédiatement après Descartes” pp. 156–158. He puts a question on Descartes’ Géométrie in the discussion of Hyppolite’s paper p. 176. And he gave his own paper, “L’unité de la pensée et de la méthode” p. 393. All are checked in the table of contents and text.

MG
Martial Gueroult
d. 1976 · Collège de France
His preface to Descartes selon l’ordre des raisons (1953) states the charge against Alquié without naming him. It warns against “a transformed, romanticized doctrine,” like “the virtuoso who would dare play Mozart in the style of Schumann, Chopin, or Ravel” (trans. Ariew, p. xvii checked). An English reader has had that sentence since 1984.
“…alors Descartes ne sera plus Descartes.” — Royaumont, p. 36

04The three “not obtained” sources, obtained

The chat flagged these three as its highest-value unread items, and it was right to. Each changes the picture, though not in the direction it expected.

T4 · Bannan 1960: the quarrel in one sentence

“Le Cercle Culturel de Royaumont held an international colloquium on Descartes in October 1955. Lefèvre is not listed among the participants… The final session turned, perhaps inevitably, to the nature of the history of philosophy, and seems to have been dominated by the exchanges of Guéroult and Alquié: system vs. psychology.”

J. F. Bannan, “Contemporary French Readings of Descartes,” Review of Metaphysics 13.3 (1960) pp. 435–436 checked

What Bannan is actually about. His subject is Roger Lefèvre’s three volumes of 1956–58, “one of the most abrupt entries on any intellectual scene” p. 413. Gueroult comes in over the ontological proof and his debate with Gouhier pp. 429–431. Alquié gets two pages on Descartes, l’homme et l’œuvre pp. 434–435, and a verdict: “This is, obviously, an existentialist reading. The results are uneven.” Bannan also advises that Alquié “should make a concession to Guéroult on his own (Alquié’s) ground” p. 435. His closing sentence is the most generous English statement of Alquié’s aim before Peden: “Alquié makes his reading of Descartes the man an encounter with being, a category adequate to situating scientific method, metaphysics and faith” pp. 437–438. Verdict: Bannan is the first English report that the quarrel happened. He is not an account of what it was about.

T5 · Beck 1965: one sentence, and a decision not to say more

“M. Gouhier and M. Alquié, supreme historians of ‘la critique’, would not admit that M. Gueroult’s choice of technique was the only, or sounder, method of interpretation. It is impossible here to detail the many controversial articles on this subject.”

L. J. Beck, The Metaphysics of Descartes: A Study of the Meditations (Oxford, 1965) p. 22 n. checked

Three corrections to the chat. (1) Beck names Royaumont once, and for Gouhier’s paper, not Alquié’s: Gueroult’s view of the proofs “was criticized by M. Gouhier, in his paper at Royaumont” p. 231. There is no Royaumont apparatus at “pp. 28–38.” (2) Beck sides with Gueroult on the order of the proofs: “we have … implicitly committed ourselves to a point of view akin to that of M. Gueroult” p. 231. The opponent he names is Gouhier, not Alquié. (3) What Beck says of Alquié is warmer than the chat suggests: “Despite disagreement over details of interpretation of texts, I have found M. Alquié’s book one of the most original and stimulating works on Descartes. In no other comparable work does Descartes’s thought seem so living and linked with the personality of its author” p. 13 n. checked. The preface, dated 4 July 1964, thanks all three Frenchmen together p. vii.

T6 · Ariew 1984: the gateway text frames a truce

“And in France, Ferdinand Alquié, Gueroult’s great ‘psychologist’ opponent (who thinks that Descartes’ metaphysics is tied to Descartes’ historical situation and the fact that Descartes has always had problems knowing where reality was, or distinguishing waking from sleeping states), has written favorably about Gueroult’s Descartes…”

Roger Ariew, Introduction to Gueroult, Descartes’ Philosophy Interpreted According to the Order of Reasons I (Minneapolis, 1984) p. xv checked

The brief was right that this is “the most likely place an English reader would first meet the quarrel.” What that reader meets is a single parenthesis that reduces Alquié to biography, followed by his praise of the other side. On the previous page Ariew explains Gueroult’s polemic against the “psychologists” p. xiv. The substance of the dispute, being against concept, is not mentioned. The notes to the introduction (nn. 13–14, which give Alquié’s source) are blank in the held scan not legible. A point of convergence: Ariew’s gloss on Alquié, that Descartes had trouble “distinguishing waking from sleeping,” is the thesis Curley cites from Alquié’s Garnier notes (§06).

Part Two

About this part

The brief’s diagnostic question, whether Alquié is cited as an editor or as an interpreter, and its descent thesis.

In short

The chat is right about the proportion and wrong about the exclusivity. When Alquié is cited as an interpreter, it is on side questions: freedom, the Discours against the Meditations, incarnation. His central thesis is almost never cited. The descent is real, and Marion states it in English.

Citation and descent.

“If overwhelmingly the former, that is the finding — he is present in the apparatus and absent from the argument.”

06Editor or interpreter?

There is much to be said in favor of using the Alquié edition for most purposes. It contains virtually everything one would want, in careful French translations, and, as a bonus, has a better Latin text of the Meditations than AT. Curley, Descartes Against the Skeptics, Bibliographical Note checked

Curley, counted. The brief says Curley cites Alquié “roughly sixty times.” This review counted the held text layer and found 63 mentions. About three-quarters are to the edition (“Alquié, Oeuvres”), and three are to La découverte, which Curley lists among the works he “wished to cite frequently.” The chat’s verdict is right in proportion. The exceptions are where it gets interesting.

All checked. The finding is sharper than the chat’s. In Curley the line between editor and interpreter does not hold. The Garnier notes carry Alquié’s reading, notably the thesis that Descartes really did doubt waking from sleeping. So the reading reaches English footnotes as editorial apparatus. It is the same thesis Ariew uses to sum up Alquié (p. xv).

Beyond Curley. The Oxford Handbook of Descartes and Cartesianism (2019) is the kind of source the brief meant by “mainstream Anglophone.” It cites Alquié 1950 as an interpreter. In C. P. Ragland’s “Descartes on Freedom” (ch. 10), he is cited on whether Descartes “changed his mind about freedom over time, as suggested by Gilson 1913, Alquié 1950, and Schmaltz 2007” p. 180 n. 6. He is cited again for the view that we can “deny clear perceptions” (“Alquié (1950: 286)”) p. 182 n. 16. Both are checked, and La découverte p. 286 is indeed on the liberty of indifference checked. Paul’s 2024 citation of “Alquié 1950 (pp. 286, 288–292)” on the 1645 letter, which the chat reports, falls in the same pages not held. Marion cites him on the body’s “incarnation” (Passive Thought, pp. 59 n. 32, 143 n. 45). He also records “objections” to Descartes’ pineal-gland claim “raised by Alquié, La Découverte, 312, and Margaret Wilson” p. 144 n. 47. And he credits Alquié with the point that the Discours “is still unaware of hyperbolic doubt due to omnipotence” (On the Ego and on God, p. 20).

The pattern, restated

In English, Alquié is cited as an interpreter on peripheral questions: the development of the doctrine of freedom, the gap between Discours and Meditations, and the union and incarnation. On his central thesis, that in the cogito being exceeds concept, he is cited almost nowhere. The two exceptions found are Curley’s bare “cf.” (p. 90 n. 24) and Marion (§07). So “present in the apparatus and absent from the argument” is too strong. The accurate version is “present in the argument everywhere except where his argument was.”

07The descent: Henry, Marion, Ricoeur, Deleuze

The chat’s descent thesis has two parts. Alquié’s position survives in translated successors. And “the Henry–Alquié relationship is essentially never stated explicitly in the English-language literature (it is made in German by Diefenbach).” The first part holds. The second does not.

T7 · Marion states the lineage, in English

“The reasons to avoid the exclusively reflexive and representative interpretation of the ego cogito have often been set out by, among others, Ferdinand Alquié, La Découverte métaphysique de l’homme chez Descartes (Paris: Presses Universitaires de France, 1950), chap. 9 (of which ‘a presence so intimate … that no reflection, no doubt … would know to prevail against it,’ 189); Michel Henry, Généalogie de la psychanalyse … chap. 3 [trans. Douglas Brick, The Genealogy of Psychoanalysis …]; and Jean-Luc Marion, Cartesian Questions, chap. 5, etc.”

Marion, On the Ego and on God: Further Cartesian Questions (Fordham, 2007), note 21 to pp. 8–9 p. 197 checked

Alquié, then Henry, then Marion: the chat’s lineage, in that order, stated by its last member in an English book. Marion also quotes the very sentence of 1950 (T2, p. 189) that states the non-reflexive cogito. The chat did not have this volume. Its conclusion, “available in substance, invisible in attribution,” should become “available in substance, and attributed in a footnote.”

But Marion is not simply Alquié’s heir. On the very point disputed at Royaumont, whether the principal attribute can be distinguished from substance, Marion takes Gueroult’s side:

It suffices to recall the celebrated polemic between F. Alquié and M. Gueroult (who will become our support here), for admitting that the question still remains open. Marion, On the Ego and on God p. 96 checked

That supports the chat’s description of Marion as the one author who “consciously stands between” the two masters. Here is the text that shows it. Marion wrote his dissertation under Alquié (Passive Thought, translator’s introduction, p. xviii checked). On substance he follows Gueroult, and on the cogito he follows Alquié.

The brief’s two test cases, anti-transparency and the primacy of sum. They turn out to be one question, argued at Royaumont with texts on both sides.

Paul and Alquié share a label but not a thesis. The occult-quality charge has an answer, and Alquié gave it: the “surplus” is Descartes’ own inference from thought to a thinking thing. That inference is the one Chat 6 reconstructed.

08Anti-transparency: Alquié, Gueroult, Paul

The chat’s strongest claim of “systematic distortion” is that Paul (2018) rediscovered Alquié’s 1950 thesis without knowing it. The negative half is confirmed. Paul’s article is held in full, pp. 1083–1129 including the references, and it never mentions Alquié checked. The positive half, that it is “the very thesis Alquié advanced,” does not survive a side-by-side reading.

Paul 2018
“Without such radical doubt, introspection remains confused and we lack certain knowledge not just of the specific features of our thoughts, but even of the minimal claim that a thought exists. Far from being the high priest of Transparency, Descartes is radically opposed to it.” p. 1083 checked
Object: introspective access to one’s own thoughts. Claim: it is not self-certifying and needs the method.
Gueroult 1955
“Tout ce qu’il y a dans ma pensée, cela peut devenir instantanément — si je le veux — conscient en moi. Il n’y a rien qui m’échappe.” Royaumont p. 50 checked
Object: the contents of the mind. Claim: none is in principle inaccessible, “if I will it.”
Alquié 1950
The cogito is not “une pensée transparente à soi”: it points to a being it does not contain pp. 181–182. Yet it rests on “une présence si intime … que nulle réflexion, nul doute … ne sauraient, contre elle, prévaloir” p. 189. Object: the being of the thinker. Claim: thought is not self-sufficient.

Assessment [P]. Paul’s anti-transparency concerns how we know our thoughts. Alquié’s concerns whether thought is all there is to the thinker. On Paul’s question, Alquié’s intimacy of the cogito, against which “no doubt” prevails, looks closer to the view Paul opposes. And Gueroult’s “si je le veux” is compatible with Paul: accessibility is conditional on a method of attention. So in the terms of 2018, Paul is nearer Gueroult than Alquié. The case for distortion by non-translation is therefore weaker than the chat claims. What English lacks is not Paul’s thesis under an earlier name. It lacks Alquié’s different thesis altogether. For the two-tier account of certainty that Paul feeds into, see Chat 5.

T8 · the texts each side brought

Gueroult’s text: “there can be nothing in the mind, in so far as it is a thinking thing, of which it is not aware, this seems to me to be self-evident.”
Alquié’s text: “But I do not on that account deny that there can be in the soul or the body many properties of which I have no ideas; I deny only that there are any which are inconsistent with the ideas of them that I do have…”

Fourth Replies, CSM 2:171 checked · To Gibieuf, 19 January 1642, CSMK 3:203 checked. Alquié quotes the Gibieuf letter at Royaumont in answer to Gueroult’s transparency claim p. 50 checked.

These two texts do not contradict each other. Their joint consistency is the quarrel. The Fourth Replies speak of the mind “in so far as it is a thinking thing”: its acts. The Gibieuf letter speaks of “properties” of the soul. If a soul can have properties of which it has no idea, then the thinking thing is more than its thinking, and the anti-transparency thesis is Alquié’s. If “properties” here are only unnoticed modes of thought, Gueroult’s reading stands. Neither chat 8 nor, as far as the folder shows, any English source puts these two passages side by side.

09The primacy of sum, and a reply to the occult-quality charge

The chat’s Question 4 answer is “No English-language reply … was located. … This is a genuine, live scholarly opening.” The negative finding holds for English. But a reply exists, in three places: Alquié made it himself at Royaumont, he had made it in 1950, and it rests on texts this series has already examined.

1 · The charge
A being of thought that is not thought is “une qualité occulte, la plus occulte des qualités” Royaumont p. 36. That is, “un support … extra intellectuel, inconnu et inconnaissable” p. 39.
2 · Alquié’s first answer: the reply to Hobbes
“Il déclare que « nous ne connaissons pas la substance immédiatement par elle-même ». Tout cela est dans le texte des réponses à Hobbes”; and Descartes calls the subjects of acts “des « matières métaphysiques »” p. 44. CSM 2:124: “we do not come to know a substance immediately, through being aware of the substance itself; we come to know it only through its being the subject of certain acts,” and “‘matter’, i.e. metaphysical matter” checked.
3 · The concession
The distinction of thought from thinking thing is “une distinction de raison, je l’accorde. Mais cela veut dire … qu’il n’y a pas de pensée sans chose qui pense” p. 45. The surplus is not a second thing. It is the thing, known only through its acts.
4 · 1950: the argument
The sum exceeds the cogito “au nom du principe selon lequel « le néant n’a aucunes qualités »” (Principles I.11); “l’âme n’est pas cogitatio, mais bien res cogitans” p. 182.
5 · The reply, stated [P]
If the surplus of being over thought is an occult quality, it is the one Descartes introduces whenever he infers a thinking thing from thinking. That is Third Replies CSM 2:124 and Principles I.11, CSM 1:196. This is the inference Dicker’s reconstruction formalises: property requires substance, and thought requires a thinking thing (CSM 2:156, 2:124). Its genealogy runs through nihili nulla sunt attributa (Chat 6, steps 2 and 4, and §03). So Gueroult’s charge convicts Descartes’ substance doctrine or it convicts nothing. Gueroult half sees this: Descartes, he says, “n’a que la terminologie scholastique,” and a later writer “aurait pu dire: la pensée c’est l’activité pensante qui subsiste par soi” p. 44. That is a rational reconstruction, announced as one. Chat 7’s Rule 3 applies to it too.

Where the texts split. Gueroult has Principles I.63: the distinction between thought and thinking substance “is merely a conceptual distinction” (CSM 1:215 checked). Alquié has the Third Replies and the Gibieuf letter. Beck, the one Anglophone present, sided with Gueroult (p. 45). So the English reply the chat calls for would be less an invention than a translation, plus one step: showing that the surplus Alquié defends is the substance Chat 6 found Descartes inferring. The same inference yields, for Dicker, a substance known by its modes, and for Alquié, a being known in “une expérience familière, mais intraduisible” (Royaumont p. 42). The inference is shared. What each takes it to reach is the dispute.

Question 5, again. Does anyone in English hold the primacy of sum? Of the analytic readers in this series, Dicker comes closest: from “I think” he gets a thinking substance, more than a bundle (Chat 6). But he gets it by a conceptual principle, which is exactly what Alquié denies exhausts it. Henry and Marion are the chat’s candidates, and Marion’s note (T7) supports them. The texts are not held for Henry, so the verdict stays “plausible, unverified here.”

Corrections and additions from the folder

  1. Bannan, Beck and Ariew are held. The chat could not open any of them. Bannan is about Lefèvre and reports the quarrel in one sentence (p. 436). Beck cites Royaumont once, for Gouhier (p. 231); “pp. 28–38” is unsupported. Ariew does discuss Alquié (p. xv).
  2. Beck sides with Gueroult against Gouhier on the order of the proofs (p. 231), not “against Alquié and Gouhier.”
  3. The brief’s Alquié formulas: “transparente à soi” is 1950 p. 181. “L’évidence du sum est première” is Royaumont p. 42, not the 1950 book.
  4. “Never stated in English” (the Henry–Alquié relation) is wrong: Marion, On the Ego and on God, p. 197 n. 21.
  5. Peden: “the phenomenologisation of Descartes” is not his phrase (p. 67 has “a phenomenologist avant la lettre”). The “domesticated surrogate for Heidegger” line is ch. 6, p. 195. His word is “novelistic philosophy” (p. 73), not “reads like a novel.” Baugh’s “p. 87” paraphrase does not match p. 87.
  6. Editor/interpreter: interpreter citations exist in Curley (pp. 23, 90), Ragland (Oxford Handbook, pp. 180, 182) and Marion (2007; 2018).
  7. Paul 2018: confirmed to cite no Alquié. The claimed identity of theses is this review’s main disagreement with the chat (§08).
  8. Unverified here: everything the chat quotes from Henry, Ricoeur, Schmaltz, Lærke, Baugh, Dussort, Gutting, Janicaud and Diefenbach.

12Where this leads

The line to keep

The English reader has Gueroult’s system whole, and Alquié in pieces. The pieces are a slogan, a compliment and a footnote. The argument that connects them is in French: the cogito leads to a being it cannot contain, known only through its acts.

The translation this review would put first [P]: not the 1950 book, but the Royaumont discussion of Alquié’s paper, Cahiers de Royaumont pp. 10–71. In about sixty pages it holds both positions, both sets of texts (Principles I.63, the reply to Hobbes, the Gibieuf letter, the Fourth Replies), the occult-quality charge and its answer, and Beck.

Next. Alquié and Gueroult both read Descartes from inside. The contextualists of Chat 7 read him from behind, through his sources. Chat 9 sets up the longest of those readings, Menn’s Augustine. The first thing Menn’s chapter 1 examines is an old claim: that the cogito was already in Augustine’s si fallor, sum.
