The canon of articles

Plato's metaphysics and epistemology — what the field actually argues with

Read this first — what "most cited" could and could not be established

I could not obtain real citation counts. Google Scholar blocks automated access outright; the Semantic Scholar API rate-limited every request. And even if they had answered, their numbers would have misled you here: coverage of pre-1990 humanities journals is patchy, so Vlastos 1954 — the single most-replied-to article in modern Plato studies — typically shows a count an order of magnitude below its real standing, because forty years of citations sit in JSTOR back-content nobody indexed.

So I substituted two measures that are harder to fake and, for this particular field, better. Both are computed from books already in your folder.

Measure 1
Anthologisation
Three collections defined this field for three generations — Allen (1965), Vlastos (1971), Fine (1999). Editors selecting from thousands of papers is peer review at its most brutal. An article in two of the three is canon; the ones in all three are the spine.
Measure 2
Citation inside the corpus
I counted every mention of every major scholar across ~2.8 million characters of full text — Fine's 1999 volume (all 18 chapters plus bibliography) and On Ideas. This measures who the leading modern scholarship is actually in dialogue with.
Verification
Journals from the source
Allen's 1965 Preface names the journal for every one of his twenty articles by number. Every year and journal below for those twenty is read off that page, not recalled.

The surprise in that table

Owen outranks Vlastos almost two to one, and in On Ideas by four to one. Outside the field Vlastos is the famous name — the Socrates books, the Third Man. Inside it, Owen is the one whose arguments everyone is still trying to get past. He published no monograph on Plato; his influence runs entirely through articles, which is exactly why an article-level survey shows what a book-level one hides.

Two caveats on reading these numbers. They are surname counts, so "Owen" absorbs any other Owen and "Frede" merges Michael and Dorothea; and On Ideas is a book about a text Owen wrote the founding modern paper on, which inflates him there specifically. The Plato 1 column is the cleaner signal, and Owen still leads it on articles alone.

The spine — articles that appear in two or more of the three canonical anthologies

Selected independently by three different editors across thirty-four years. This is as close to an objective canon as this field has.

01
The Philosophical Economy of the Theory of Ideas
H. F. Cherniss · 1936 · American Journal of Philology
Placed first by Allen and second by Vlastos — the only article both editors put at the front. Argues that the theory of Ideas is a single economical hypothesis that answers ethical, epistemological and ontological demands simultaneously, rather than three separate theories bolted together. This is the direct ancestor of the question your dispute table is built on: what explanatory demand do the Forms answer? Cherniss's answer is "all of them at once, and that economy is the argument for it."
Allen 1965 · ch. IVlastos 1971 · ch. 2
02
The Third Man Argument in the Parmenides
Gregory Vlastos · 1954 · Philosophical Review
The most-replied-to article in modern Plato studies, and the Stanford Encyclopedia still calls it seminal. Vlastos formalised the regress at Parmenides 132a as requiring two premisses Plato never states — Self-Predication (the Form of Large is itself large) and Non-Identity (the Form is not one of the things it explains) — which are jointly inconsistent. He concluded Plato was recording an honest perplexity, not a solved problem. Everything written on self-predication since is downstream of this paper, including Fine's "broad self-predication."
Allen 1965 · ch. XIIreply, postscript & sequels: Allen chs. XIII–XIV, Vlastos ch. 9, Fine ch. X
03
Participation and Predication in Plato's Middle Dialogues
R. E. Allen · 1960 · Philosophical Review
The classic attempt to defuse Vlastos by denying that self-predication means what Vlastos assumed. Forms are not self-exemplifying members of their own class but standards — "the Form of Large is large" is a different kind of statement from "this brick is large." If that works, the regress never starts. Fine rejects the move but has to answer it, and her account of Forms as explanatory properties is built on the wreckage of this debate.
Allen 1965 · ch. IVVlastos 1971 · ch. 8
04
Plato and the Copula: Sophist 251–259
J. L. Ackrill · 1957 · Journal of Hellenic Studies
Argues Plato distinguishes the "is" of predication from the "is" of identity — and, on Ackrill's reading, the existential "is" as well. The claim that Plato saw the ambiguity of esti is the hinge of every later argument about Republic V, including Fine's contents-versus-objects analysis. Owen's "Plato on Not-Being" (below) is the great counter-attack.
Allen 1965 · ch. XVlastos 1971 · ch. 11
05
Symplokē Eidōn ("The Interweaving of Forms")
J. L. Ackrill · 1955 · Bulletin of the Institute of Classical Studies
Eight pages, and it opened the modern study of the late dialogues. On how Forms combine with one another — which is what makes statements possible at all, and what turns the theory of Forms from a list of separate entities into a structured system. Anthologised twice for a paper barely longer than a book review.
Allen 1965 · ch. IXVlastos 1971 · ch. 10
06
Plato on Not-Being
G. E. L. Owen · 1971
Owen's demolition of the Ackrill line: Plato is not distinguishing senses of "is," and does not need to. The Sophist solves the problem of falsehood without any theory of ambiguity. This is the paper that made Owen the most-cited name in the table above, and the reason "how many senses of esti?" is still an open question rather than a settled one.
Vlastos 1971 · ch. 12Fine 1999 · ch. XVII, p. 416
07
A Proof in the Peri Ideōn
G. E. L. Owen · 1957 · Journal of Hellenic Studies
The reconstruction of Aristotle's Argument from Relatives out of the fragments in Alexander's commentary — and the paper that made Aristotle's lost treatise a live topic in English. Fine's entire On Ideas is, in one sense, a book-length engagement with this article; she cites it constantly and disagrees with its central claim about what conception of Forms the argument requires.
Allen 1965 · ch. XV
08
Plato's Parmenides
Gilbert Ryle · 1939 · Mind
The most famous outsider's intervention: Ryle read the Parmenides as Plato's own logical self-criticism, anticipating twentieth-century worries about type and category. Owen's "Notes on Ryle's Plato" (Fine ch. XI) is the sustained reply, and the pairing is one of the great set-pieces of the field. Read them together or not at all.
Allen 1965 · ch. VIreply: Fine 1999 · ch. XI, p. 298
09
The Place of the Timaeus in Plato's Dialogues
G. E. L. Owen · 1953 · Classical Quarterly
Argued the Timaeus belongs to the middle period, not the late — which, if right, means Plato never abandoned the classical theory of Forms in his last phase. Cherniss's furious reply four years later (Allen ch. XVII) is printed immediately after it in Allen's volume. This exchange is where modern developmentalism was forged, and every claim about "the late Plato" still has to take a side.
Allen 1965 · ch. XVICherniss's reply: Allen ch. XVII (1957, AJP)

Why the three barely overlap — and why that is useful

Fine states in her Preface that for the Oxford Readings volume "all the selections are relatively recent: the earliest appeared in 1970." Allen's runs 1932–1960. So Allen and Fine are almost disjoint by design, and Vlastos (1971) sits between them, sharing four articles with Allen. Together they give you three consecutive generations with very little duplication — about 47 distinct articles, which is close to the whole working canon of the field.
