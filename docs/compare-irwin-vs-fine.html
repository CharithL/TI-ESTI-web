A comparison

Irwin and Fine

Two accounts of the theory of Forms — and they are not the two sides you expect

T. H. Irwin, "The Theory of Forms"
Plato 1 (1999), ch. V, pp. 143–170 Gail Fine, On Ideas (1993) &
Plato on Knowledge and Forms (2003)
The headline

They are allies, not opponents — and they cite each other saying so.

Irwin's footnote 24, on Plato's views about flux, sends you to On Ideas, 54–7. Fine's footnote 46 in On Ideas sorts the possible positions on flux into three options and files Irwin under option (ii) — her own. Three notes later she writes that several commentators argue Plato appeals to compresence rather than succession, "and I concur," citing Owen and Irwin.

So the central thesis you have been tracking through Fine — that the engine of the theory is compresence of opposites, not Heraclitean flux — is a thesis Irwin argues for independently and Fine explicitly endorses him on. If you came to this chapter expecting the orthodoxy Fine attacks, that is not what it is.

A correction to what I told you earlier

In the reading path I framed Irwin's chapter as "the orthodoxy she is attacking," and in the canon survey as "the standard middle-period picture." Having now read the chapter itself rather than relying on its position in the anthology, that framing was wrong and I have corrected the reading-path file.

The mistake was inferring a position from placement — Fine put Irwin at ch. V, immediately before the chapters that dispute the standard picture, so I assumed he supplied it. He does not. Fine's real opponents on flux are Cornford and Cherniss, whom she and Irwin both cite in order to reject. Irwin's chapter also never discusses separation, self-predication, or the Two Worlds Theory at all — I checked, and the terms appear zero times or once in passing.

Where they actually differ

Four real divergences. Note that none of them is the one you would have predicted.

QuestionIrwinFineNature of the gap
Is there a second kind of compresence? No such notion. His account stops at compresence in types. Nothing corresponds to indeterminacy, and he offers no general account of how sensible particulars fall short. Yes — broad compresence. Something is F and not-F broadly if, besides being F, it also has features inessential to being F. "Every F thing other than the property of F is F and not F in the broad sense." This is how she handles the Timaeus (every sample of fire is fire and not fire) and artefact forms.On Ideas ch. 7 §5, p. 100 Substantive. Fine's apparatus is strictly larger
Are Socratic forms universals? No — in Plato's Moral Theory (1977), with Penner, he argues Socratic forms are not universals but such things as conditions or powers. Yes, with Aristotle — but she then defuses the dispute: "Penner and Irwin do not disagree with Aristotle about the nature of Socratic forms; rather, they disagree with him about the nature of universals."On Ideas ch. 2, n. on universals Real but verbal, on Fine's own diagnosis
Why exactly do the senses fail? Context-dependence. His positive contribution. Six inches is largeness in a mouse and smallness in an elephant; which it is depends on a standard of comparison that is not itself observable. "if contextual facts are essential to the nature of a property, that property is not an observable property."Irwin §11, pp. 162–3 She does not run this argument. Her route to non-sensible Forms goes through the epistemology — PKW and PKD — not through an analysis of what perception can register. Different route, not contradiction
What is the governing question? How do we get from Socratic definition to non-sensible Forms? Reconstructing the arguments, dialogue by dialogue, testing Aristotle's report against the text. What are Forms, and what can be known? Their ontological status — separate, immanent, causal — plus the epistemology that requires them. Different projects that happen to overlap

How to use the two together

They divide the labour cleanly, and reading them in the wrong order wastes the fit. Irwin answers "why did Plato think there had to be Forms at all?" — argument by argument, from the Euthyphro through the Phaedo to the flux question. Fine answers "granted there are Forms, what are they and what can we know?" — separation, immanence, causation, the two worlds.

So: read Irwin ch. V before Stage 5 of the reading path, not as opposition but as the long version of the argument Fine compresses into three pages at 2003 Introduction §VII. He spends twenty-eight pages establishing what she states in two sentences. If her hinge passage felt too fast, this is the remedy.

Where to find the real opposition instead

If you want the position Fine is actually arguing against, it is not Irwin. On flux and the unknowability of sensibles: Cornford, Plato's Theory of Knowledge (1935), 99–101, and Cherniss, Aristotle's Criticism, 211–18 — both in your folder, and both cited by Fine and Irwin as the view to reject. On knowledge and belief having distinct objects: Cross & Woozley, "Knowledge, Belief and the Forms," in the Vlastos anthology, ch. 5 — and, at book length and much more recently, Jessica Moss, Plato's Epistemology (2021).
