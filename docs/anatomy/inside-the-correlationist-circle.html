Inside the Correlationist Circle

Solipsism, Meillassoux’s After Finitude, and the brain that sees itself.

The thread starts from a semantic objection to solipsism and tests it against Quentin Meillassoux’s After Finitude. It isolates the two steps his argument rests on: turning “for all we know, things could be otherwise” into “things really can be otherwise”, and generalising from the possibility of my death to the contingency of everything. It ends with a neuroscience version of the correlationist circle, the brain that sees itself.

Terms used in the thread

Correlationism
Meillassoux’s name for the view that we only ever have access to the correlation between thinking and being, never to either term apart from the other.
Facticity
The forms of that correlation can be described but not derived, so for all we know they could be otherwise.
Ancestral, arche-fossil
A reality anterior to the emergence of humans, or of life itself; and present material that indicates such a reality, such as an isotope or the light of a distant star.
Principle of unreason
Meillassoux’s thesis that nothing has a reason to be as it is, so that only contingency is necessary.
Epistemic and real possibility
“For all I know, it could be otherwise”, as against “it really can be otherwise”.
Ro, Rs
The ordinary sense of “real” and the sense the word has in the solipsist’s conclusion.
The two beams
The two steps the thread finds Meillassoux’s argument resting on: the conversion from “for all we know” to “really”, and the step from my non-being to everything.
B0, BEA
The brain that produces experience, and the brain as it appears inside A’s epistemological horizon.

Part I

Solipsism and meaning

A semantic objection to solipsism, and how to answer it.

§1

Analysing the semantic objection

Sun 27 Sep · 20:13
Charith

How do you analyse and reply to this argument? Answer in English.

Attached argumentA semantic objection to solipsism

I would like to propose an objection to solipsism concerning the meaning of the concepts used to formulate it. By metaphysical solipsism, I mean the position that only my mind and its experiences exist, and that there is no reality independent of them. My question is whether this position can preserve the meanings it needs to express its own conclusion.

Consider how we distinguish a perceived tree from an imagined tree, an actual event from a fictional event, or an independently existing object from our representation of it. Expressions such as “real,” “imaginary,” “mental,” and “external” acquire determinate content within these distinctions. My concern arises when a philosopher employs this vocabulary to conclude that the entire world is merely mental or that nothing exists independently of experience.

Let Ro designate the meaning of “real” in the ordinary distinction under examination, and let Rs designate its meaning in the solipsist’s conclusion. I am not assuming that “real” has only one ordinary meaning; the question concerns whichever meaning the argument initially employs.

Suppose we begin by classifying the tree as real:

Ro(t).

The philosopher subsequently concludes that the tree is not real:

¬Rs(t).

What makes the second statement a substantive denial of the first? The repetition of the word “real” cannot by itself establish that the same claim is being assessed.

My argument rests on the following premises:

  1. An expression’s familiar wording does not guarantee continuity of meaning. Its meaning must be identifiable through its application, its contrasts, or its implications.
  2. An argument must preserve the meanings on which its inference depends, or justify the connections between different meanings. Support for a premise stated in one sense cannot automatically support a conclusion stated in another.
  3. If certain distinctions or conditions are constitutive of a concept’s meaning, retaining that meaning requires retaining those distinctions or conditions. Merely retaining the word is insufficient.
  4. A philosophical revision must therefore explain what is retained, what is rejected, and how the resulting statement remains intelligible.

These premises generate three possibilities.

First, Rs retains the meaning of Ro. In that case, simultaneously maintaining Ro(t) and ¬Ro(t) is contradictory. However, withdrawing the original judgment is not itself contradictory. The further question is whether the philosopher can withdraw it while preserving the conditions that make the relevant sense of “real” intelligible. My objection concerns this possible dependence of meaning on what the conclusion rejects.

Second, Rs has a different meaning. The philosopher must explain that meaning and its relationship to Ro. Calling it a “deeper meaning” or an “extension” does not supply this explanation. What distinguishes the proposed concept from an unrelated property, such as being amusing or having an umami taste? The explanation must identify what the term permits us to assert, deny, distinguish, or infer. Until that connection is established, ¬Rs(t) cannot simply be presented as a refutation of Ro(t).

Third, no determinate account of Rs is supplied. In that case, the argument has not established the content of its supposed metaphysical conclusion. This is a failure to explain the claim, even before we assess its truth.

The same problem arises with “mental.” If that term initially distinguishes an imagined representation from the object represented, its later application to everything requires explanation. Otherwise, the argument risks transferring support from one meaning of “mental” to another.

The immediate conclusion is that a solipsistic argument must demonstrate semantic continuity or justify its conceptual revision. It cannot establish its conclusion merely by reusing familiar words after altering their role.

My stronger hypothesis is that solipsism rejects conditions essential to the meaning of its own vocabulary. Let C stand for those conditions. The stronger argument would be:

  1. Meaningfully using the relevant concept requires C.
  2. Solipsism entails that C does not obtain.
  3. Therefore, solipsism cannot be both true and meaningfully stated using that concept.

The substantive burden lies in identifying C and defending both premises. I recognize that showing how a concept was originally learned does not by itself establish what is necessary for its continued meaningful use. Nor does understanding a contrast automatically establish actual instances on both sides.

My question is whether the solipsist can provide a determinate account of “real,” “mental,” and “independent” that supports the intended denial while avoiding reliance on conditions that the denial itself excludes.

Claude

The short version: the argument is well built and appropriately hedged, its modest conclusion is right, and its stronger conclusion is where all the work remains, because the solipsist has a standard move it doesn’t yet block. Here is how I’d take it apart and where I’d push.

What kind of argument it is

A meaning-theoretic self-refutation argument in two tiers: a burden-shifting trilemma (same sense / new sense specified / no sense specified) and a transcendental claim that solipsism denies the conditions C of its own vocabulary. Its nearest relatives are Austin on “real” as a trouser-word in Sense and Sensibilia (the negative use wears the trousers, so “not real” needs a specific contrast: fake, painted, dreamt, hallucinated), Carnap’s internal/external distinction (Ro is an internal question inside the thing-framework, Rs looks like an external one, which Carnap says has no cognitive content), Putnam’s brain-in-a-vat argument (the strong version’s exact structure), and Tractatus 5.62–5.64. The relatives matter because their known counters transfer.

What it gets right

The trilemma is exhaustive, so it does establish a burden: anyone reusing “real” owes an account of what the word now licenses and excludes. Austin strengthens horn 2 considerably. “Not real, as opposed to what?” has to be answered, and “as opposed to nothing” isn’t an answer.

Horn 1 can do more than it currently does, without the genetic fallacy the author rightly flags. If Ro has application criteria (that is what “perceived, not imagined” comes to) and t satisfies them, then ¬Ro(t) is not a withdrawal but a misapplication, which is evidence the speaker has left Ro. That pushes horn 1 into horn 2 by criteria rather than by learning history.

The solipsist’s best reply, which the argument doesn’t yet block

Berkeley, Principles §§29–33: real things are those ideas that are involuntary, vivid, steady, orderly and coherent; imagined things are the ones that aren’t. That reconstructs the perceived/imagined contrast entirely within experience. Then §35: nothing apprehended by sense is denied, only what philosophers call Matter. The solipsist inherits this move. On it, Ro(t) stays true, “real” keeps its criteria and its contrast, nothing is equivocated, and the metaphysical conclusion is not ¬Rs(t) at all but ¬M(t): t is not mind-independent. The trilemma is discharged by taking horn 2 and giving the account.

So the objection survives only if it relocates to “independent,” which the final sentence already does. But the body is written for “real” and “mental,” where it’s weakest. I’d move the weight.

Where the strong version is exposed

  1. Universality is not vacuity. “Everything is mental” has the same form as “everything is physical,” and nobody thinks physicalism is meaningless for lack of non-physical things. The author concedes this (contrast needs possible, not actual, non-instances), but the concession means horn 3 must show that the solipsist’s “mental” has no determinate satisfaction conditions, not merely no contrast class. That’s harder, since “mental” has positive candidates: experienced, phenomenally present, intentional.

  2. Stroud’s objection (1968). Premises of the form “meaningful use requires C” reliably deliver at most “the speaker must take C to obtain,” not “C obtains.” Read strongly, premise 1 is undefended; read weakly, premise 2 fails, since solipsism entails ¬C, not ¬(taken-as-C). Closing that gap usually needs a verificationist or idealist bridge, which is the anti-realist’s premise, not the realist’s.

  3. Dependence on a semantic theory. The version that actually delivers “cannot be both true and stated” is Putnam’s: reference is fixed by use or causal contact, so if solipsism were true, “real” in the solipsist’s mouth would refer to the phenomenal role-player and ¬Rs(t) would be false. Strong, but it imports externalism, which is exactly what the solipsist rejects. A Wittgensteinian version (no private standards of correctness) is more congenial but drags in the whole community-vs-individualist dispute over the private language argument.

  4. The equivocation charge assumes a route. “We classify t as real, then the philosopher concludes it isn’t” fits an argument that starts from Ro(t). The Cartesian route doesn’t: it starts from “I have experiences as of a tree” and “no valid inference runs from that to M(t).” “Real” never appears as a premise, so no support is transferred between senses. Against that route the objection must target the intelligibility of M itself.

Where I’d locate C

In inferential role, not contrast class. The ordinary “real” licenses counterfactual and third-person commitments: the tree persists unperceived; had I not looked it would still have been there; someone else could see it. A phenomenal surrogate either drops these (then premise 3 applies: the concept has changed and the solipsist’s “real” is a different predicate wearing the old word) or keeps them (then what, in an ontology of actual experiences only, makes “had I looked, I would have seen” true? Mill’s permanent possibilities of sensation are the classic attempt, and a solipsist has nothing for the possibilities to be grounded in). Kant’s refutation of idealism and Davidson’s triangulation are alternative ways of filling C, with the same shape: something the solipsist’s ontology has no room for. This is a C that solipsism specifically denies and physicalism doesn’t, which is the asymmetry the argument needs. The overgeneralization test (“does this also refute physicalism?”) is the instrument for finding C, and it points at publicity and independence of standards.

The natural terminus

Pressed to the end, the objection may not deliver “solipsism is false.” If “independent” is defined within experience, the solipsist’s denial is false; if it’s defined as transcending all possible experience, the denial is as contentless as the assertion, and the objection cuts symmetrically. That is Wittgenstein’s result: solipsism, strictly carried out, coincides with pure realism. Dissolution and refutation are different outcomes with different burdens. The strong hypothesis promises the second; the argument’s structure delivers the first.

How I’d reply

Grant the immediate conclusion. Deny that the strong hypothesis is yet established. Put the burden back: the argument needs (i) C specified as inferential role, (ii) an explicit premise that no phenomenal reconstruction preserves that role, defended against Berkeley and Mill, and (iii) a response to Stroud, most plausibly by settling for dissolution rather than falsity and presenting that as the win: what’s left of solipsism is not a rival to realism but an alternative notation for it.

Meillassoux and the “we”

Must Meillassoux earn the community of thinkers before he can use it?

§2

The argument against Meillassoux

Sun 27 Sep · 20:30
Charith

AttachedAfter Finitude (PDF), and an earlier exchange with another assistant. In it, Charith argued that by the standard of Meillassoux’s own goal he should have established other minds first; that if the argument is run for a single cogito, the language and concepts it needs disappear; and that, as in chess, a win depends only on the rules, yet without an outside there would be no game.

How do you analyse my argument against Meillassoux?

Claude

The short version: as it stands, the argument doesn’t land as an inconsistency, because it aims at a link Meillassoux doesn’t need. But the instinct behind it is right, and once you redirect it, the same instinct yields two objections that do bite, both of which use the semantic framework from your solipsism paper.

What you’re claiming

Two claims run together in the transcript:

  1. Meillassoux must establish other minds (or the legitimacy of the community) before his argument can begin, because his goal requires it.
  2. Run his argument for a single cogito and language, concepts and mathematics vanish, so the very thing he defends vanishes; hence he owes an account of how cogitos share experience.

What’s right

The cogitamus is never argued for. He takes it from the correlationist, names it “species solipsism,” and never returns to it. And there is a real textual seam: in Chapter 1, footnote 13, he admits the correlationist can re-correlate individual death through the community (to die is to die for other consciousnesses), calls that a “desperate sophism” without answering it, and says he restricted himself to ancestrality precisely to avoid the loophole. Chapter 3 then runs the derivation of factiality through individual death anyway. You found that seam; hold onto it.

Why it doesn’t land as stated

  1. The argument is immanent. The “we” is the opponent’s premise, not his. Anyone who argues is inside a discourse, so a solipsist who argues is caught too, and one who doesn’t argue is no interlocutor. Worse for your version: against a solipsist, the individual-level mortality argument works better, because the community loophole isn’t available.

  2. The loophole closes one level up. The correlationist must grant that total extinction is thinkable, or she collapses into Husserl’s eternal ego (Chapter 5, footnote 54). Then the argument reruns: to think our extinction as a real possibility, we must think it as needing none of our thought to be actual. That is where he actually runs it in Chapter 5. So the repair for the seam is inside the book. The objection should say: state the derivation at the extinction level and check whether the regress argument still works there.

  3. Your objection is the same logical genus as his. Premises 1–3 form a refutational argument: you cannot deny the community without using what requires it. That is Meillassoux’s anhypothetical form. You can’t indict the genus. And your version yields community-for-thought at most, whereas his is built for the one content (thought’s own absence) that can’t be a correlate. Since he only ever uses community-for-us, no illegitimate crossing of G₁ is needed.

  4. The chess analogy cuts both ways. Validity versus existence-conditions favours him, not you. And chess positions have player-independent properties (mate in three) that play discovers and doesn’t create: that is his mathematical realism in miniature. The Wittgensteinian rejoinder (the rules are conventions) is the correlationist’s, and his reply is the statement/referent distinction you were already given.

Where the instinct bites

A. Act-conditions versus content-conditions. His derivation of the “there is” runs on a conceivability premise: the two realms of existence and non-existence are the condition for the conceivability of facticity, so necessarily something exists. The pattern is: contingency is thinkable; its thinkability requires X; therefore X is absolutely necessary. Now feed in your premise 3. Community is required for the thinkability of anything. By the same pattern, the community is absolutely necessary, which his own principle forbids (no determinate entity is necessary) and the arche-fossil empirically refutes. Trilemma: the pattern is unsound (then neither “there is” nor non-contradiction is derived); or premise 3 is false; or “condition of thinkability” means content-condition, not act-condition, a distinction the text never draws and now has to. That is the internal form of your objection. It demands no proof of other minds; it shows his derivation method equivocates on “thinkability.”

B. Semantic continuity on “possible.” Your Ro/Rs instrument applies exactly to the hinge of Chapter 3: “possibility of ignorance” (for all I know, the in-itself is otherwise) is converted into “real possibility” (it can be otherwise). He calls this a change in outlook and defends it by the regress: to de-absolutize you must re-absolutize. Apply your premise 2. The agnostic’s “could” is epistemic; the speculative “could” is ontological; the regress works only if the first has no content except through the second. That step, not the other-minds step, is the load-bearing one, and it is the one Hallward attacked (“Anything is Possible,” Radical Philosophy 2008). Your framework sharpens it: run the trilemma (same sense / different sense explained / no sense) on “possible” rather than on “real.”

C. The convergence. Your solipsism objection says “exists,” “real” and “independent” lose content when the subject is subtracted. Meillassoux subtracts every subject. The same test applies: is “exists” determinate for a world with no givenness? His answer is mathematizable properties, and the derivation of mathematics’ absolute scope is exactly what the book says it does not deliver (he later attempts it in “Iteration, Reiteration, Repetition,” 2012, through the meaningless sign, a meaning-theoretic move, i.e. on your home ground). Note what this makes you: a correlationist about meaning, which is his opponent’s position, and the book’s whole answer to that opponent is Chapter 3. So the meaning-theoretic debt sits at the mathematics step and the modal hinge, not at other minds.

One decision you have to make

Either you accept refutational arguments, in which case you can’t say he merely “presupposes” the community without also granting that presupposing can establish it, and the fight moves to contents (B and A). Or you go Stroudian: such arguments show only what thought must take to be so. That would hurt him, but it disarms your anti-solipsism argument and your premise 3 in the same stroke. A and B survive on the first option; the other-minds demand survives on neither.

§3

Must the project start from solipsism?

Sun 27 Sep · 21:04
Charith

Let’s forget opponents or friends and just consider the argument. My argument is: if anyone tries to fulfil Meillassoux’s goal as stated in Chapter 1, they have to start from solipsism. And solipsism has its own internal contradictions, as I have shown above with the semantic problems. Whatever the solution for solipsism, my reformulation is: should Meillassoux, or anyone pursuing his goal, start from solipsism?

Claude

Yes, with one distinction that changes what the claim does. “Start from solipsism” can mean two things: take the given-to-one as the starting standpoint, granting nothing beyond it (methodological solipsism, Descartes’ Second Meditation, Husserl’s sphere of ownness), or treat “only I exist” as a thesis to be settled before anything else. Your earlier formulation was the second, which is why it didn’t land. The reformulation is the first, and I’d defend it.

Why the goal requires the standpoint

On his own terms. He says the correlation is never given anywhere but in individuals; the transcendental subject is instantiated by bodies and has no existence apart from its instances; and the circle’s force comes from not being able to get out of our own skins. Skins are singular. So the rigorous circle is drawn around one instance. Other subjects fall on the correlate side of that instance’s correlation, exactly as trees do. The cogitamus is therefore a plurality of correlates, not a plurality of thinkers, and the communal starting points of the strong model (language-games, forms of life, Mitsein) are what that model asserts, not what the given delivers. Husserl saw this and made transcendental solipsism a stage one must pass through (Cartesian Meditations, Fifth Meditation, §§42–44); Kant’s “I think” is first-personal even though his criterion of objectivity is intersubjective. Meillassoux’s cogitamus is a dialectical concession to Wittgenstein and Heidegger, not a principled standpoint. And his own Chapter 3 derivation is written in the first person throughout. So the book already half-starts where you say it should, while saying it doesn’t.

It also costs him nothing to be explicit. Run the mortality argument from c₁ and the footnote 13 loophole (death re-correlated as death-for-others) is unavailable, because there are no others yet to re-correlate it. The explanandum survives too: science is given to c₁ as a practice-in-the-given, so the question “what legitimates these ancestral statements?” can be posed about given statements without first earning the scientists.

What your semantic objection then does

It doesn’t undermine the standpoint; it’s the first theorem you prove from it. Methodological solipsism is a standpoint; metaphysical solipsism is one possible verdict on that standpoint. Your objection says the verdict isn’t statable: the given-to-one cannot preserve the meanings it needs to say “only this exists.” Read positively, that is a result about the standpoint: the given-to-one already contains the conditions of its own meaning, and those conditions reach beyond it. That is an exit from the circle. It is a semantic exit, not a factial one, and it happens before factiality gets going.

The fork this opens

Everything now turns on whether that semantic result actually establishes anything beyond the given, which is the same decision as before.

  1. If the semantic objection succeeds (meaning requires public standards, which require others, subject to Stroud’s caveat about what transcendental arguments deliver), then the circle cannot be drawn at c₁ at all. The first exit from correlation is semantic, and it earns the community and at most a synchronic shared world (Kant’s Refutation of Idealism and Davidson’s triangulation are the models). It cannot earn the ancestral: meaning-conditions deliver a world coeval with speakers, never a world before them. So factiality’s job shrinks to the dia-chronic. “Getting out of ourselves” is done by someone else’s argument; Meillassoux gets us to the past.

  2. If it fails (a solitary thinker can have standards), then c₁ is a stable standpoint, the factial argument runs from it cleanly, and the community is never earned at all: others stay correlates from beginning to end. The absolute reached then contains contingency and, if the promised derivation ever came through, mathematizable structure. Other minds as minds are not in it, since mentality is neither a figure of factiality nor a primary quality. His materialism reaches the arche-fossil before it reaches a neighbour. He might accept that, given his hostility to vitalism, but it has to be stated, and it is the precise sense in which the solipsism problem is “encompassed” by his goal rather than skipped.

So what the reformulation establishes

The c₁ stage can’t be skipped, and the book skips it. That conclusion holds on both horns. What differs is the force: on the first horn it’s a re-attribution (the exit from the circle is semantic, and factiality is credited only with the dia-chronic); on the second it’s a scope result (the absolute reached excludes subjects as subjects). Neither is a refutation. The honest name for the demand is a Fifth Meditation, and your semantic work is a candidate for it in place of Husserl’s analogical apperception.

One consequence you may not want but should own: on the first horn your argument makes his book more defensible on a narrower claim. If semantic arguments can only reach the synchronic, then something like factiality is the only route to the dia-chronic, which is a defence of his originality that the book itself doesn’t state clearly. The paper that comes out of this is a division-of-labour theorem, not an objection: semantic exit for the contemporaneous, factial exit for the ancestral, and a demonstration that the second cannot be built without passing through the first.

Part III

From my death to everything

How the death argument is meant to reach every law, and why it doesn’t.

§4

From my death to every physical law

Tue 29 Sep · 22:49
Charith

AttachedAfter Finitude (PDF), and another assistant’s five-step reconstruction of the death argument, with Charith’s question to it: “So Meillassoux first says we need to prove that the world exists before the thinker, and then he says there will be a time without thinkers in the future, which is the same as what he is trying to solve? What if, instead of ancestral statements, we take future statements?”

How does Meillassoux show that the possibility of the thought–object relation not existing gives the conclusion that absolute contingency is possible? I mean, even if we grant that the correlationist holds that the thought–world relation could be contingent, how does it follow that every physical law is contingent?

Claude

It doesn’t follow from that alone, and the death case isn’t meant to carry it alone. Its job in Chapter 3 is to convert the kind of possibility, from “for all we know it could be otherwise” to “it really can be otherwise.” The universality, laws included, comes in through other routes, and none of them can carry it.

Where the laws come in

  1. Inherited scope. The correlationist’s facticity was never only about the thinker. In Chapter 2 (p. 39) it covers the invariants of the given, and his list includes causality, the forms of perception and the laws of logic. Absolutize facticity and all of that comes along.
  2. Same ground, same verdict. In the death dialectic (pp. 55–57), what licenses “I could cease to be” is not physiology. It is the absence of any reason for my being and staying as I am. That ground isn’t special to me: nothing grounds the laws either (Hume’s billiard balls, and Chapter 2’s refusal of real necessity). So if unreason yields real possibility for me, it yields it for everything. The text never states this as a parity argument. The extension happens inside one clause, where the agnostic is said to access the capacity-to-be-other of everything, ours and the world’s. But it is the only step that gets from “I” to “everything.”
  3. The time argument (pp. 61–62). A time governed by fixed laws can be thought of as giving way to another time with other laws, so only a lawless time can be thought as eternal. This is the one place in Chapter 3 that argues about laws specifically.

Chapter 4 adds no support. It answers an objection (contingent laws should change often), and at the end of the chapter he says his answer is a supposition, not a derivation.

Why the laws don’t follow

A counter-model. Take this position: states without thought are really possible, and the laws are necessary. It accepts everything the death argument could give and denies the conclusion. It is roughly the naturalist’s view: thinkers perish because the laws are what they are. His own example of precariousness (p. 62) is a human being perishing under unchanged laws.

In the dialectic, this position is excluded by the correlationist’s first principle: nothing can be thought outside the correlation. Once Meillassoux has broken the circle, as he claims to, that exclusion lapses. What remains against it is Chapter 2’s point that no entity’s necessity can be demonstrated. That is “for all we know” again, so excluding the position needs the very conversion at issue. The inference is invalid as it stands.

Why the death argument can’t reach further. What forces the possibility of my death outside the correlation is that my death is an event whose actuality excludes my thinking. A possibility of it that needed my thought to persist would not be a possibility of my death. That is a feature of the content (thought’s absence), not of the ground (unreason).

Parity carries the ground over to the laws, but not the feature that did the de-correlating. “Gravity could reverse tomorrow” contains no absence of thought. So the correlationist can hold it as a possibility-for-us without collapsing into idealism. Even granted, the argument de-correlates only possibilities whose content is the absence of thought.

Why the ground looked universal. Inside the circle, unreason is the only ground the correlationist can cite for the correlation’s possible non-being, because she can’t appeal to laws in themselves. The conclusion is asserted outside the circle, where a realist can ground the same possibility in the laws. The universality is borrowed from the opponent’s lack of grounds, and kept after that lack is gone.

The conversion can’t be general. Reaching the laws requires turning “for all we know, otherwise” into “really otherwise” everywhere. Test that on his M1/M2 exchange, right after the death dialectic. The correlationist’s options are:

  • M1 is necessary.
  • M2 is necessary.
  • Both are really possible.

Convert all three. On the standard (S5) logic of metaphysical modality, whatever is possibly necessary is necessary, so M1 and M2 both come out necessary. They are rival accounts of death, so that is incoherent. The same step, applied to the necessary God the strong correlationist can’t rule out, is the modal ontological argument: the argument Chapter 2 treats as the keystone of metaphysics.

His answer is that what’s absolute is the openness itself. But the agnostic’s openness is between the necessity options and the contingency option. Identifying it with the third option settles the question it left open. The principle behind the regress is also false in general: Goldbach’s conjecture is open for us either way, and whichever answer is true is necessarily true.

For laws, this leaves a live alternative that his map of necessity doesn’t show. He recognizes two kinds: logical necessity, and real necessity proved from sufficient reason. Grant him that the second can’t be proved. But since Kripke, necessity can be a posteriori: water’s not being H₂O is conceivable but not possible. Dispositional essentialists (Bird, Nature’s Metaphysics, 2007) hold that laws are necessary in exactly that way. For them, Hume’s billiard balls are conceiving different properties, not these properties behaving otherwise.

The Hume–Kant critique refutes proofs of necessity, not that view. Chapter 4 answers it with Ockham’s razor. That is a parsimony argument, and the dispositionalist will contest it, since for her necessity is what explains stability.

The time argument assumes both disputed steps: that a lawful time can be thought of as giving way to another, and that thinkable-otherwise is really-possible-otherwise. It adds nothing independent.

What the argument actually reaches

Suppose the death argument survives its own problem. (The strong correlationist says she accesses the possibility of her non-being only as a correlate, not that it exists only as one; the collapse into idealism needs the second.) Then it establishes that states without thought are really possible.

That is exactly the class of dia-chronic contents. It is the modal half of the Chapter 5 thesis, possible existence outside thought, and it is what ancestrality needs. Contingent laws, hyper-Chaos, and the derivations of non-contradiction and the “there is” all need the universal principle, which the argument doesn’t reach.

The surplus also costs him. Chapter 1 notes that dating relies on constant decay rates. Factiality leaves that constancy a brute fact, and Chapter 4 shows only that stability is compatible with contingency, not that it is to be expected. That is an awkward result for a book whose aim is to ground science’s ancestral statements.

Claude

Close, but the analogy puts the fault in the wrong place, and locating it exactly makes your objection stronger.

Why the flow can’t start from the principle

Your 1→2→3 is the order of application: given the principle, my non-being is an instance of it. It can’t be the order of proof, because the principle of unreason is what the death case is supposed to establish. Meillassoux announces the thesis first, but as something still to be proved.

Suppose (1) is the correlationist’s unreason (“I know of no reason why things are thus”). Then (2) comes out only as “for all I know, I could cease to be,” which is not yet a real possibility. Suppose instead (1) is Meillassoux’s unreason (no reason, full stop). Then the argument assumes its conclusion.

So the proof has to run the other way:

  • the opponent’s epistemic unreason, which is universal in scope,
  • then the death case, where it supposedly must be thought as real,
  • then the principle, now real and universal.

The death case isn’t just an instance of the principle; it’s where the principle is supposed to be proved. That puts your question in exactly the right place: what takes “real for my non-being” to “real for everything”?

Why it isn’t simply the crow

The crow is induction from an observation, and one observation never supports “all.” The death case isn’t observed; it’s argued. In an argument, one case can support “all” if the case is arbitrary. If you prove something about “any number n” without using anything special about n, it holds for every n.

That’s the charitable reading of his step: my non-being is meant as an arbitrary instance of facticity, and the ground, unreason, is meant to be general. So the step isn’t a fallacy merely because it starts from one case.

Where it actually fails

The case isn’t arbitrary. What made the possibility of my non-being real, rather than merely for-us, wasn’t unreason. It was the content: a possibility of my not-being that depended on my thinking it would not be a possibility of my not-being. That feature belongs to this case only.

So the step has the form “2 is prime, and 2 is even, so primes are even.” It’s correct about the instance, but the proof used what’s special about it.

Your crow, repaired

The analogy does fit once both of its faults are made explicit, because Meillassoux’s argument has the same two.

  • No reason vs. no known reason. Crow colours have reasons: white crows are albino or leucistic. “There is no reason a crow should be white” really means “none I know of.” That’s the slide from epistemic to real.
  • The exceptional case. Even if this crow’s whiteness were truly uncaused, that would say nothing about ordinary crows. A white crow is exactly the unrepresentative case.

The death case is supposed to repair the first fault, but it does so with a feature only that case has, so it commits the second. The faithful version of your crow reads like this:

  • this crow’s colour must be genuinely uncaused, because of something peculiar to this crow;
  • no one knows the reason for any crow’s colour;
  • so every crow’s colour is genuinely uncaused.

The peculiarity does all the work in the first step and is missing from the rest.

What would make the step valid

He would need an argument that the de-correlation doesn’t depend on the content: that for any x, treating x’s capacity to be otherwise as merely for-us collapses into idealism.

In terms of his own method, since the principle is proved by refuting its denial: he needs a refutation that works against every denial, the way Aristotle’s does for non-contradiction (anyone who says anything at all presupposes it). Meillassoux’s refutes only one denial, namely that thought’s absence is possible merely for-us.

Now take someone who holds that the laws are necessary and also grants that thought can cease. She doesn’t refute herself. So his refutation never reaches her.

Granting coherence: access and mathematics

Access: three grants, three different results

  1. Grant coherence. You’ve granted nothing his opponent didn’t. Strong correlationism already counts “the in-itself is contingent” as one of the equally valid hypotheses about the in-itself, alongside “it is necessary” and “it is not” (Chapter 2). Coherence is also symmetric: the denial is coherent too. The whole of Chapter 3 is the attempt to get from thinkable to true.

  2. Grant that the refutation is valid, so that no one can coherently deny the principle. By his own verdict on Aristotle (pp. 60–61), that is not yet access. A refutational proof shows what thought cannot coherently deny. He says Aristotle’s proof of non-contradiction establishes only that much, not that contradiction is impossible in itself. His claim that unreason is different (absolute, not merely anhypothetical) rests on the regress. That regress is the conversion from “for all we know” to “really,” which we’ve already found wanting.

  3. Grant that it’s true and known. Then there is access in his sense: “absolute” means severed, holding whether or not we exist (Chapter 2’s opening). But look at what is accessed:

    • “Necessarily, every entity is contingent” is true in every possible world, so it can’t tell you which world is actual.
    • Add the two figures, and you know that something exists and that it is non-contradictory.
    • He sums up the result himself at the end of Chapter 3: for now we “occupy” a Kantian in-itself. That is Kant’s thinkable thing-in-itself, proved rather than assumed, plus contingency.
    • Nothing about what it is, beyond these formal features, is known.

Mathematics: nothing follows, for a specific reason

The missing step isn’t about modality; it’s about which properties are relations to us.

  • Red can’t exist unperceived because it is a relation to a perceiver (Chapter 1).
  • The correlationist says mathematical properties are relational too: forms of representation, acts of consciousness, a formal language.
  • The Chapter 1 thesis needs the opposite: that mathematical properties are not relations to us.

Contingency is true of relational and non-relational properties alike, so it can’t settle this. Neither can the consistency principle he takes from Hume. Whether “this magnitude exists unthought” is consistent depends on the same question.

The methods that worked elsewhere don’t transfer either:

  • The non-contradiction pattern. A contradictory entity is excluded because it would be necessary. For mathematics he would need the claim that an entity which isn’t mathematically describable would be necessary. Nothing suggests that.
  • The refutational exit. It only works for what the correlationist must presuppose. She must presuppose facticity to refute idealism, but she needn’t presuppose that mathematics describes the in-itself. So mathematics can’t leave the circle by the same door; it has to be derived as a figure, and that derivation is missing.

The closest the figures come to mathematics is determinacy. The non-contradiction derivation ends with the claim that a thing must be this and not that, so that it can become that. Identity and difference are the material of set theory, the route Badiou would take. But “every entity has some determinate structure” is far from “whatever mathematics can describe could exist unthought.” It is further still from “the decay rate belongs to the rock, not to our measuring.” Even the one place the book applies mathematics to the in-itself, the Cantorian non-All in Chapter 4, he labels a supposition awaiting derivation.

Where that leaves the arche-fossil

Grant him everything the book claims to prove. The correlationist’s codicil “for us” then drops off the modal claims: a world without thought is possible, whatever exists is contingent and non-contradictory, and something exists. It stays attached to every quantitative claim, including 4.56 billion years. The arche-fossil’s possibility becomes absolute; its date stays for-us.

It’s doubtful that more of the same method could close this. The figures yield intrinsic determinacy. But which determinations are mathematical rather than relational is a question about representation: what it is for a description to capture a property rather than a relation to us. That’s why his later attempt (“Iteration, Reiteration, Repetition,” 2012; in English in Genealogies of Speculation, 2016) goes through the sign devoid of meaning, the arbitrary, endlessly repeatable mark that formal languages are built from. It is a semantic argument, which puts the unfinished half of his project on the ground your solipsism paper works on.

Your glass houses

The picture is a good test of what the principle delivers. Knowing the walls can go tells Or and Ob nothing about x’s colour. At most it tells them the walls aren’t everything: something is there whether or not the walls are. Your question: two upgrades, two arguments

“Absolute” and “necessary” are different claims, and he argues for them separately.

Absolute means holding independently of thought. You’ve already stated the mechanism. The possibility of my non-being is a possibility of a state with no thinker, and he argues it must therefore also hold with no thinker. A possibility of my not-being that needed my thinking in order to obtain would not be a possibility of my not-being (pp. 56–57). The content fixes the status. This is the step the strong correlationist resists: she accesses the possibility only as a correlate, without claiming it exists only as one.

Necessary means that contingency itself couldn’t fail. His argument is the non-iterability of facticity (pp. 74–75 and 79):

  • Suppose contingency were only a fact, so that it could fail and something could become necessary.
  • What would ground that possibility? Only the absence of any reason for contingency to hold, which is facticity again, one level up.
  • Doubt it at that level and it reappears above.
  • So every attempt to make contingency contingent reapplies it, and it can’t be one fact among others.

The time argument (pp. 61–62) says the same in temporal form: a time that can abolish everything could only be abolished in time, that is, by itself.

The necessity step is the cheap one

In the standard logic of possibility and necessity (S5), this upgrade is almost free, because of two rules:

  • Whatever is possible is necessarily possible. So your possibility without a thinker is already a necessary possibility; nothing has to make it one.
  • Whatever is possibly necessary is necessary. So if some entity could have been necessary, it would actually be necessary. Hence if actually no entity is necessary, necessarily none is.

So the necessity of contingency follows from actual universal contingency alone. Once you’ve granted that everything is really contingent, the book’s subtitle adds nothing further. (This treats his “could” as ordinary metaphysical possibility. Read as a temporal “could come to be,” the logic is less standard, which is what the time argument is meant to cover.)

He doesn’t argue this way, because he wants the necessity to come out of the correlationist’s own doubting. That is where the regress fails. “Maybe contingency is itself only a fact” is an epistemic maybe. Entertaining it needs the concept of possibility, not a real contingency one level up. The regress only starts if each “maybe” is read as a real “could,” which is the same conversion as before.

So there’s no third step to worry about. “Absolute” is the conversion from “for all we know” to “really,” done in the death argument. “Necessary” comes for free once that conversion and the universalization are granted. If those two hold, the necessity of contingency holds. If they fail, the regress can’t rescue it, because it runs on the same conversion.

Beam 1: which “possible”?

Or looks at his situation and finds no reason why he must go on existing. He can say this in two ways:

  • (a) “For all I know, I could cease to be.” This is about his knowledge.
  • (b) “I really can cease to be.” This is about how things are.

The correlationist grants (a), and (a) is all she needs against the idealist prisoner who says “my house is everything, and I cannot cease.” She isn’t trying to prove him wrong, only to show he can’t know he’s right, and for that “you don’t know that” is enough.

Meillassoux’s argument works on (b). If Or says “I really can cease,” that possibility can’t depend on Or thinking it. A possibility of his absence that needed him present would not be a possibility of his absence. So (b), once asserted, holds with no prisoner at all. That’s your “possibility without thinker,” and it’s absolute.

The gap is that Meillassoux claims Or needs (b) to answer the idealist, when he only needs (a). And (a) doesn’t have to hold without Or, because it’s a statement about what Or knows. Meillassoux’s reply is that even thinking (a) requires thinking a real “could.” It doesn’t:

  • For all we know, Goldbach’s conjecture is false. But whichever it is, it couldn’t have been otherwise.
  • In your houses: for all Or knows, x is blue. That doesn’t make x’s colour open; it is whatever it is.

So your sentence is valid only if its first “possible” means (b). The correlationist only ever granted (a).

Beam 2: from me to everything

To get the principle, he reasons: “I found no reason why I must exist. I find no reason why x must have its colour, or why the laws of light must be as they are. Same ground, same result.” That’s beam 2.

It fails because the ground isn’t what made his ceasing real. What made it real was its special content: his absence can’t depend on his presence. “x could be another colour” and “the laws of light could change” contain no absence of Or. So for them, he can stay at “for all I know” without any contradiction. The special argument never reaches them.

A prisoner can even hold both: “I really can cease, and I will, because the glass, the walls and the laws of light are what they are.” That accepts beam 1 and denies beam 2, and it’s consistent. It’s the naturalist’s view: people perish because the laws are what they are.

  • If beam 1 fails, nothing absolute has been found inside the circle, and the correlationist keeps her “for us” everywhere.

  • By agreeing to (a), the correlationist commits to nothing ontological. Yes, on her own understanding. That is her whole position.
  • Meillassoux claims to reveal a hidden ontological commitment under her epistemic claim. Yes, exactly. He says she can only think “for all I know, I could cease” because she already thinks “I really can cease.” The whole dispute is whether that hidden commitment is really there.   • (b) is no different from the apple rotting. No.

Why (b) isn’t the apple

Your apple is almost his own example: a book that could rot from mould, and later a fruit bound to perish. The difference is what the possibility is about.

  • “The apple will rot in a few days.” This is about something inside the world as it appears to us. The correlationist knows it, but only “for us”: it’s objective in her sense (anyone could check), not a claim about the thing in itself.
  • “My thinking could cease” (the correlationist). This is about the frame itself, the relation in which the apple and everything else appears. She can’t know it either way, because she can’t step outside the frame to check. She has only (a).
  • Meillassoux’s (b). This is also about the frame, but it’s claimed as knowledge, and as holding in itself.

So is “I really can cease” knowledge for the correlationist? It depends which “I.” About her body, yes: she knows it will die the way the apple will rot, for us. About the thinking to which the world is given, no.

Treating (b) like the apple is exactly what the correlationist accuses Meillassoux of in Chapter 3. She says he’s handling the frame’s groundlessness as if it were the perishability of a book in the world. His answer is the regress: her ignorance about the frame is only thinkable through a real “could.”

§11

The Goldbach case

Wed 30 Sep · 12:13
Charith
Quoting §10

The Goldbach case says it needn’t be: “for all I know, it’s false” commits you to nothing about what could really be.

What is the Goldbach case?

Claude

Goldbach’s conjecture is a famous statement in number theory: every even number greater than 2 is the sum of two primes. For example, 4 = 2 + 2, 6 = 3 + 3, 8 = 3 + 5, 10 = 5 + 5 and 28 = 11 + 17. Christian Goldbach proposed it in a letter to Euler in 1742, and for nearly three centuries nobody managed to prove or disprove it. Computers have checked it for every even number up to about 4 × 10¹⁸, but checking cases isn’t proof: a single counterexample further out would make it false.

Philosophers use it because a mathematical truth, if true, is necessarily true; there is no possible world where 2 + 2 is 5. So Goldbach’s conjecture is either necessarily true or necessarily false. Whichever it is, it could not really have been the other way.

Yet while it’s unsettled, both of these are correct:

  • “For all we know, it’s true.”
  • “For all we know, it’s false.”

That gives a clean case where “for all I know, it could be otherwise” is right and “it really could be otherwise” is wrong. Philosophers call the first epistemic possibility (what’s open for all we know) and the second metaphysical possibility (what could really be). That’s your epistemological/ontological distinction.

This is how it bears on Meillassoux. His regress claims you can only think “for all I know, things could be otherwise” if you already think “they really can be otherwise.” The Goldbach case shows that’s false in general. A mathematician can say “for all I know, it’s false” while knowing that it couldn’t really go either way, and she isn’t contradicting herself.

It even matches the correlationist’s position in his M1/M2 passage: for all she knows, one thesis is necessary, or the other is, or neither is. As with Goldbach, the openness is in her knowledge, not in the things.

Yes, in shape. Your version is the arche-fossil problem with the subject made anatomical, and Meillassoux uses almost this move himself. But the neural version is easier than his problem in one respect and identical in another.

Where it matches

In Chapter 1’s second rejoinder, the Kantian says the transcendental subject isn’t an object, so it can’t be born in time; only bodies are. Meillassoux replies that the subject has to take place somewhere, as a point of view, so it needs a body. He calls the body the “retro-transcendental” condition of the subject. Bodies emerged in time, so the conditions for there being any subject at all emerged in a time that preceded them.

Your S and Nu are that body. “The relation evolved inside the earth” is his point that the correlate is in time, rather than time in the correlate.

Where it differs

To say “perception depends on S and Nu,” the neuroscientist treats S, Nu and the earth they evolved in as real things, known by science independently of how they appear. That is the realism Meillassoux wants to earn; neuroscience simply assumes it. So inside neuroscience there’s no paradox: the earth existed, brains evolved in it, and brains shape how it appears. It’s the primary/secondary distinction, naturalized. Meillassoux’s task isn’t to show that the earth came before brains (geology does that) but to show that a philosophy can take that statement literally without assuming realism.

The paradox appears only if you make the dependence total, so that brains and evolution are themselves only “for S and Nu.” Then the view refutes itself: the organs that produce the world are part of that world, so the organs would be products of the organs. Nietzsche made exactly this reductio (Beyond Good and Evil, §15), and Schopenhauer ran into the same circle. That’s why the philosophical correlationist insists the subject is not an organ. It’s also why Meillassoux’s reply, that the subject needs a body to take place, is the bridge from her view to yours.

What your flat earth actually shows

A different visual system would change how the earth looks, not what measurement says. We didn’t learn that the earth is round by seeing it; Eratosthenes worked out its circumference from the angles of shadows in two cities. Bees see ultraviolet and bats hear the shape of things, and if either could do geometry, they would get the same radius.

That’s your glass houses again. Comparing systems shows which features depend on the system (colour, depth as experienced) and which survive every change (the measured quantities). What survives is the mathematizable, which is Meillassoux’s Cartesian thesis.

Where the hard part comes back

Measurement and geometry are also done by a nervous system. Neuroscience even has candidates for the part every human shares: place cells and grid cells, which map space (the work behind the 2014 Nobel Prize). A Kantian could say they are space as a form of intuition, implemented in tissue. Then agreement across every nervous system we know shows only invariance across nervous systems like ours, not independence from all of them. It’s the wall every house shares.

So your analogue makes the first half of his problem look easy, but only because neuroscience starts as realism: a world before brains is just biology. The second half it leaves exactly where the book leaves it: is the mathematics the world’s, or ours?

§14

Abstracting the correlation: x → y

Wed 30 Sep · 19:57
Charith
Quoting §13

Yes, the structure doesn’t need a mind. Meillassoux describes correlationism as a form that takes different correlates: consciousness, language, Heidegger’s co-belonging of man and Being. After Kant, he says, philosophers argue over which is the proper correlate. A receiver–world correlation is one more candidate, and on mind–brain identity it’s the natural one: you can’t know the earth except through your brain.

So can we abstract the correlation into just any entity, not just world and receiver, while maintaining the original correlation relation between thinker and world? So it would be like x → y. Then do what Meillassoux tries to do to that relation?

Claude

Yes, and it’s a good way to see what the argument actually uses. But it can’t be just any entity: the argument relies on certain features of x, and if x lacks them there’s no correlation left to work on.

What x must be

For x → y to be a correlation in his sense:

  1. Access. Whatever x can take y to be, it takes y to be as given to x.
  2. Reflexivity. x can represent that relation itself. Otherwise there’s no correlationist, only a relation someone else describes from outside, and then the real correlation is theirs.
  3. Self-absence. x can represent its own non-existence. Otherwise the death step can’t start.
  4. Dependence. Something can hold “for x” only while x exists.

A mind, a community, and arguably an AI with a self-model can fill the x slot. A thermometer can’t, since it fails 2 and 3. Nor can God, who can’t represent his own absence as possible; putting “for God” everywhere is simply the hypostatized correlation, the ancestral witness of Chapter 1.

Running his moves on x → y

Write A(x) for “x exists.” The correlation principle has two readings:

  • Access: whatever x knows, it knows only as holding for x.
  • Existence: whatever obtains, obtains only for x.
  1. The circle. x can say nothing about y except as y-for-x. This is the access reading.
  2. Facticity. x can describe the form of x → y but can’t derive it, so for all x knows, the relation could fail.
  3. The death step. Suppose the existence reading. Everything that obtains, obtains for x, and by condition 4 that requires A(x). So not-A(x) can never obtain, and A(x) is necessary. That’s idealism. So a correlationist who isn’t an idealist must allow that not-A(x) could obtain not-for-x. Meillassoux calls that the absolute.
  4. Universalization. Take any state of y that doesn’t include x’s absence, say “y has property P while x exists.” Holding “that could be otherwise” as merely for-x causes no collapse, because its content doesn’t clash with A(x). Only states that include not-A(x) are forced out of the for-x.
  5. Necessity. This comes free in standard modal logic once 3 and 4 are granted.
  6. Figures and mathematics. The figures (non-contradiction, the “there is”) are formal and concern any entity, so they would carry over if 4 and 5 held. Mathematics needs something about y’s internal structure, and the schema contains nothing about y except that it is given to x.

What the abstract version shows

  • Step 3 is a valid theorem, but against idealism. It proves that the existence reading makes x necessary. The correlationist’s official thesis is the access reading. From it nothing follows except the triviality that x can never witness its own absence. The whole dispute is whether access can stay distinct from existence with only “for all I know, not-A(x) could obtain,” or whether it needs “not-A(x) really can obtain.” That’s beam 1, now in two lines.
  • The reach is exactly y-without-x. With x as me, that’s the world after my death. With x as the whole community of thinkers, it’s the world before or after thinkers. The argument runs identically in both cases, so it never needs other minds; choosing x as the community only borrows them.
  • It’s blind to y. Nothing in the schema says what y is like, so no property of y, mathematical or otherwise, can come out of it. That’s the mathematics gap, now visible in the form of the argument.

One more parameter matters for ancestrality: whether x is itself inside y. If it is (a body, a brain, a species), then y-without-x includes y-before-x, which is the arche-fossil. Kant’s subject isn’t inside y, which is why Meillassoux needs his argument that the subject can only take place through a body.

So the repaired take would be:

  1. The circle, made rigorous, ends at the solipsistic standpoint with agnosticism about anything beyond.
  2. Your semantic argument, aimed at the circle’s own codicil, shows that the codicil adds nothing.
  3. Ancestral statements then mean what they say. The necessity of contingency is neither needed for that nor established by it.

  3. BEA is how his brain appears, and A refers through it to the brain itself, the way every perception refers through appearances to things. There’s no contradiction. But A is now a realist about reference: what he refers to isn’t confined to the horizon.

You wrote that A “is always referring to BEA.” The whole question is whether he refers to BEA or through it. The correlationist says “to”; the realist says “through.”

What your first image adds

The intervention loop: A alters BEA, and his whole experience changes. Of everything in A’s horizon, only BEA behaves like that; moving the fossil doesn’t change the whole horizon. That’s the strongest reason for reading 3: BEA behaves exactly like the appearance of the horizon’s own condition. The correlationist can still add “for A,” but the loop is what her codicil has to explain away.

Notice that the image takes her side in its labels. The outer brain is captioned as a biological model known through experience, and the footnote says the models are representations, not a view from outside experience. A literal reading would label the outer brain simply “A’s brain.”

The link to Meillassoux

BEA is to A’s brain what the isotope is to the earth’s formation: evidence given inside the horizon of something prior to the whole horizon. With the isotope, “prior” means earlier in time; with the brain, it means the condition of all givenness. It’s the same paradox, the “givenness of a being anterior to givenness.”

And mind = brain removes the Kantian escape, which says the conditions of experience aren’t an object. So A faces Meillassoux’s dilemma in the first person: read “my brain” literally, or let the codicil turn neuroscience into a statement about experience alone.

§23

How do we know the true temperature?

Thu 1 Oct · 16:17
Charith
Quoting §22

A thermometer’s reading is produced by the thermometer, yet it tells you the room’s true temperature.

No, forget Meillassoux or any topic we discussed above. How do we know the true temperature? If we can know it directly, then why do we need a thermometer?

Claude

Fair challenge: my sentence took for granted the very thing you’re asking about. We never know the true temperature directly. The only direct access we have is feeling hot or cold, and Locke showed how unreliable that is: put one hand in hot water and the other in cold, then both into the same lukewarm bowl, and it feels warm to one hand and cold to the other. That’s why we need thermometers.

So how can any reading be called true? Not by comparing it with the temperature itself; no one can do that. It’s done by checks of three kinds:

  1. Independent instruments agree. Mercury expansion, gas pressure, electrical resistance, thermocouple voltage and infrared radiation work by unrelated mechanisms. If they all give the same value, a shared error would be a remarkable coincidence.
  2. Fixed points reproduce. Ice melting and water boiling at a given pressure happen at reproducible states, and every instrument has to give the same value there, every time.
  3. Predictions hold. Readings fed into physical laws predict other things: how fast a cup cools, the pressure a sealed gas will reach. Wrong readings would make those predictions fail.

This is how it actually happened. Early thermometers disagreed with one another, and scientists had no master thermometer to settle it. In the 1840s Regnault found that mercury thermometers made with different glass disagreed at high temperatures, while air thermometers agreed with one another, so air became the standard. Hasok Chang’s Inventing Temperature (2004) is a whole book on your question. He calls it the problem of nomic measurement: to calibrate a thermometer you need the law connecting its reading to temperature, and to discover that law you need a calibrated thermometer.

So every check compares readings with readings, and predictions with outcomes; none compares a reading with the temperature itself. What “true temperature” means then depends on what you demand:

  • If it means temperature checked with no instrument at all, nothing could ever pass that test, for any measurement of anything.
  • If it means the value that independent methods converge on and that predictions keep confirming, thermometers pass.

Yes, now I see it, and I agree. Your argument has this shape:

  1. Start in naive realism. A, B, their brains and the world are simply there.
  2. Neuroscience, done inside that realism, finds that every experience is produced by a brain. Call the producing brain B0.
  3. That generates the horizon. Everything A experiences, including the brain he sees (BEA), is B0’s product.
  4. But B0 isn’t BEA. BEA is an item inside the horizon. To mean “the brain that produces my experience,” it has to refer to B0, beyond the horizon, and the horizon says nothing beyond it can be referred to.

So the contradiction isn’t inside the horizon. It’s between the horizon and the premise that produced it. The conclusion takes away the meaning of the premise: if the horizon is real, “B0 produces all experience” has no referent; if that sentence has a referent, the horizon isn’t real. The argument climbs a ladder and then says there was never a ladder.

Your image shows this exactly. The heads at the top are B0, the realist starting point, and the circles are the conclusion. The caption calls the top heads “scientific representations, not a view outside all experience.” That caption is the conclusion swallowing its own premise: the producer has become one more item inside the circle.

What follows

  • Keep the premise. B0 is real, and A refers to it through BEA. Then experience is brain-produced and still reaches beyond itself, so there’s no sealed horizon, only mediation.
  • Keep the horizon. Then it can’t be learned from neuroscience. It has to rest on other grounds, the way Kant’s does, and neuroscience becomes a story told inside it.

Either way, neuroscience can’t establish the horizon. Once the horizon is in place, every neuroscience statement, about neurons, synapses or lesions, becomes a statement about items inside it, including the evidence that produced the horizon. They didn’t live “inside a horizon” in any sense they could feel. The horizon is a conclusion philosophers draw from neuroscience, not a wall anyone runs into. Early humans and other primates lived in what your step 1 calls naive realism, and it worked. Science doesn’t break through a wall they were stuck behind; it extends the same mediated contact with more instruments and mathematics. Tracking an animal from its prints is already inference from present traces.

Here is what evolution does show:

  • Experience guided action well enough to survive. A brain whose experience misrepresented food, predators or distances would have been selected against. So survival is evidence that mediation reaches the world, at least where survival depends on it. Konrad Lorenz made this point against Kant in 1941: the forms of perception are adaptations, a priori for the individual but a posteriori for the species. They fit the world the way a horse’s hoof fits the ground of the steppe.
  • Not harmful isn’t the same as accurate. Selection favours what pays, not truth for its own sake. Colour is the standard example. Colour vision tracks the way surfaces reflect light, and is thought to have helped primates find ripe fruit, but how colours look is shaped by our particular cones and brain. So evolution predicts reliability where it matters and simplification elsewhere, with limits that science can later find, as it did with colour. Hoffman pushes this all the way to “perception hides reality,” but his premises are real organisms under real selection, so he can’t conclude that we know nothing of reality.

So evolution supports neither a sealed horizon nor a perfect window. It supports mediation that works, with limits that can be discovered.

And it repeats your point from last time

The evolutionary story, like neuroscience, starts in realism: real organisms, a real environment, real selection. It can’t end in a sealed horizon without kicking away its own premises. A Kantian can still absorb survival as “success for us,” but then the horizon isn’t coming from biology.

§27

Why would evolution build a horizon?

Thu 1 Oct · 17:28
Charith

But then why did this sort of horizon develop in evolution?

Claude

Evolution didn’t choose a horizon over direct access, because direct access was never an option. Any organism that responds to anything does so through receptors: light becomes nerve signals, pressure becomes nerve signals. Being mediated is simply what it is for a physical system to be sensitive to its surroundings. What evolution built on top of that is a model, and there are good reasons for that.

Why a model rather than raw signals

  • Signals are poor. They’re noisy and incomplete. Each eye has a blind spot where the optic nerve leaves, and you never notice it because the brain fills it in. The brain has to infer what’s out there from partial evidence; Helmholtz called this unconscious inference.
  • Signals are late. Nerve transmission takes time, on the order of a tenth of a second from eye to awareness. To catch a ball or dodge a predator, the brain has to model where things will be, not where they were.
  • There’s too much. The world carries far more information than any nervous system could process, so the brain keeps what matters for action and compresses the rest. Objects, colours and depth are compressions.
  • Models allow rehearsal. Kenneth Craik argued in 1943 that a brain carrying a small-scale model of the world can try out actions in imagination before risking them for real.

Aimed outward, not sealed in

The model is built to track the world, not to hide it. Colour constancy shows this. A banana looks yellow at noon and at dusk, though the light reaching your eye is very different. The brain discounts the changing light to recover a stable property of the banana itself. A cage wouldn’t do that; an instrument would.

So from evolution’s angle the “horizon” is an instrument, judged by how well it guides action. It looks like a horizon only when a philosopher asks what the instrument is like from inside.

A live debate in neuroscience

Predictive-processing theorists split on exactly your question. Jakob Hohwy describes the brain as secluded behind an “evidentiary boundary,” inferring hidden causes it never touches. Andy Clark replies (in “Busting Out,” 2017) that because the model is built for action and constantly corrected by it, the boundary is a bridge, not a veil.

One question evolution doesn’t yet answer is why the model is experienced at all, rather than run without any felt horizon. That’s the hard problem of consciousness, and there’s no agreed answer.
