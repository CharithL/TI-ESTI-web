00The chapter’s own shape

Before the survey begins, Wolfsdorf sets his terms, states what the elenchus is supposed to be, names the problem the whole literature circles, and — unusually for a companion chapter — explains and defends the order he is going to use. Those four moves are the frame everything below hangs on.

Wolfsdorf’s own statement of what he is doing · pp. 34–37

He restricts the subject twice over — to a character, and to a list:

“By ‘Socratic philosophizing’ I understand ‘the manner in which the character Socrates in Plato’s early dialogues engages in philosophia’. (Hereafter, I will refer to the character Socrates in Plato’s Socratic dialogues simply as ‘Socrates’. I will refer to the historical Socrates as ‘the historical Socrates’. By ‘Plato’s early dialogues’ I mean to include: Apology, Charmides, Crito, Euthydemus, Euthyphro, Gorgias, Hippias Major, Hippias Minor, Ion, Laches, Lysis, Republic 1, Protagoras.)”

Wolfsdorf, “Socratic Philosophizing,” p. 34.

The bracket is doing real work. Everything the chapter surveys is a claim about a character in a list of texts, not about a man. Wolfsdorf returns to this at p. 67, where he asks why we should speak of “Socrates’ method” rather than “Plato’s” at all — see §22.

Then the word itself, and the adversarial reading he attributes to Vlastos:

“‘Elenchus’ is a Latinization of the Greek elenchos, which often in its original context and usually in the recent literature on Socrates’ method means ‘refutation’. … Accordingly, the elenchus is conceived as an adversarial approach to claims or to people. Thus Vlastos understood the Socratic elenchus; the word ‘adversarial’ is his.”

p. 34. Wolfsdorf notes in the same parenthesis that he uses the Latin form throughout “despite the fact that a number of the most recent contributions prefer the Greek form” — which is why quotations below from Scott’s 2002 volume read elenchos where his own prose reads elenchus.

The problem, in his own compressed form:

“Indeed, Vlastos himself centrally raises this problem: how can the elenchus refute a thesis? Vlastos calls this ‘the problem of the elenchus’. Most scholarship of the last quarter century has been devoted to solving, dissolving or criticizing attempts to solve or dissolve the problem of the elenchus.”

p. 35.

And the defence of chronological order — the sentence that makes this chapter usable as an anthology spine rather than a topic list:

“My discussion of the interpretations of Socrates’ method of philosophizing will proceed chronologically, from Vlastos (1983a) to Santana (2009). In this respect, the organization of the discussion in this chapter differs from the topical organizations of the other chapters in this companion. My justification for proceeding in chronological order is that this approach preserves the integrity of the contributions as well as illustrates their dialectical engagement within this vein of scholarship.”

p. 36.

I

The opening exchange

A thesis, a reply printed beside it, a concession printed beside that, and two critiques in the next two volumes. The whole shape of the subsequent literature is set in thirty‑six months.

Vlastos states the five‑step schema, names the problem, and proposes assumptions (A) and (B) yielding (C). Kraut, in the same volume, denies there is a problem. Vlastos, four pages later in the same volume, concedes the point for every dialogue but the Gorgias. Brickhouse and Smith then show that (A) and (B) do not in fact yield (C); Polansky shows that (C) would not cover the premises Socrates actually uses.

Read §01 with its provenance warning in view: the essay that starts all this is the one text in this anthology that cannot be quoted from itself.

Wolfsdorf’s account · pp. 37–38

Wolfsdorf gives Vlastos’s definition and then the five‑step schema:

“Vlastos himself defines Socratic elenchus as ‘a search for moral truth by adversary argument in which a thesis is debated only if asserted as the answerer’s own belief and is regarded as refuted only if the negation of his thesis is deduced from his own beliefs’ (1983a: 30).”

Wolfsdorf p. 37, quoting Vlastos 1983a p. 30.

Socrates’ interlocutor asserts a thesis p
… which Socrates considers false and targets for refutation.
Socrates secures agreement to a premise set Q
Q includes one or more premises q, r and so on, relevant to p.
Argument is from Q, not to it
The premises are never themselves argued for inside the elenchus.
Socrates argues and the interlocutor agrees that Q entails not‑p
Socrates claims that not‑p has been proven true, and thus p false
This is the step that generates the problem. Wolfsdorf, p. 37, citing Vlastos 1983a p. 39.

On step (1) Wolfsdorf stresses the “existential dimension”: “elenchus has a double objective: to discover how every human being ought to live and to test the single human being that is doing the answering – to find out if he is living as one ought to live” (Vlastos 1983a: 37, quoted at Wolfsdorf p. 37). On step (5) he identifies the crucial text: Gorgias 479e, “Has it not been proved (apodédeiktai) that what was asserted [by myself] is true?” — adding, “More generally, as we will see, the main evidence for Vlastos’s thesis derives from Gorgias” (p. 37).

He then works the Charmides case — sound‑mindedness is restraint (p); sound‑mindedness is always fine (q); restraint is not always fine (r); therefore not‑p — and puts the objection: the discussants could reject q or r instead. “Thus, what the exchange reveals is simply that Q and p are inconsistent, not that p is false rather than Q or some component of Q” (p. 37).

And then the problem, in Vlastos’s own words as Wolfsdorf prints them:

“The problem of the Socratic elenchus: how is it that Socrates claims to have proved a thesis false when, in point of logic, all he has proved in any given argument is that the thesis is inconsistent with the conjunction of agreed upon premises for which no reason has been given in that argument?”

Vlastos 1983a p. 49, quoted at Wolfsdorf p. 38.

The solution, in three steps, with Wolfsdorf’s page references to the 1983a text:

AAnyone who ever has a false moral belief will always have at the same time true beliefs entailing the negation of that false belief. 1983a: 52
BSocrates’ set of moral beliefs is consistent. 1983a: 55
CSocrates’ set of moral beliefs consists exclusively of true beliefs. 1983a: 55 — entailed by (A) and (B).

Wolfsdorf adds two things about (A) that matter later. Socrates never argues for it — his reason for holding it is that it “proves true in his own experience” (1983a: 53); the evidence is inductive. And (C) is what licenses step (5): “Since Socrates assents to the premise set Q from which not‑p is deduced, Q must consist of true premises” (p. 38).

Finally, the appendix: in Euthydemus, Lysis and Hippias Major — late among the early dialogues — “Socrates abandons adversarial argumentation and thus the elenchus. Instead, Socrates both proposes and criticizes his own theses. Vlastos conjectures that at this point in his career Plato had lost faith in the elenchus” (p. 38).

Route 1 — Vlastos 1983a quoted verbatim by others

“What Socrates … does in any given elenchus is convict p not of falsehood but of being a member of an inconsistent premiss‑set; and to do this is not to show that p is false, but only that either p is false or that some or all of the premisses are false. The question then becomes how Socrates can claim … to have proved that the refutand is false, when all he has established is the inconsistency of p with premisses whose truth he has not undertaken to establish in that argument: they have entered that argument simply as propositions on which he and the interlocutor are agreed. This is the problem of the Socratic elenchus …”

Vlastos 1983a, 30 — quoted in full as the opening block of Hugh H. Benson, “The Problem of the Elenchus Reconsidered,” Ancient Philosophy 7 (1987), 67.

This is the single most‑quoted paragraph in the literature and it is recoverable exactly, because Benson reproduces it as an epigraph before beginning his own argument. It is also the version Benson quotes back at the end of his 1995 paper (p. 112), there with the concordance “1983a: 30 ~ Socratic Studies, 3.”

“A — Everyone’s moral beliefs always include a subset of true beliefs which entail the negation of each of his false moral beliefs.”

Vlastos’s assumption (A) as Richard Kraut prints it, “Comments on Gregory Vlastos, ‘The Socratic Elenchus’,” OSAP 1 (1983), 67–68.

Kraut’s formulation and Wolfsdorf’s (“Anyone who ever has a false moral belief …”) are the same proposition in different words — both are reports of the same 1983a text.

“(A) Anyone who ever has a false moral belief will always have at the same time true beliefs entailing the negation of that false belief, and
(B) The set of moral beliefs held by Socrates at any given time is consistent,
which according to Vlastos entail
(C) The set of moral beliefs held by Socrates at any given time is true.”

Vlastos’s three assumptions as Benson prints them, with the note “‘The Socratic Elenchus’, 52–5 … and ‘Elenchus and Mathematics’, 369” — Benson, “The Dissolution of the Problem of the Elenchus,” OSAP 13 (1995), 48.

Benson adds the adjectives Vlastos used of his own assumptions: “two ‘tremendous’ and ‘exceedingly bold’ assumptions.” Note the wording of (B) here — the set of moral beliefs — and compare Route 2 below, where the 1994 revision narrows it.

“regardless of which conceded premisses they [his interlocutors] choose to retract, there will always be others in their belief system which entail the Socratic thesis”

Vlastos 1983a, 52 (Vlastos’s own emphasis) — quoted twice by Kraut, at pp. 65 and 67, and made the target of his central objection.

“Without some contra‑endoxic premisses how could he [Socrates] hope to get contra‑endoxic conclusions?”

Vlastos, “in the longer version of his paper,” 43 n. 41 — quoted by Kraut, p. 63.

This is the claim Polansky attacks in 1985 (§05): that Socrates could not have reached contra‑endoxic conclusions from endoxic premises.

“The [premise set Q] from which Socrates deduces the negation of the opponent’s thesis are logically unsecured within the argument: no reason has been given to compel agreement to them”

Vlastos 1983a, 40 — quoted at Wolfsdorf p. 37.

“[Socrates] never troubles to say why his way of searching is the way to discover truth or even to say what this way of searching is. He has no name for it. Elenchos and the cognate verb elenchein (to refute, to examine critically, to censure) he uses to describe, not to baptize, what he does; only in modern times has ‘elenchus’ become a proper name.”

Vlastos 1983a, 28 — quoted by Gary Alan Scott, Introduction to Does Socrates Have a Method? (2002), 5 n. 5. Full context at §11.

The concession that gives the deflationists their opening: on Vlastos’s own account the term is a modern proper name for something Socrates never named. Brickhouse and Smith build their 2002 chapter on exactly this (§16).

“if that were all Socrates had expected of the elenchus — exposure of inconsistencies in his interlocutors — where did he find positive support for those strong doctrines of his on whose truth he based his life? If the elenchus, his only line of argument, gave those doctrines no rational grounding, what did? … I could not reconcile myself to Grote’s missionary of the examined life, who was a dogmatist himself.”

Vlastos 1983a, 46 — quoted by Benson 1995, 109 (Benson’s emphasis). Full context at §09.

The motive for the problem, as distinct from its statement. Vlastos wants the elenchus to be constructive because otherwise Socrates is a dogmatist. Benson quotes it in order to attack the motive.

Route 2 — the 1994 revision, Socratic Studies ch. 1 (held in full)

Vlastos revised the 1983 essay and its Afterthoughts together as ch. 1 of Socratic Studies (Cambridge, 1994), where his own headnote reads: “Duly revised, it appeared under the title ‘The Socratic Elenchus’, in Oxford Studies in Ancient Philosophy 1 (1983), 27–58 and 71–4.” Every quotation in this block is from the 1994 text with 1994 pagination, and none of it should be cited as 1983a.

“Socratic elenchus is a search for moral truth by question‑and‑answer adversary argument in which a thesis is debated only if asserted as the answerer’s own belief and is regarded as refuted only if its negation is deduced from his own beliefs.”

Socratic Studies, 4.

Compare the 1983a wording at Wolfsdorf p. 37: “a search for moral truth by adversary argument …”. The revision inserts “question‑and‑answer” and replaces “the negation of his thesis” with “its negation.”

“This brings us smack up against what I had called earlier on ‘the problem of the elenchus’: how is it that Socrates claims to have proved a thesis false when, in point of logic, all he has proved is that the thesis is inconsistent with the conjunction of agreed‑upon premises for which no reason has been given in that argument? Could he be blind to the fact that logic does not warrant that claim?”

Socratic Studies, 21.

“I see the inconsistency in what I have conceded, and I must do something to clean up the mess. But I don’t have to do it your way. I don’t have to concede that p is false. I have other options. For example, I could decide that p is true and q false. Nothing you have proved denies me this alternative.”

Socratic Studies, 21 — the speech Vlastos writes for Polus, “if he had had his wits about him.”

This is the objection in its most vivid form, put by Vlastos into the mouth of the interlocutor. Everything from §02 onward is an answer to it.

“Surely Socrates would be aware of this ever present possibility. Why then is he not worried by it? Because, I submit, he believes that if that wrong choice were made he would have the resources to recoup that loss in a further elenchus.”

Socratic Studies, 22 (Vlastos’s italics).

“Socrates then is making a tremendous assumption. Stated in fullest generality, it comes to this: [A] Whoever has a false moral belief will always have at the same time true beliefs entailing the negation of that false belief.”

Socratic Studies, 25.

“If this is what Socrates assumes, why does he not argue for it? Because it is a meta‑elenctic statement. To support it Socrates would have to engage in meta‑elenctic inquiry. And this … Socrates never does in Plato’s earlier dialogues. In every one of them prior to the Meno Socrates maintains epistemological innocence, methodological naivety.”

Socratic Studies, 25.

The footnote to this sentence (n. 65) is the sharpest thing Vlastos ever wrote against his own reconstruction: “[A] is not a premise in any elenctic argument, nor does Socrates suggest that it is. The remarks from which I have teased it out are obiter dicta. The interlocutor would be perfectly justified if he ignored them as pure Socratic bluster: he has been given no reason why he should think them true.”

“So he has this purely inductive evidence for a part of [A] — for all of it except the claim in [A] that the beliefs from which he deduces the negation of his interlocutors’ theses are true. For this he would have to fall back on nothing better than the pragmatic value of those beliefs: they articulate intuitions which prove practically viable in his own experience; they tell him who is happy and who isn’t; he does what they tell him and he is happy.”

Socratic Studies, 26.

“[B] The set of elenctically tested moral beliefs held by Socrates at any given time is consistent. Socrates would not wish to say, and never does say, that he knows that all of his moral beliefs are mutually consistent.”

Socratic Studies, 28.

This is a substantive revision, not a rewording. Wolfsdorf’s (B) — and Benson’s, quoting 1983a: 55 — is “the set of moral beliefs held by Socrates”. The 1994 (B) is restricted to the elenctically tested ones and is explicitly downgraded from something known to something believed and open to challenge. If you are assessing the strength of (B), it matters a great deal which text you are assessing.

“The answer is in assumptions [A] and [B] whose conjunction … entails that Socrates’ belief‑set consists entirely of true beliefs, from which it follows that q and r are true: to show that not‑p follows from premises which are true, is to prove not‑p true.”

Socratic Studies, 28.

“At this point I am much indebted to helpful criticism from Brickhouse and Smith (1984: 187–92). They convinced me that the contrary view I had expressed in the earlier version of this paper was wrong, and I withdrew it in my next publication.”

Socratic Studies, 26 n. 66.

The concession recorded in the revision itself. What was withdrawn is the claim that the inductive evidence supports the whole of (A), including the truth of the premises — see §04. It is not a withdrawal of (A).

“That wildest of Plato’s metaphysical flights, that ultra‑speculative theory that all learning is ‘recollection’, is understandable as, among other things, an answer to a problem in Socratic elenchus.”

Socratic Studies, 29 — the closing sentence of ch. 1 before the appendix on the demise of the elenchus.

Wolfsdorf’s account · pp. 38–40

“The first response to Vlastos (1983a), by Richard Kraut, was published in the same volume…. Kraut begins by praising Vlastos’s contribution as surpassing ‘every other discussion this topic has received’ (1983: 59). After this, Kraut criticizes Vlastos’s account of the problem of the elenchus and his proposed solution to it.” Wolfsdorf sets out the three points as Kraut himself numbers them at p. 59:

  1. The elenchus, as Vlastos describes it, “can provide proof without Socrates’ commitment to (A) and (B).”
  2. “Socrates does not rely on (A) and (B) to reach the conclusion that all his moral beliefs are true (C).”
  3. “In fact, Socrates does not think that all his moral beliefs are consistent (B).”

On (1): “Kraut emphasizes that any argument must contain premises for which no argument is given. In that case, the reason Socrates thinks his arguments are proofs is simply that he thinks his premises are true.” On (2): it would be “astonishing and arrogant” for Socrates to believe he could always find new resources; and “(A) presupposes that Socrates has sorted out true from false moral beliefs.” On (3): Socrates’ professions of perplexity are “not, as many have claimed, ironic, but sincere.”

Wolfsdorf’s summary verdict, p. 40: “In short, as Kraut sees it, there is no problem of the elenchus; thus, there is no need to think – and indeed no compelling evidence for thinking – that Socrates has a commitment to (A) and (B) and so (C) that resolves the problem.”

Kraut in his own words

“I think that Professor Vlastos’s paper on the Socratic elenchus surpasses every other discussion this topic has received, and in many cases I believe that his observations are correct and his arguments decisive. But I want to raise some questions about what he calls ‘the problem of the elenchus’, and about his attempt to solve it. … I am not convinced of one major thesis that Professor Vlastos propounds: that the elenchus is too weak an instrument to provide proof, unless it is supplemented by the two methodological assumptions, labelled A and B, which can allegedly be found in the Gorgias.”

Kraut 1983, 59 — the opening paragraph.

“I suggest that this attempt to raise a problem about the elenchus rests on a mistake. Granted, whenever someone claims to have a proof, then the statements that constitute his argument must be true. But it is not necessary that he give or even have reasons for the premisses used in his argument. As Aristotle realized, if arguments contain a finite number of steps and if circularity is to be avoided, then every demonstration will contain statements for which no argument is given.”

Kraut 1983, 62.

This is the sentence Brickhouse and Smith go back to in 2002 (§16) when they announce that Kraut was right all along. It is also the sentence Santana in 2009 (§21) treats as generating the infinite regress that only a properly interpreted (EA) can avoid.

“So, if we want to know why Socrates thinks he has proofs, the answer is simple: he thinks that his premisses are true, and he thinks that those premisses force us to a certain conclusion. If we want to know why Socrates thinks those premisses true, he may have no answer to our question, but that is no fault of his. One can’t always give a reason for everything one believes, and this fact does not deprive one of proof.”

Kraut 1983, 62.

“I think the weak point in this interpretation lies in its a priori assertion that a surprising and unorthodox conclusion cannot be derived from a premiss‑set that consists of nothing but humdrum, orthodox truths. Such derivations are what philosophers often search for, and sometimes they succeed. Think of Arrow’s paradox, for example … Why couldn’t Socrates’ arguments have this same feature? Surely, the only way to tell whether they do is to look — we can’t decide the matter a priori.”

Kraut 1983, 63.

Kraut’s answer to “without some contra‑endoxic premisses how could he hope to get contra‑endoxic conclusions?” Polansky presses the same point two years later from the other direction — “Quite unusual conclusions, surely, may derive from most ordinary premises” (§05).

“He makes no appeal to self‑evidence or the common consensus, but he does begin his arguments with propositions so eminently reasonable that they are as yet in no need of justification. They will come to need justification only if Socrates or one of his interlocutors discovers a good reason for challenging their status as starting points.”

Kraut 1983, 65 (Kraut’s italics on as yet).

This is the formula Wolfsdorf later adopts as his own position in 2003 (§17). Note that the words are Kraut’s here, but the phrase “picks premisses which he considers so eminently reasonable” that Wolfsdorf quotes in 2003 is Vlastos’s, from the Afterthoughts (§03) — written four pages later, in reply to this paragraph.

“I find that an astonishing claim for Socrates to make, and I think that the text does not support this interpretation. It is astonishing because however arrogant Socrates was he must have realized that he had only a finite number of arguments for his conclusions. At some point, he is going to run out of arguments, and therefore he cannot seriously believe that no matter how often his interlocutor demands that he start all over again from new premisses, he will be able to find a new argument.”

Kraut 1983, 65 — on Vlastos 1983a p. 52.

Vlastos answers this precise objection in Socratic Studies n. 64: “Both Socrates and his opponent are finite creatures and sooner or later each of them will run out of steam. What Socrates is claiming is that his resources will always match those of his interlocutor. Anything impossible about that?”

“Socrates is assuming a certain amount of psychological and moral fixity in all of his interlocutors: they are all compelled to live with the premisses he uses in his arguments against them. … I prefer to interpret him to be making the less arrogant claim that, given a certain fixity in human beliefs, he can always find contradictions in his opponents.”

Kraut 1983, 67.

Kraut’s replacement for (A): an empirical claim about how rigid people’s moral outlooks are, in place of a universal claim about the structure of belief.

“So assumption A itself presupposes that Socrates has sorted out the true from the false: whatever confidence he has in his ability to recognize which beliefs are true is therefore independent of and prior to his belief in A.”

Kraut 1983, 68.

The circularity charge: (A) cannot be the ground of Socrates’ confidence in his moral beliefs, because the inductive evidence for (A) already requires that confidence.

“My conclusion, then, is that consistency is not quite so important for Socrates as Professor Vlastos thinks it is. He strives for it, of course, but he does not think he has achieved it, and he claims to have proofs in spite of this failure. If my interpretation is correct, then Socrates neither has nor needs an answer to the absurdly general question, ‘What makes you think that your ethical views are correct?’ … By its nature, all argument and all proof rests on assumptions open to challenge, and no argument loses its force merely because the speaker has, somewhere or other, inconsistent beliefs.”

Kraut 1983, 70 — the closing paragraph.

One disagreement worth recording

Kraut’s third piece of evidence against (B) is Gorgias 527d, where Socrates tells Callicles that both of them “never think the same about the same matters.” Kraut, p. 69: “This is as clear a confession of inconsistency as we could want, and I see no reason to ignore or belittle it.”

Vlastos’s 1994 reply, n. 68, is blunt: “The irony at G. 527D should be transparent: Callicles had been convicted of numerous inconsistencies, Socrates of not even one. “Brickhouse and Smith’s argument is brief, but taut and subtle.” Wolfsdorf gives the strategy first: Vlastos needs (C) to guarantee that the moral propositions of Q are true; but “Socrates has no means of assuring and no good reason to believe that his moral belief‑set is wholly true.” Worse, “it is not merely moral propositions that serve in Q to entail not‑p” — so the solution would require the consistency and truth of all Socrates’ relevant beliefs, moral and non‑moral.

The argument then runs in two movements.

Against the justification of (A). “(A) concerns true beliefs, but the elenchus can only reveal inconsistency. Thus, Socrates would have no means by which to infer whether any particular one of his interlocutor’s beliefs was true.” Wolfsdorf notes that Kraut makes the same point at 1983 n. 4, and adds a correction of his own: their discussion at 188–90 “is slightly confusing insofar as it also incorporates the claim that Socrates could have no justification for (B) as well as (A). Nothing in their argument to this point tells against (B).”

Against the entailment of (C). This is the technical heart. “(A) merely states that if one has a false moral belief, then one will also have true beliefs – not necessarily moral ones – that entail the negation of that false moral belief.” And a belief‑set can be consistent while containing both true and false beliefs. Their example, as Wolfsdorf reports it: {‘Socrates is married to Xanthippe’, ‘Socrates can fly’}. So Socrates could have a consistent moral belief‑set one of whose members is false, while his moral‑plus‑non‑moral set is inconsistent. (A) and (B) do not yield (C).

Two repairs are tried and both fail:

A1Every person’s belief‑set always includes a subset of true moral beliefs that entails the negation of that person’s false moral beliefs. — Refuted by the texts: “Socrates sometimes uses … non‑moral propositions … to gain his elenctic conclusions” (191).
A2For any person with a false moral belief, that person’s belief‑set includes true moral beliefs that, in conjunction with relevant true non‑moral beliefs, entail the negation of the false belief. — Still fails to yield (C): the true moral subset may be consistent with the false belief, since it only entails its negation with non‑moral help.

The same for (B): (B1) “All of Socrates’ beliefs – not merely his moral beliefs – are consistent” is rejected because “there is no reason to suppose that Socrates, even on Vlastos’s view, subjects all of his non‑moral beliefs to the sort of rigorous scrutiny” he gives his moral ones (192). (B2), restricted to beliefs he might use elenctically, lacks textual support: Gorgias 482a5–b1 shows Socrates “always maintains the same beliefs” (193) but it would be an “extremely unrealistic overestimation” of the method to think it had produced a fully consistent set (193).

And the constructive residue, which Brickhouse and Smith themselves pick up seven years later: an adequate account must “allow us to make sense of [the elenchus] as a method [of moral inquiry that] Plato or Socrates would prescribe” (194). Wolfsdorf’s comment: “On Vlastos’s view, which requires (C), the elenchus is in fact not a method that even Socrates can use.”

Two related criticisms. The first defends the endoxic reading of Q that Vlastos had ruled out; the second attacks (A), (B) and (C) directly.

Part one: the premises may be endoxa after all

“Polansky suggests that ‘none of Vlastos’s [three] arguments aiming to prove that Socrates cannot rely exclusively upon endoxic premises is effective’ (1985: 249).” Wolfsdorf takes the three in order.

  • Vlastos’s first argument — Socrates never says Q consists of endoxa. Polansky: we should not expect “to find Socrates pointing out to the interlocutor that the proposed premises are generally accepted” (249). Since Socrates wants the interlocutor to say what he believes, it “would be counterproductive for Socrates to proclaim that the premises are common beliefs” (249).
  • Vlastos’s second argument — Socrates rejects Polus’ view at Grg. 472b–c and 474a–b that everybody disbelieves him. Polansky: “Polus is rejecting Socrates’ conclusion, not his premises.”
  • Vlastos’s third argument — contra‑endoxic conclusions cannot come from endoxic premises. Polansky: “Quite unusual conclusions, surely, may derive from most ordinary premises” (251) — and the refutation of Polus is itself an example.

Beyond the three, Polansky charges Vlastos with an “impoverished” conception: “Endoxic views are not equivalent to conventional views. Endoxic views are the views of the wise and such views may be resisted by the ‘mass of men’ (252).” And he re‑reads the Republic I argument (335b–c) that Vlastos had taken as non‑endoxic. Wolfsdorf adds the Xenophontic corroboration Polansky invokes: Mem. 4.6.15, “Whenever Socrates himself argued something out he proceeded from the most generally accepted opinions, believing that security in argument lies therein.”

Part two: three problems with (C)

(C) does not cover the premises Socrates actually uses
“There are many cases in the dialogues in which Socrates appeals to non‑moral premises” (256). Polansky distinguishes his point from Brickhouse and Smith’s “more difficult” one: “I am making the very straightforward point that (C) will not accomplish what Vlastos thinks it will, that is, guarantee for Socrates the truth of his premises” (n. 14). And Vlastos cannot simply widen (C), because he would then have to abandon his view that Socrates is “solely a moral philosopher.”
(C) already covers what was to be shown
If Socrates believes Q and Q entails not‑p, what new moral belief is he acquiring? The elenchus looks less like inquiry than like “a means … to convey the results of [Socrates’ prior] research to his interlocutors” (257) — a Socrates “confirming his moral doctrines to others rather than developing his moral doctrines for himself” (257).
(C) is nearly irrelevant to what single elenchi achieve
Socrates’ central moral tenets are “never established by a single elenctic argument and could not be so established” (258) — caring for the soul over the body, virtue as knowledge, the wisest man being aware of his ignorance. Single elenchi demonstrate only not‑p. Must we then hold “that Socrates’ … set of moral beliefs prominently includes numerous [negative] views … ?” (258).

Everyone in Part One accepted Vlastos’s second premise — that Socrates claims to have refuted the thesis — and argued about the first. Benson accepts the first and attacks the second.

Wolfsdorf frames the paper by the distinction it introduces. “On the constructivist view, endorsed by Vlastos, the elenchus demonstrates as well as persuades the interlocutor that p is false. … On the non‑constructivist view, which Benson endorses, the elenchus demonstrates and persuades the interlocutor merely that Q and p are inconsistent.”

He notes the demonstration/persuasion split Benson uses: “Benson characterizes the aim of demonstrating not‑p as ‘impersonal’ and persuading an interlocutor that not‑p as ‘personal’.” And he simplifies Benson’s formal structure — “more complex than Vlastos’s (1987: 69)” — by writing K for the conjunction of p and Q, so that both sides agree K is false and disagree over whether all but one conjunct is “true or … has some other property that can plausibly be seen to be associated with truth” (69).

The two constraints, in Wolfsdorf’s summary:

AVAvailability. The interlocutor must understand the argument from Q to not‑p — because “the goal of the elenchus is to persuade the interlocutor of his ignorance as a necessary first step in the attainment of knowledge” (75). Evidence: Men. 84a3–c6, Sph. 230c3–d4, Grg. 471d3–472c4.

The two‑premise analysis is the pivot of the whole subsequent debate. Almost everyone in Part One argued about the first premise. Benson grants it and goes after the second.

“First, it should be noticed that it is a conditional thesis. The position is only that if Vlastos is right concerning ‘the problem of the elenchus’, then it cannot be solved. I point this out because while the first premise upon which ‘the problem’ rests may be uncontroversial, the second is not. 1987 announces the dissolution but does not yet argue for it. The argument against the second premise is the work of 1995 (§09).

“I want to suggest that the evidence on behalf of the theoretical constraints is sufficiently strong that in such a case we will simply have to face the fact that Socrates takes the results of his elenchi to be stronger than his own constraints will allow.”

Worth marking. On Benson’s taxonomy Kraut and Vlastos are on the same side — both hold the elenchus can prove falsity. Their quarrel is only over what licenses it. Wolfsdorf’s topical map (§00) sorts them the same way.

“The constructivist holds that all but one of the conjuncts at (5), usually t and u, either are true or have some other property which can plausibly be seen to be associated with truth and so Socrates can conclude that the remaining proposition, usually p, is false. Original text

Wolfsdorf’s account · pp. 46–49 — the primary register for this section

This is the constructive answer to their own 1984 demand for “a method … Plato or Socrates would prescribe.” The central move: “the elenchus tests not merely propositions but human lives.”

Socrates sees himself “as doing something more than just attempting to display the falsehood of his interlocutors’ propositions … Through the elenchus Socrates examines the manner in which his interlocutors live” (1991: 135).

Quoted at Wolfsdorf pp. 46–47.

Two things connect propositions to lives. First, the say‑what‑you‑believe rule: “Only if the interlocutors answer his questions with their sincerely held beliefs can Socrates be confident that he is really testing at least some aspect of how they think they should live” (137). Second, intellectualism: agents never act against what they believe is best, so “one will always act in such a way as to follow one’s beliefs about how it is best for one to live” (140–1).

The functions, as Wolfsdorf separates them

Destructive
To destroy the false conceit of moral knowledge. “Thus, if he is to make progress, Socrates must first attack the beliefs that hinder inquiry, thereby inducing in the interlocutor an openness to reconsideration of what he thought he already knew” (140).
Constructive within the destruction
Exposure of inconsistency shows the interlocutor that his pursuit of the good life “is likely to be or has actually been in some substantial way self‑defeating” (141). Refutations are “lessons in and provocations toward self‑knowledge.”
Sorting by depth of commitment
The elenchus reveals that some beliefs are held more deeply than others; the interlocutor jettisons the less firmly held. This is only safe if the deepest beliefs are the right ones — it must be “a feature of the ‘examined life’ not just that we clarify what we really believe, but that in doing so we invariably come to see that we believe what is right, and not what is wrong” (144).
Generating and defending moral propositions
“to the extent that he has generated inductive evidence through previous elenctic examinations for the necessity of his own view concerning a coherent [life], Socrates can claim to have established a truth applicable to all men” (146). Hence “another use to which Socrates may put the elenchus is to generate and to defend moral propositions, for he has reason to think that those he generates and defends in this way are of value to everyone” (148).
Hortative
Not merely to change belief but to change action — getting the jurors to acquit, getting Euthyphro to desist. “It follows that the elenchus is a tool for normative persuasion intended to make a real difference in the actions people undertake … Thus, it is not merely intended to further one’s understanding of moral concepts” (156). Shame does the work: Socrates “hopes to shame his interlocutors into positive action” (153).

Wolfsdorf notes the divine supplement: the Delphic oracle and the divine sign both support the truth of Socrates’ moral beliefs and mark the limits of his wisdom — “Socrates has, as they put it, good moral judgement, but not wisdom” (151). And that they do not accept Benson’s strict (PD) (n. 25).

Their conclusion, quoted at Wolfsdorf pp. 48–49:

“… On our account of the elenchus, moral philosophy for Socrates is not a matter of demonstrating which propositions in the moral sphere are true and which false. Rather, it is a rich and complex enterprise in which one must purge others of their pretence of wisdom, undertake to determine what kinds of things people must believe about how to live if their lives are to be happy, test and refine definitions of the virtues, deliberate about right action, and when the nature of right and wrong action is clear enough, exhort others to pursue what is right and shun what is wrong.” (159)

Quoted at Wolfsdorf p. 48; compare Brickhouse and Smith 1994a: 3–29.

Quoted at Wolfsdorf p. 51.

Four positions in one book, and they do not line up on a single axis. Tarrant says the word is wrong. Carpenter and Polansky say the logic is not general. Wolfsdorf’s account · pp. 58–60 — the only register for the surveyed paper

The paper is a revival. “In his 1941 Plato’s Earlier Dialectic, Richard Robinson proposes that Socrates refutes his interlocutor by showing that his thesis p is self‑contradictory (1953: 28…). In his 1983a article Vlastos mentions Robinson’s position only to reject it as an earlier misguided attempt to explain the Socratic elenchus (1983a: 29…). Three theses, in Forster’s numbering: that “at least sometimes [Socrates understands] his refutations as attempts to reduce a thesis to a self‑contradiction without the aid of independent premises” (9); that this conception is “reasonable [in a] significant number” of cases (9); and that “it is not implausible of [Socrates] to suppose that theses of the sort those refutations attack are in fact self‑contradictory” (9).

The prima facie case. Phaedo 101d–e, where Socrates examines a hypothesis to see whether the propositions that “rushed forth from it” agree (10); Euthyphro 11b, where the theses will not stand still and Socrates compares them to the self‑moving statues of Daedalus — the analogy of moving “by themselves” suggesting self‑contradiction (10–11); and Zeno of Elea’s influence on the young historical Socrates (11–13).

Three problems, and the solution. (i) None of the early elenchi actually works by exposing self‑contradiction (15). (ii) Socrates uses “a multiplicity of premises in order to generate a logical difficulty” (15) and knows it — Grg. 498e–499b, Prt. 332d, Chrm. 164c–d, 165a–b. (iii) The conception is implausible anyway. Forster’s answer to (i) and (ii) turns on “certain implicit assumptions about meaning” that are “alien to us” (14): the premises are “internal to the sense of the thesis” (16).

“the sense of any given belief statement that someone utters is in part constituted by, and hence only determinable by investigation, a network of related beliefs which he has … [And] cross‑examinations of the sort Socrates performs have the function of uncovering this network” (23). And: “this network of sense‑internal beliefs … is quite broad (somewhat broader than the semantic intuitions of most modern philosophers would be likely to allow)” (23).

Quoted at Wolfsdorf p. 59.

Sense‑internality is not analytic implication: it concerns the “speaker‑meaning” of the interlocutor’s claims, not the “meaning‑in‑the‑language” (52) — which is why Socrates sometimes asks what the speaker means by p (Euthphr. 7a, Hp. Mi. 369d, at 16–17).

The payoff for this debate, in Wolfsdorf’s words: “An additional virtue of Forster’s interpretation is that it provides a solution to Vlastos’s problem of the elenchus. Socrates is able to refute his interlocutor’s theses precisely because he exposes them as self‑contradictory (29).” Forster’s rejection of Benson’s dissolution is at 25–9.

The third problem is answered from Wittgenstein and from empirical work on ordinary language: analyticity/syntheticity, meaningfulness/meaninglessness, synonymy/non‑synonymy are “more vague and mutable than modern philosophers of language typically admit” (32); Socrates draws the analytic/synthetic distinction “in a different place from us” (33), and there is no “good reason to assume in advance that” ours is better (34). Add the “highly unstable state of ethical thought in fifth‑century Athens” (35) and Socrates had reason to expect his interlocutors’ beliefs to be contradictory.

And the unresolved tension Forster himself concedes. The principle of univocity — “whenever a single general term is involved there is just a single meaning involved as well” (48) — is at odds with the focus on speaker‑meaning, since univocity “entails uniformity in the meaning attached to [a general term] by different individuals” (54). Forster: “it is not altogether clear whether Plato’s Socrates perceives this tension between or has a way of reconciling these two opposing sides of his position” (54).

Wolfsdorf calls it “McPherran’s important paper” and notes the gap it fills: Aristotle at Metaphysics 1078b7–32 names epagogic argument as one of two distinguishing features of Socratic philosophizing; Plato and Xenophon “target epagōgē as an innovative, distinguishing mark of Socratic methodology” (348); earlier accounts — Robinson, Gulley, Santas, Vlastos — conflict; and “no article prior to McPherran’s is devoted to the subject.”

The paper begins from Robinson’s classification of three types of epagōgē, which Wolfsdorf sets out, with a warning about symbols:

TypeMovementExample
1Premise set → a proposition superordinate to itEvery opposite has only one opposite (Prt. 332c–d)
2Premise set → another coordinate propositionExpert builders have the best success at building (Ap. 27b; Hp. Ma. 284a–b)

And three conceptions of the movement to a universal: (i) intuition of the universal from a sample, yielding certainty; (d) complete enumeration then trivial deduction; (g) probable inductive generalization, defeasible by a disconfirming instance. Wolfsdorf flags his own relabelling: “McPherran uses the symbols ‘(A)’, ‘(B)’ and ‘(C)’. I have avoided these to avoid confusion with the symbols Vlastos uses.”

Robinson said (d) is the most conspicuous form and that there is no clear case of (g). Against Vlastos’s “explicative epagōgē” — where epagōgē communicates a meaning rather than supporting a claim — Wolfsdorf notes the stake for the elenchus: “Vlastos’s position here is significant for his view of Socratic elenchus, specifically for his view that argument is from Q not to it [= (3)]. In other words, Vlastos’s view that Socrates employs intuitive induction … is consistent with the view that Socrates never tries to persuade an interlocutor of the truth of a premise.”

McPherran grants Vlastos the Ion case but denies it is representative: there is a “considerable presence of probabilistic, inductive, persuasive epagōgē” in Plato and Xenophon (359), and the Charmides argument at 159b–160d is his main evidence.

McPherran in his own words · JHP 45.3 (2007)

“Hence, there is clearly a need for a fresh, critical account of Socratic epagōgē — one tied to its occurrences in several key Socratic elenchoi. This paper is an introductory sketch of just such an account. Among other things, it shows not only that Socrates sometimes uses a non‑inferential epagogic form to communicate the meaning of some general claim in order to obtain or cement an interlocutors’ consent to that claim (‘explicative epagōgē’ — what Gregory Vlastos called ‘intuitive induction’), but also that, pace some commentators, Socrates uses another, legitimately inductive form of epagōgē to persuade interlocutors to adopt various beliefs.”

McPherran 2007, 349.

The second half is what bears on this debate. If Socrates sometimes argues to a premise rather than only from it, then Vlastos’s step (3) — “argument is from Q, not to it” — is not universally true, and the picture of the interlocutor as merely conceding what he already believed is incomplete.

“Finally, it seems to me that anyone attempting to maintain that there are few or no epagogic cases of type (a) — that Socrates rarely or never attempts to persuade his interlocutors to accept beliefs that they did not harbor prior to their encounter with him … — must address what we may term the ‘Number of Instances Problem.’ That is, if Socrates always or generally uses epagōgē to explain, but not to persuade, why does he frequently use more cases — more than just one or two sample cases — than are needed for mere explanation?”

McPherran 2007, 358–59.

The method is a three‑property test for whether Socrates takes an elenchus to establish falsity:

Before the argument
Socrates makes statements indicating that he aims to establish that the interlocutor’s thesis is false.
After the argument
Socrates makes statements indicating that he has established it.
Expectation
Socrates expects his interlocutor to abandon his thesis and accept the conclusion. “Santana emphasizes that of the three properties taken individually, (3) has the strongest evidential weight” (255).

Three elenchi are examined: Crito 44c–48b (all three properties), Republic I 335b–336a and Euthyphro 8b–e (properties 2 and 3) — “thus vindicating (CA)” (summarized at 263).

Against the disavowal of knowledge: “one can be a fallibilist yet still give proofs of which one is confident” (265). Against Benson’s doxastic constraint: given (3), “Socrates must himself affirm the truth of [Q] and therefore believe [Q]” (265). And the fourth — that Q’s premises must have an epistemic property more plausibly related to truth than the thesis — Santana “simply acknowledges … as a problem that a defender of (CA) must answer. The 2009 paper pays the debt left at the end of 2007. Its stated purpose: “to address the … controversy [about whether there is a genuine problem of the elenchus], part of which centers around an important but ambiguous assumption which seems to require that Socrates give or have particular epistemic reasons for the truth of the premises he uses to refute the interlocutor” (2009: 41).

Splitting the problem in two

FormalBecause agreement to Q is ad hoc — the premises are logically unsecured — Socrates cannot validly conclude not‑p: the interlocutor agrees to Q but also believes p.
Epistemic“because Socrates secures only the interlocutor’s agreement to the [premises of Q], their epistemic status remains undisclosed” (43). It is unclear how Q has a “weightier epistemic status” than p (43).

Which yields what Santana calls the central question: “How does Socrates think he and the interlocutor are justified in thinking that [the premises constitutive of Q] in his elenctic arguments are true?” (44) — and the assumption that generates it:

EASocrates must give or have epistemic reasons for how the interlocutor and he are justified in thinking that the premises of Q are true. 2009: 44

Three readings of (EA), two of which dissolve the problem

ReadingContentConsequence
(EA1)Reasons for the truth of every premise of QKraut’s infinite regress follows; (EA1) is unjustifiable; and with it the problem is resolved. Wolfsdorf: Santana discusses this at 44–5.
(EA2)Reasons for some premises — those that need themSantas’s route: there are “at least piecemeal solutions,” since an argument for q unsecured in one elenchus may be found elsewhere in the early dialogues (45–6).
(EA3)General epistemic reasons for the truth of the premises of QSantana’s reading, and the one that preserves the problem: Vlastos’s was “a general methodological problem that seeks to reveal Socrates’ background methodological assumptions regarding the epistemic status of [the premises of Q], whatever these may be” (47).

The solution: corroborative agreement

“Socrates’ general epistemic rationale for the truth of the premises of Q, which lends them more evidential weight than his interlocutor’s thesis p, is that Socrates and his interlocutor agree to Q, whereas only the interlocutor agrees to p. In short, the mutual agreement of Socrates and his interlocutor to Q corroborates the truth of Q” (50).

The everyday analogy Wolfsdorf reports: two people identifying someone at a distance; each asserting it is Jones gives the other more reason to think it is (52).

And the distinction that has to hold for this to work: corroborative agreement inside an elenchus versus mere agreement in rhetorical contexts not aimed at truth (51). It has to hold because “Socrates himself dismisses the mere agreement of others as a reason for thinking a claim is true” — he “dismisses the popularity of a claim as a reason for thinking that claim is true” (50–2). “Thus, corroborative agreement in elenctic contexts has a special weight that tips the evidence in favour of Q and so not‑p.”

What the last section of the survey does to the first

Santana’s solution and Vlastos’s are the same shape and different in one respect that matters. Both look for something general that raises Q above p. Vlastos found it in a claim about the structure of everyone’s beliefs, backed by induction from Socrates’ own experience. Santana finds it in the bare fact that two people assented and one did not — no claim about belief structure, no induction, nothing that needs a thirty‑year track record. The obvious objection is the one Wolfsdorf makes at p. 66: it “is open to debate.” Two people agreeing that Q is not obviously better evidence than one person’s conviction that p, particularly when one of the two chose the question.

“Prospects for further study” · pp. 66–67, quoted at length

“A theory of Socrates’ method depends upon the interpretation of arguments and as such to some extent falls within the domain of logic. But as we have seen, the interpretation of Socratic arguments is not limited to their logical form; it also requires interpretation of the psychological attitudes of the participants, in other words, the pragmatics of argumentation. … In that case, it is necessary to survey and clarify the evidence that informs our knowledge of the relevant psychological states of the characters. It is possible, for various reasons, that Plato employs Socrates and his interlocutors variously in different argumentative contexts.”

Wolfsdorf p. 66.

“As far as the elenchus is concerned, Benson’s dissolution has not won much support. Indeed, the most recent contributions to the debate, Santana’s, reaffirm that there is a problem of the elenchus. Santana’s proposed solution, that corroborative agreement carries greater evidential weight than the interlocutor’s sole assertion, is open to debate. In short, Vlastos’s problem remains alive and invites further reflection.”

Wolfsdorf p. 66 — the verdict of the chapter.

Note what the verdict is and is not. It is not that Vlastos was right; the survey has recorded that the founding thesis was withdrawn in 1983, that (A) and (B) do not entail (C), and that (C) would not cover the premises anyway. It is that the problem survived all of that. Twenty‑six years, twenty‑one papers, and the question — how can showing an inconsistency show which member of the set is false? — is still open.

“At the same time, as other scholars have suggested, we should broaden examination of Socrates’ arguments beyond the elenchus and the problem of the elenchus. Not all of Socrates’ arguments aim at or achieve refutation. But even among those that do, not all pursue or accomplish this objective in the same way. For example, some arguments operate through some form of reductio; some by analogy; some are epagogic; some use certain premises as dialectical expedients; some are deliberately fallacious and variously so. It may be useful to consider to what extent Socratic arguments, both critical and constructive, can be classified.”

Wolfsdorf pp. 66–67.

A research programme, and a countable one: a form‑by‑form census of Socratic argument. Nobody in the survey has done it. Carpenter and Polansky opened the question, Forster added self‑contradiction, McPherran added epagōgē.

“In short, between Vlastos’s monolithic conception of the elenchus as Socratic method and Kraut’s general claim that Socrates argues by picking premises he considers eminently reasonable, a good deal of middle ground lies open for investigation of details and specifics.”

Wolfsdorf p. 67.

The two poles named, twenty‑six years after they were set out in consecutive papers in the same volume. Everything in between is what the chapter has surveyed. (And see §25 no. 5: “Kraut’s general claim” is stated in Vlastos’s words.)

“The primary data or domain of evidence on the basis of which a theory of Socrates’ method is to be constructed is Plato’s early dialogues. But there are controversies surrounding which Platonic dialogues are early and which were composed by Plato. For example, many scholars simply reject developmentalism. … Insofar as we are serious about studying Socrates’ method in the early dialogues of Plato, we must be prepared to defend the view that a certain set of dialogues is early and Platonic.”

Wolfsdorf p. 67.

The chapter’s last move is to put its own evidence base in question. Every section above depends on a list of dialogues given in a parenthesis on p. 34 — and that list is a chronological claim, not a datum.

“To this it may be objected that it suffices to focus on a set of dialogues uncontroversially identifiable as Platonic and perhaps early. But this would only be acceptable if there were a uniform Socratic method. Why should we presume that? Since questions of chronology and authorship are extremely difficult to settle, it may be prudent simply to examine Socrates’ method in a set of dialogues and allow our results to encourage or discourage the view that the method is consistent or continuous among these texts. Finally, insofar as we are concerned with the method of the favoured character, if not the authorial mouthpiece, in a group of Platonic texts, why should we speak of ‘Socrates’ method’ rather than ‘Plato’s’?”

Wolfsdorf p. 67 — the last substantive paragraph of the chapter.

The proposal in the middle is the important one, and it inverts the usual order: do not fix the chronology and then read the method, but read the method across a set of texts and let the results bear on whether the set is continuous. The closing question is the one the chapter opened with in a parenthesis on p. 34, now asked without an answer.

“Scholarship of the last quarter century on the philosophical method or methods portrayed in a set of Platonic dialogues has been an unprecedentedly vibrant period in the history of the subject. The foregoing survey of central contributions should help to advance the discussion still further.”

Wolfsdorf p. 67 — the closing sentence, signed “David Wolfsdorf.”

The shape of the whole

Vlastos’s answer did not survive its first three years. Vlastos’s question has survived everything thrown at it — including a sustained attempt to show that the object it is a question about does not exist.

Every solution offered after 1985 replaces (A) and (B) with something weaker: endoxa (Polansky), survived testing (Brickhouse and Smith, McPherran), internal evidence (Adams), self‑contradiction (Forster), corroborative agreement (Santana). Every dissolution offered denies a different premise: that Socrates claims falsity (Benson), that the word is right (Tarrant), that a general logic exists (Carpenter and Polansky), that the object exists (Brickhouse and Smith), that the pursuit is adversarial (Wolfsdorf).
