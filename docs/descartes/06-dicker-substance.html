The cogito rebuilt on the substance theory: where its concepts come from, how its six steps run, why they fail, and why a stream of thoughts cannot supply the continuity the wax supplies

Abstract

This conversation works through chapter 2 of Georges Dicker’s Descartes: An Analytical and Historical Introduction in four questions. Where do Dicker’s concepts come from: bundle and substance, determinate and determinable, “nothingness has no properties,” the argument from change? How does his six-step reconstruction of the cogito run, and is it his own? How does “a thought is a property” get established? And then your objection: I only ever know individual thoughts. The wax shows a continuity I can observe. What entitles me to a continuity of thoughts unless I presuppose the substance?

The chat’s answers: Dicker “isn’t quoting anyone.” He is doing rational reconstruction with a mixed pedigree: Hume’s bundle, W. E. Johnson’s determinables of 1921 (“a frank anachronism”), the scholastic nihili nulla sunt attributa, and Aristotle’s substratum. Steps (2)–(6) are Kenny’s; step (1) and its grounding are Dicker’s. On your objection, the chat says the wax does not show substance either (Dicker’s carpenter’s plane). What the wax has is an independent criterion of continuity, and thoughts have none. So the argument from change is question-begging in its application to the mind, and Descartes concedes as much by making the persistence of the self depend on continuous creation.

What this review adds. Every page reference is checked in your copy of Dicker (printed page = PDF page − 21). Four additions. First, Dicker’s own last word is not the failed reconstruction but a 16-step argument (p. 79). In it the whole gap you identified sits in one premise, step 13, which Dicker calls “very plausible.” Second, Hyperaspistes’ objection of 1641, which Kenny and Dicker cite for the world-soul, begins with the question your Chat 5 thesis asks: you do not know what “thing,” “exist” and “thought” mean (CSMK 3:192 n.). Third, Cottingham, in a book in your folder, calls Kenny’s analysis of the cogito as presupposing the scholastic maxim “mistaken” (Cartesian Reflections, p. 70 n. 53). Fourth, one locator changes: the reply to Hobbes is CSM 2:124, not 123.

The questions this conversation asks

“But I can only know the individual thoughts. Even the thought that thoughts are determinates is also just another thought. … How are we certain that we are in a continuity of thoughts unless we presuppose the substance?”

The first three questions are genealogical and structural. They concern where Dicker’s tools come from, what the reconstruction is, and whose it is. The fourth is philosophical, and it is the reason this conversation belongs in the series. It is your objection, and the chat’s verdict is that “Dicker doesn’t press it.”

How to read the citations. Dicker is cited from the 2nd edition (Oxford, 2013), your file Descartes-An-Analytical-and-Historical-Introduction.pdf. Its text layer drops digits, so pages were fixed by position: printed page = PDF page − 21, confirmed at the section openings on pp. 54, 58 and 71. Descartes is cited by AT and CSM/CSMK, checked. The thing that makes this conversation worth reading twice

Your objection finds a failure Dicker’s own four critiques miss. Dicker attacks each step. You attack the support for step (1) when it is applied to the mind. In the chat’s words: “the argument from change is question-begging in its mental application specifically, in a way it isn’t for bodies. That’s a real result, and Dicker doesn’t press it.”

The reason is that the argument from change needs a premise of the form “all the thing’s determinate properties changed, and it is still the same thing.” For the wax, that premise has an anchor outside the argument: spatio-temporal tracking. For the mind, the Synopsis simply asserts it. “You have to already believe you’re one continuing mind to grant it.”

The disanalogy in two sentences — from the chat’s last answer
The wax
Substance is not observed, but continuity is independently checkable: I can track the wax, cool it, remould it. “Same wax” is anchored without presupposing a substrate.
The mind
I meet only individual thoughts, and “these are one mind’s thoughts” is itself another thought. “Same mind” has no anchor except the substance it was meant to prove.
“Having no rival isn’t evidence. It just means the question stays open, which is roughly where Kant leaves it.”

Dicker’s opening: “The bundle theory holds that a thing is merely a collection of coexisting properties. … The substance theory holds that a thing is composed of various properties plus an underlying substance to which these properties belong” (p. 50 checked). The chat’s genealogy:

ConceptSource, as the chat gives itIn the folder?

Hume’s sentence, as Foster quotes it, is the first half of your objection almost word for word: “I never can catch myself at any time without a perception, and never can observe anything but the perception” (Foster, p. 213). The chat says so at the end: “The first half of your objection is Hume.”

02Determinate and determinable

Dicker introduces the pair to state the argument from change exactly: “A determinate property is one that is absolutely specific, and a determinable property one that is not” (p. 51 checked). Elliptical is a determinable; only an ellipse satisfying a particular equation is a determinate.

The chat’s attribution
W. E. Johnson, Logic, Part I (Cambridge, 1921), ch. XI.

Johnson coined both terms, to distinguish the relation from genus and species: determinates are not produced by adding an independent differentia to a determinable. Follow-up: A. N. Prior, “Determinables, Determinates and Determinants,” Mind 58 (1949). “So this is a frank anachronism in Dicker’s reconstruction: 1921 machinery applied to 1641.” not held Two other AI reports in your folder, Copilot.pdf and Descartes Scholarship Methodology Survey.pdf, make the same attribution.

03“Nothingness has no attributes”

T1 · Principles I.11

“…we should notice something very well known by the natural light: nothingness possesses no attributes or qualities. It follows that, wherever we find some attributes or qualities, there is necessarily some thing or substance to be found for them to belong to.”

AT 8A:8 · CSM 1:196 checked; again at I.52, CSM 1:210 checked

Dicker reconstructs the inference Descartes seems to draw from the maxim, and grades it:

(i) Nothingness has no properties. (ii) If there were a property that did not belong to anything, then nothingness would have a property. (iii) Every property belongs to something. This, however, is a weak argument. For even if we grant (i), (ii) is not obviously true. … Furthermore, (iii) does not imply that the “something” to which a property belongs is a substance. Why could it not just belong to a thing, conceived as a bundle or cluster of properties? Dicker p. 56 checked

The chat supplies the scholastic background: non entis nullae sunt proprietates, resting on Aristotle’s doctrine that accidents have being only as of a substance, and transmitted to La Flèche through the Coimbra commentaries, Toletus and above all Suárez’s Disputationes Metaphysicae (DM 33, 37–39, 54). It cites Gilson’s Index scolastico-cartésien (1913), Ariew’s Descartes and the Last Scholastics (1999) and Rozemond’s Descartes’s Dualism (1998). Ariew and Rozemond are both in the folder. The scholastic background is the subject of Chat 9 and of your Ariew and Secada notes.

04The argument from change

T2 · the Synopsis: the argument applied to the mind

“…even if all the accidents of the mind change, so that it has different objects of the understanding and different desires and sensations, it does not on that account become a different mind; whereas a human body loses its identity merely as a result of a change in the shape of some of its parts.”

AT 7:14 · CSM 2:10 checked

Dicker: “This is simply the argument from change, now applied directly to the mind” (p. 54). This is the sentence your objection targets: its first clause asserts the mind’s identity through change and does not show it.

The chat’s three Aristotelian loci are Categories 5, 4a10–b19 (numerically one substance receiving contraries); Physics I.7, 189b30–191a22 (form, privation and a persisting hypokeimenon); and De Generatione et Corruptione I.4 (alteration versus generation). All three are not held. It adds a caveat: Aristotle’s persisting subject is matter or a primary substance, not a bare substrate. “The bare-substratum conclusion Dicker’s premises generate is really Lockean, not Aristotelian.”

Sections 05–06the steps · the attribution
01

The reconstruction.

“A note first: this is chapter 2 (‘Meditation II: The Cogito and the Self’), §4 (Substance Theory, p. 49) and §5 (the reconstruction, p. 54) — not chapter 4.” The chat’s first correction of the question, confirmed.

05Six steps, with the text under each

(1) “A thing is composed of its properties or characteristics plus an underlying substance to which they belong.” (p. 54)
“Thing” here means “an entity that could conceivably exist independently of all other entities,” not shadows or edges. So “the minimum that can exist on its own is always a substance and its properties” (p. 55).
(2) “If there is a property or characteristic, then there must be a substance to which it belongs.” (p. 55)
A corollary of (1). Descartes: attributes “must inhere in something if they are to exist; and we call the thing in which they inhere a ‘substance’” (Fourth Replies, AT 7:222, CSM 2:156 checked); and T1.
(3) “A thought is a property.” (p. 56)
An assumption, “quite explicit” in the Synopsis, where thinking, willing and perceiving are “accidents” of the mind (T2).
(4) “If there is a thought, then there is a substance to which it belongs.” (p. 56)
Descartes to Hobbes: “it is certain that a thought cannot exist without a thing that is thinking; and in general no act or accident can exist without a substance for it to belong to” (AT 7:175–6, CSM 2:124 checked). Dicker’s observation is that the clause after the semicolon is (2), the clause before it is (4), and (3) is “left unstated or ‘understood’” (p. 57).
(5) “There is a thought.” (p. 57)
The cogito’s premise “once the apparently question-begging ‘I’ is expurgated from it,” the premise “Bertrand Russell was prepared to ‘give’ to Descartes.”
(6) “There is a substance to which this thought belongs: ‘I.’” (p. 57)
“This completes the reconstruction of Descartes’s argument.”

Kenny does not point out that (2) derives from (1) and is thus a corollary of the substance theory; the logical starting point of his reconstruction is the second step of ours. Aside from this difference, the two reconstructions are the same. Dicker p. 58 checked

Cottingham rejects exactly the analysis Dicker takes over from Kenny. He follows Kenny’s wording on La Flèche for his own purposes, then adds: “Kenny, however, applies the criticism to the Cogito argument, which he analyses (mistakenly in my view) as presupposing the scholastic maxim nihili nulla sunt attributa” (Cartesian Reflections, p. 70 n. 53 checked). So the reconstruction’s reading of the cogito is contested at its root, not only in the critique of §6.

Part Two

About this part

Dicker’s §6, one subsection per step, as the chat summarises it and as the pages confirm.

In short

Substance is unperceivable. The corollary takes a side on universals. The assumption is unargued. And even granting all of it, nothing makes the substance “I.”

Sections 07–09§§6.1–6.4
02

Dicker’s critique of his own reconstruction.

“This is where it dies.” The chat’s verdict on §6.4. The first three subsections weaken the reconstruction; the fourth breaks it.

076.1: substance is unperceivable

Imagine, for example, that you want to see the substance of a block of wood. So, you obtain a carpenter’s plane and plane away a layer of wood. What do you then see? Well, you see a new set of properties—a new size (slightly smaller), shape, and color. Dicker p. 58 checked
Scientific essences
Must a thing keep at least its chemical or atomic constitution? Lose H2O and it is not water; lose atomic number 79 and it is “fool’s gold” (p. 60).
Necessary but not sufficient
The chat’s “vanishing gold statue”: qualitatively identical, numerically distinct. The contemporary replacement is “spatio-temporal continuity under a sortal” (p. 62).
(R*): one determinable
A thing survives if it keeps at least one determinable (p. 62). This looks false: “your car were put into a powerful compactor and reduced to a six-by-six foot solid cube of metal” (p. 63).
But Descartes would accept (R*)
Extension is the one determinable; ordinary bodies are regions of one physical substance. Substance becomes the principal attribute, not a mysterious substratum. The price: ordinary objects stop being things in their own right.

086.2–6.3: universals, and the unargued assumption

6.2, the Corollary, takes a side in the problem of universals: Platonic realism (properties exist independently, so the corollary is false), moderate realism, or nominalism (p. 65 checked).

6.3, the Assumption. “A thought is a property” cannot be argued from dualism at this stage without circularity. Dicker supplies an argument he flags as not Descartes’:

A substance is something that can acquire and lose properties while remaining the same substance … But could a thought acquire and lose properties while remaining the same thought …? It seems not: if, for example, I think of a ship with one chimney and then of an exactly similar ship except that it has two chimneys …, am I not thinking a different thought? Dicker p. 68 checked
Descartes did not question these assumptions, not because he was deliberately trying to cheat, but rather because they were such fundamental assumptions of medieval thought that it simply did not occur to him that they were assumptions standing in need of justification, and perhaps untenable ones. Richard Schacht, quoted by Dicker p. 69 checked

The chat’s answer to your third question (“explain simply how thought-is-a-property is reached”) turns the chimney argument into seven steps. A thought’s identity “is entirely exhausted by its content,” so it fails the substance test, and with only two boxes it must be a property. The essential attribute is thinking as such (Principles I.53: “thought constitutes the nature of thinking substance,” CSM 1:210 checked). The accidental ones are particular determinate thoughts.

096.4: why “I”?

[T]he principle that where there are attributes there must be a substance does not seem as unquestionable since the writings of Berkeley and Hume as it did to Descartes. Too often, when Descartes tells us that something is taught by the natural light in our souls, he produces a doctrine taught by the Jesuits at La Flèche. Kenny, quoted by Dicker p. 70 checked

Then Kenny’s decisive objection, also on p. 70: what “I” refers to must at least be distinct from what “you” refers to, “otherwise the argument might as well run ‘cogitatur, ergo es’ … as ‘cogito ergo sum.’” He cites Hyperaspistes, and the letter is in your folder:

T3 · Hyperaspistes’ fifth objection (1641)

“The fifth objection was against the cogito, ergo sum. ‘Is it possible for you to understand a proposition without understanding its subject and predicate? But you do not know what is meant by “thing”, what is meant by “exist”, what is meant by “thought”. … Moreover, you do not know whether it is you yourself who think or whether the world-soul in you does the thinking, as the Platonists believe’”

AT 3:403, quoted in CSMK 3:192 n. 1 (to Hyperaspistes, August 1641) checked

Kenny and Dicker use only the last clause, the world-soul. The first clause is the framework objection of your two-tier thesis: the cogito needs the concepts thing, existence and thought, and the meditator has not shown that he has them (Chat 5 §05). Hyperaspistes raised both objections in one breath in 1641. Descartes’ answer to the first is the Sixth Replies’ “internal awareness which always precedes reflective knowledge” (CSM 2:285).

Dicker’s small correction of Kenny: the world-soul case is badly chosen, since “the world-soul is thinking in me” does entail that I exist (p. 71). “Still, this does not affect the basic point that ‘I exist’ doesn’t follow from the premises of the reconstruction … From those premises, nothing at all follows about the identity of the thinking substance in which the thinking occurs” (p. 71 checked).

03

Your objection.

“You’ve isolated the exact place where the transfer breaks. Let me sharpen it, because the disanalogy isn’t quite where you put it.”

10A stream of thoughts has no criterion of continuity

The wax doesn’t show substance either
The carpenter’s plane (p. 58). What the wax has instead is an independent criterion of continuity: spatio-temporal tracking.
This does not sink the cogito directly
The reconstruction’s inference is synchronic: this property, now, needs a bearer, now. “I am, I exist” is necessarily true quoties, as often as it is conceived (CSM 2:17). Lichtenberg attacks the synchronic inference; you attack the diachronic support.
It does sink premise (1) for the mind
The argument from change is “irreducibly diachronic.” Applied to the mind (T2), its premise “asserts the diachronic identity that the conclusion (substance) is supposed to explain.”
Descartes agrees with you
“For a lifespan can be divided into countless parts, each completely independent of the others” (Med. III, AT 7:48–9, CSM 2:33 checked). Continuity is not read off the stream; it takes continuous creation. “The persisting self is not a Meditation II result at all — it’s downstream of the theology.”
The reflexive point
“Thoughts are determinate properties” is itself a cogitatio, and so are premises (1)–(4). “The entire reconstruction runs on metaphysical credit not yet earned.”

Where you have arrived, as the chat puts it: the first half of the objection is Hume (§01); the second is Kant’s Third Paralogism, on which the identity of consciousness across time is a formal condition of my thoughts hanging together and “proves nothing about the numerical identity of a persisting subject.” B131–2 makes unity a structural requirement: “The I think must be able to accompany all my representations” (Guyer/Wood p. 246 checked). The same Kantian division, existence secured and nature refused, organises Chat 5 §09. The same dependence on God for continuity closes Chat 3 §12.

11Dicker’s own answer, and the premise that carries the gap

The chat stresses that §7 is the payoff but does not describe it. Dicker’s defence of the unreconstructed cogito begins by redefining question-begging epistemically:

We propose the following definition: a question-begging argument =df an argument such that it is impossible to know the premise(s) of the argument without already knowing its conclusion. Dicker p. 72 checked
The cogito needs to be, so to speak, “deceiver-proof” (using the term “proof” as in “waterproof”), but it cannot be deceiver-proof if it requires a premise other than “I am thinking.” … Here, so to speak, epistemology is prior to (formal) logic. Dicker pp. 78–79 checked

Then Dicker integrates the defended cogito with the substance theory in a sixteen-step argument (p. 79 checked). Steps 1–3 are the cogito (“I am thinking … I exist”). Steps 4–12 reach “There is a substance to which T belongs.” Then:

T4 · Dicker’s step 13

“13. If I have a thought T and there is a substance to which T belongs, then I am the substance to which T belongs. … The only premise that goes beyond those ideas is (13), and that premise seems very plausible.”

Dicker p. 79 checked

Step 13 is Hyperaspistes’ question (T3) turned into a premise and marked plausible. It is also exactly what your objection denies we are entitled to. For a single momentary thought, “I am its bearer” may be harmless. For the mind of the Synopsis, the same substance through all its changing thoughts, step 13 has to hold at every moment and of the same “I.” That is the continuity no stream of thoughts supplies.

13Where this leads

The line to keep

For bodies, continuity can be checked without presupposing substance. For minds it cannot, so the argument from change proves the mind a substance only by assuming what it sets out to prove.

This conversation also raises a question of method. Dicker’s tools are 1921 logic and 20th-century labels applied to a 1641 text. Is that legitimate? Chat 7 asks exactly that, and uses the cogito as its test case.
