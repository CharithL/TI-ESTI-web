What demand do the Forms answer?

You asked me to open out one line from the anthology. Here is the dispute properly done — every position taken from the actual text of the book it comes from, with what each one commits you to and who it is fighting. Two of them correct what I originally wrote.

First — two corrections to what I told you

I was too quick about Prior. I wrote that he "takes the demand to be broadly epistemological." His actual position is more interesting and more radical: he thinks that in the early and middle dialogues Plato never argues for the Forms at all. He assumes them. Position 2 below has the quotation.

I missed Gail Fine's position entirely, which is the sharpest one in the folder and cuts against the framing I used in the anthology's Chapter I. I leaned on the flux argument from the Cratylus. Fine argues that flux is not Plato's argument for Forms — compresence of opposites is — and she names Cornford and Cherniss as the people who got this wrong. Position 5.

The eleven positions

Ordered roughly from the classic statement to the newest. Quotations are verbatim from the volumes named.

01Three demands at once — the "philosophical economy"H. F. Cherniss (1936)
in Allen ed., ch. I · also Vlastos ed., ch. 2
The demand
Not one demand but three, converging: ethical, epistemological, ontological. Late-fifth-century thought had produced paradoxes in each area, and Plato found a single hypothesis that resolved all three. The convergence is itself the argument for the hypothesis — the same principle of economy by which an astronomer prefers the theory that "saves the phenomena" with fewest assumptions.
In his words
The phenomena for which Plato had to account were of three kinds, ethical, epistemological, and ontological. In each of these spheres there had been developed by the end of the fifth century doctrines so extremely paradoxical that there seemed to be no possibility of reconciling them with one another or any one of them with the observable facts of human experience. The dialogues of Plato, I believe, will furnish evidence to show that he considered it necessary to find a single hypothesis which would at once solve the problems of these several spheres and also create a rationally unified cosmos."The Philosophical Economy of the Theory of Ideas," in Studies in Plato's Metaphysics
Aimed at
Aristotle's charge (Metaphysics A) that the Forms are "a superfluous duplication of the phenomenal world — as if one should think it impossible to count a number of objects until that number had first been multiplied." Cherniss's reply: that objection misrepresents the motivation. The Forms aren't a duplication; they're an economy.
Commits you to
Reading the Phaedo's method of hypothesis as genuinely scientific, and treating the ethical motivation as co-equal with the epistemological. Also to Extreme Heracleitean flux as part of the picture — which is exactly what Fine denies (position 5).
02There is no argument — Forms are assumedWilliam Prior
Unity and Development in Plato's Metaphysics, ch. 1 §I
The demand
Prior's answer is that the question is misconceived for the early and middle dialogues. Plato does not treat the existence of Forms as something needing defence; he inherits it from the Socratic "What is F?" question, where the interlocutor accepts it without objection.
In his words
In the early and middle dialogues, Plato does not argue for the claim that the Forms exist. He rather assumes that they do and argues from this fact to other conclusions. There is no indication in these dialogues that he regards the existence of the Forms as problematic, as subject to serious philosophical disagreement.ch. 1 §I, "The Existence of the Forms"
Evidence
Euthyphro 5d3–5: "does not whatever is going to be unholy have a certain single Form with respect to Unholiness?" — and Euthyphro simply accepts it. The language of Forms (idea, eidos) is already there in the earliest dialogues, unargued.
Commits you to
A developmental reading in which the interesting event is not the positing of Forms but the Parmenides' attack on them — which is why Prior's ch. 2 is where his book gets going. It also implies that anyone hunting for "Plato's argument for the Forms" in the Phaedo or Republic is looking for something that isn't there.
03Separation and essence — a Form is what it is in virtue of itselfAllan Silverman
The Dialectic of Essence, ch. 1
The demand
The explanatory work is done by essence. The One-Over-Many is the inference pattern that gets you to Forms; separation (auto kath auto) is what makes them able to do the job, because a Form is what it is not derivatively but in virtue of itself.
In his words
A Form is separate in that it is what it is itself by itself or, better, in virtue of itself. The task is to explain what it is for a Form to be what it is in virtue of itself… On my account, essence plays the crucial role in the explanation of the separation of Forms.ch. 1
We recognize that many items are the same in kind or type: Secretariat and Seattle Slew are both horses; Aristides and Socrates are both just. From such facts Plato infers that there are Forms, e.g., Justice, Beauty, Squareness, Equality.ch. 1, on the One-Over-Many
The twist
Silverman identifies three separations, all "part of the legacy of Parmenides": Forms from physical particulars; Being from Identity; and mind from what it knows. The third is why he says the boundary between metaphysics and epistemology is blurred in Plato — not sloppiness, but structure.
Commits you to
Two distinct predication relations, one for Forms and one for particulars — the machinery that lets him say "Justice is just" without generating the Third Man. Compare position 9.
04Not the modern problem of universals at allFrancis Grabowski
Plato, Metaphysics and the Forms, Introduction: "Platonism as Realism"
The demand
Grabowski's move is to name the standard answer and reject it. The standard answer says the Forms solve attribute agreement — why numerically distinct things share a character — by positing universals that particulars instantiate. He calls this "the standard interpretation" and argues Plato is not doing it.
In his words
The metaphysical realist — so named because he/she takes universals to be real, objectively existing entities — argues that numerically distinct leaves can be the same colour because they instantiate (or exemplify) the same universal: greenness… The "standard interpretation" of the Forms, as it shall henceforth be known, takes Plato to be not only the first philosopher to pose the problem of attribute agreement, but also the first to hazard a solution by positing the existence of universals.Introduction, pp. 2–3
Aimed at
Loux and the analytic metaphysics tradition that treats Plato as the origin point of realism about universals — and, by extension, at the instinct you will bring to Plato from modern philosophy. Grabowski quotes Loux insisting that "the form of explanation being recommended [by the realist] is precisely the one Plato proposes."
Commits you to
Taking seriously that Plato's Forms do things universals cannot: they are standards of value, objects of love, causes of coming-to-be, and the terminus of an education. A theory of universals is not obviously any of those.
05Compresence of opposites — not fluxGail Fine
Introduction to Plato 1: Metaphysics and Epistemology
The demand
The phenomenon that drives Plato to Forms is not that sensibles change over time (succession), but that they are F and not-F at once (compresence). A stick is equal to one thing and unequal to another; returning what you owe is just in some circumstances and not in others. The Form of Equal cannot be both. Therefore the Form is not to be identified with, or defined in terms of, sensible equals.
In her words
My own view is that Plato never believed that the sensible world is in [Extreme Heracleitean flux]. Moreover, although he believes that the sensible world, or various items in it, undergo [mere Heracleitean flux], and that that distinguishes sensibles from forms, which are wholly unchanging, that is not the sort of change he appeals to in arguing that there are forms. Rather, in arguing for forms, he appeals to compresence rather than to any sort of succession.Introduction, §on the arguments for Forms
The argument
Her reconstruction of Phaedo 74b–c in three steps: (1) sensible equals "being the same, appear equal to one, unequal to another"; (2) "the equals themselves" never appear unequal; (3) therefore the sensible equals and the Equal itself are not the same. Note there is no flux premise anywhere in it.
Aimed at
Cornford (Plato's Theory of Knowledge) and Cherniss — position 1 above — who both read Plato as appealing to Extreme Heracleitean flux. Fine names them by page.
Why it matters to you
If Fine is right, the Cratylus flux passage is not the argument for the Forms — it's a separate argument about the possibility of knowledge. And the tidy story "Heraclitus + Parmenides → Forms" that every introduction tells you (including mine) is at best half the picture.
06Sensibles really are F — imperfection isn't failureAlexander Nehamas, "Plato on the Imperfection of the Sensible World"
in Fine ed., Plato 1, ch. VI
The demand
Against the approximation view — the familiar idea that sensible Fs are only imperfect approximations to F-ness, never really F. Nehamas holds that Helen really is beautiful. The Forms are needed not because sensibles fail to be F, but because of how they are F.
The position
Nehamas argues, against this view, that Plato believes that sensible F things really are F; Helen, for example, really is beautiful. Her imperfection consists, not in her not being beautiful at all, but in various other facts. For example, according to Nehamas, she is accidentally beautiful rather than essentially so; her beauty is incomplete (that is, he explains, attributive or relational); and she is both beautiful and not beautiful.as summarised by Fine, Introduction to Plato 1
Commits you to
Dropping the "shadows and copies" reading of the Cave as a metaphysical claim. If Nehamas is right, the sensible world is not a degraded imitation — it is a domain where predication is accidental, incomplete and relational rather than essential, complete and absolute. That is a much more defensible thesis, and a much less romantic one.
07The demand is causal — Forms are powersAnna Marmodoro
Forms and Structure in Plato's Metaphysics, ch. 3
The demand
How does anything come to be F? Marmodoro takes the Sophist's criterion of being — the Eleatic Principle — at face value: to be is to be a power. Since Forms are the beings par excellence, Forms are powers, with what she calls constitutive causal efficacy.
In her words
In the Sophist (247e1–2), Plato gives the definitive criterion for an ontology comprising only powers, with what has become known… as the Eleatic Principle: EP: Those things which amount to nothing other than power (δύναμις). This principle, taken as the definition of being, entails that Plato is committed to the view that the Forms, the par excellence beings in his ontology, are powers.ch. 3 §2
Supporting move
She points out that the Republic contains "the first explicit definition of powers in ancient Greek thought" — the passage at 477c–d individuating powers by what they are set over and what they do. That is the same passage the knowledge/belief argument runs on, which links her reading directly to the epistemology.
Commits you to
Reading the Phaedo's "Forms as causes" literally rather than as loose talk, and to an Anaxagorean back-story: Marmodoro's chs. 1–2 argue Plato is building on Anaxagoras's Opposites. It also gives her a distinctive dissolution of the Third Man (ch. 5).
08One contrast and no other — intelligible vs. perceptibleVerity Harte, "Plato's Metaphysics"
Oxford Handbook of Plato (2019), ch. 19
The demand
Harte's is the most economical answer of the eleven. Plato is a realist about a mind-independent world and an optimist about knowing it — and that combination forces exactly one organising contrast on his ontology.
In her words
Plato is a realist, at least in one common use of the term realist; he is committed to the existence of a world that is objective and mind-independent. But he is a realist, we might say, of an essentially optimistic variety… he nowhere doubts that knowledge — through rational inquiry — is possible. This metaphysical orientation underlies the central contrast in his metaphysical theorizing, a contrast between what is intelligible and what is perceptible. It is this contrast and no other, I argue, that shapes the contours of his ontology.ch. 19 §1, "Identifying Our Topic"
Aimed at
Every framing that makes something else primary — being/becoming, one/many, transcendent/immanent, changeless/changing. Harte says those are downstream. Note this is a direct challenge to positions 1 and 5 alike, since both make a fact about sensibles (flux or compresence) the driver.
Worth knowing
She situates Plato between Parmenides and Democritus as a thinker "for whom reality differs from the way in which it presents itself to us in perceptual experience and must be rationally discovered," and notes that Forms are named most generally in Plato as simply "the beings" — ta onta, or ousiai Phaedo 65d, 66a.
09The question is malformed — "Justice is just" is an identityR. E. Allen, "Participation and Predication in Plato's Middle Dialogues" (1960)
in Allen ed., ch. IV
The demand
Allen attacks the assumption shared by most of the other positions: that a Form is a universal at all. If Forms were universals with themselves as attributes, the theory is incoherent from the start. So they aren't.
In his words
The dialogues often use language which suggests that the Form is a universal which has itself as an attribute and is thus a member of its own class, and, by implication, that it is the one perfect member of that class… Now such a view is, to say the least, peculiar. Proper universals are not instantiations of themselves, perfect or otherwise. Oddness is not odd; Justice is not just; Equality is not equal.ch. IV §I, "Self-Predication"
The resolution
Read "Justice is just" as an identity statement — Justice is what it is to be just — not as class membership. The demand the Forms answer is then the demand for standards of meaning and being, not for shared attributes.
Commits you to
Denying that the Third Man is a real problem, since it needs ordinary self-predication as a premise. Read against Vlastos in the same volume (chs. XII–XIV), who thinks the regress is real and fatal.
10The written answer is incomplete — principles, not FormsKenneth Sayre, Plato's Late Ontology · Hans Krämer, Plato and the Foundations of Metaphysics
The demand
Both hold that the mature answer is not in terms of Forms at all but in terms of principles: Unity and the Great-and-Small (the Indefinite Dyad), out of which Forms themselves are generated. Sayre reaches this through the Philebus and the Parmenides; Krämer through Aristotle's reports and the criticism of writing.
The doctrines
(1) that numbers come from the participation of the Great and the Small in Unity, (2) that sensible objects are constituted by Forms and the Great and the Small, (3) that Forms are composed of the Great and the Small and Unity, (4) that Forms themselves are numbers, and (5) that the Good is Unity.Sayre, summarising the doctrines attributed to Plato in Metaphysics A.6
The difference between them
Sayre argues these are recoverable from the dialogues, principally the Philebus — which is what makes his book respectable to anglophone scholarship. Krämer holds the real doctrine was never written down, and that the dialogues deliberately withhold it. That is the Tübingen thesis, and it is a minority position.
Commits you to
Treating the theory of Forms as a way-station rather than a destination — and, on Krämer's version, treating the entire written corpus as pedagogically incomplete by design.
11Three loads on one word — the GoodJ. E. Raven, Plato's Thought in the Making, ch. 10
The demand
Raven's version of the Cherniss point, aimed at a reader without Greek: the reason the theory looks incoherent to newcomers is that its central term is carrying three different jobs simultaneously, and dropping any one makes the rest unreadable.
In his words
The Good, for Plato, is first and most obviously the end or aim of life, the supreme object of all desire and all aspiration. Second, and more surprisingly, it is the condition of knowledge, that which makes the world intelligible and the mind intelligent. And third and last and most important, it is the creative and sustaining cause of the whole world and all its contents… Nobody who comes for the first time to these three great allegories can hope for much understanding of them unless he realizes that Plato is attaching this triple significance to the word 'Good'.ch. 10, "Sun, Divided Line and Cave"
Commits you to
Nothing technical — which is why it belongs at the start of your reading rather than the end. It is the same structural claim as Cherniss's, made about the Good rather than the Forms, and made without any scholarly apparatus.
12A mismatch between what our predicates mean and what perceptibles can satisfyNicholas P. White, "Plato's metaphysical epistemology"
Cambridge Companion to Plato, ch. 9
The demand
White's is the most precisely stated answer of the twelve. The notions expressed by our predicates are viewpoint-independent and circumstance-independent — when you introspect what "beautiful" or "equal" means, no reference to a standpoint, a time or a comparison class is in it. But every perceptible thing satisfies such predicates only relative to a standpoint, a time, a comparison. Forms are what it is to be F apart from viewpoint and circumstances.
In his words
Plato thinks, then, that we unavoidably do try to apply to perceptibles the notions of being F viewpoint-independently and circumstance-independently… When we do, though, we face a problem. It strikes one in a certain situation as right to call a thing beautiful in such a sense. But in another situation or at another time it strikes one, or someone else, as right to call it ugly. Thus the viewpoint, time, or situation affects the seeming appropriateness of applying a term whose meaning, as we think about it, seems to involve no reference to such things.§V
The diagnosis
And here is why Plato looks confused to a modern reader — White names the objection and disarms it:
This idea may seem like a crude conflation of epistemological and metaphysical theses. Why should something be the less F for appearing sometimes non-F? But there is incontrovertible evidence that Plato espouses a view that he formulates in this way. When we see what he is up to, it will be clear that what seems like confusion is actually the result of a quite different notion of reality and objectivity from the one that we expect and are used to.§IV
On self-predication
White goes further than Allen (position 9). A Form is not a thing that is F; it is the notion, and the property, of a thing's being F in the viewpoint-independent way:
The self-predicationist view would say that the Form of F is (predicatively) F. Plato's view is that the Form of F is what it is to be F apart from viewpoint and circumstances… When he exhibits difficulties with this idea (as he does in the so-called Third Man argument…), his aim, I think, is to make clear that he does not subscribe to it.§V
Where he stands
He absorbs Fine's compresence data (position 5) but relocates its significance: the problem is not a fact about sensibles, it is a mismatch between semantics and the world. He agrees with Allen that self-predication is not Plato's view, but goes further in reading the Third Man as Plato disowning it rather than merely failing to hold it. And his framing statement is close to Silverman's and Harte's: "Certainly in Plato there is no such divide. His views about what there is are largely controlled by ideas about how knowledge can be accounted for, and his thinking about what knowledge is takes its character from convictions about what there is that is knowable. As a result his doctrines have a different shape from characteristically modern ones."
Why it matters to you
This is the chapter that explains, better than anything else here, why Plato keeps sliding between "is" as existence and "is" as predication, and why that isn't sloppiness. Also useful: White points out that Plato's doubts about Euthyphro are not doubts about justification — the question is whether Euthyphro knows what piety is. Plato's epistemology is about definition and essence, not about warrant. If you arrive with a Gettier-shaped picture of what epistemology asks, that one observation will save you a great deal of confusion.

How to use this

The two that actually conflict head-on are Cherniss and Fine, and it is worth resolving that one for yourself before anything else, because it decides how you read Phaedo 74b–c — the single passage most often called the birth of the theory. Read Cherniss (Allen ch. I, about 20 pages), then Fine's Introduction §on the arguments for Forms. Then go to the Phaedo text and decide whether you see a flux premise in it.

The two that reframe rather than answer are Prior and Grabowski. Both are saying, in different ways, that the question presupposes something false — Prior that Plato was arguing at all, Grabowski that he was arguing about universals. Take them seriously; they are the cheapest way to avoid a whole family of misreadings.

The one to read if you only read one is Harte, because it is 25 recent pages, it is organised by question, and its thesis is stated plainly enough that you can tell immediately whether you disagree.

The one to read if you want the confusion to stop is White. He is the only author here who takes the specific charge a modern philosopher will want to make — that Plato is conflating metaphysics with epistemology — states it in the sharpest form ("Why should something be the less F for appearing sometimes non-F?"), and then answers it. Read him after Fine, since he is building on the same compresence data and putting it to different work.
