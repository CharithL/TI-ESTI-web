Plato’s Metaphysics · Companion to Chapter 1

Reading the Timaeus A working study

Whether it is Plato’s, when he wrote it, and what 48e–52d does: the what-is-it test run on fire, water, earth and air, the receptacle, and the one argument for the Forms’ existence

Where the study stands

The Timaeus is Plato’s, and late. In 48e–52d he takes the test Socrates used on definitions, whether a stable account can be given of what a thing is, and runs it on the four elements. The elements fail it, the receptacle passes it, and only the Forms pass it fully. Then, in the one place in the corpus where he does so, Plato argues that the Forms exist.

This page is a working study and will grow. Each section says what it rests on, and §11 lists which books in the Timaeus folder have been read and which have not. Charith’s questions are labelled as his. Translations of the Timaeus are Zeyl’s (Hackett 2000); translations of other dialogues are from Cooper (ed.) 1997. Quotations were checked against the books, and page numbers are the printed ones. Anything known only at second hand is marked reported.

Begun 24 September 2026. The argument at 51b–e, with the twenty-odd scholars who use it, is set out in Chapter 1, §08.

Part One

The text

This part settles two facts that the rest depends on. The dialogue is Plato’s, and it is one of his last.

01Is it Plato’s?

Yes. Few works in the corpus are better attested, and the passage that prompted the doubt is one that Aristotle discusses by name.

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 1“This part is something new. I even start to doubt whether it is by Plato himself, because the account is so mature.”
The answer

It is Plato’s. Taylor opens his commentary with the evidence: “Its authentication by testimony goes back almost to the time of composition” (1928: 1). What reads as maturity is lateness (§02), not another author.

02Late, and why that was disputed

The dispute has been about when Plato wrote it, never about whether he did.

“From antiquity onward the dialogue enjoyed an unchallenged reputation as the crowning work of Plato’s maturity” (Zeyl 2000: xvi). Stylometry agreed. It groups the Timaeus with the Sophist, Statesman, Philebus and Laws, chiefly on two late and deliberate habits of style: avoiding hiatus between words, and choosing the rhythm of sentence endings (xvii–xviii).

In 1953 G. E. L. Owen moved it back, after the Republic and before the Parmenides and Theaetetus (xvi). He argued that the late date left Plato holding two views he had already criticised (xviii–xix):

Likeness
The second regress of the Parmenides (132d–133a) attacks the view that sensible things are likenesses of Forms. The Timaeus still holds it.
Becoming
The Timaeus makes being and becoming exclusive (27d6–28a2). The Theaetetus (182c–183b) argues that what only becomes, without being anything, cannot be talked about at all.

Cherniss replied in 1957, against Owen’s use of the stylistic evidence, his reading of the Parmenides regress, and his account of being and becoming (xx). Zeyl’s verdict: “Owen’s case is far less compelling than it seemed to many when it was first presented.” The hiatus avoidance the Timaeus shares with the late group “has been reestablished as a firm indication of its lateness”. The evidence of sentence rhythm may put it before the Sophist and Statesman, but not before the Parmenides and Theaetetus (xx).

Maturity is a poor test of date

Owen read the maturity the other way. He took the Parmenides and Theaetetus to have “ushered in a less muddled and more sophisticated phase” (Zeyl’s summary, 2000: xvi), and so put the Timaeus before them. His second objection matters for this study: the passage at 49d–50a is about exactly how to talk about what is always becoming (§07, §10).

Part Two

48e–52d

The passage introduces a third kind of thing, the receptacle, and ends with the only argument in the corpus whose stated conclusion is that the Forms exist. In between, Timaeus asks what fire, water, earth and air can truly be called, and finds that the answer cannot be “this”.

03The passage step by step

Seven moves. The order matters: the bodies come first, the Forms last.

1 · 48e–49a · A third kind
“Then we distinguished two kinds, but now we must specify a third, one of a different sort.” It is “a receptacle of all becoming—its wetnurse, as it were” (49a).
2 · 49b–d · The elements will not hold still
The problem is to say which body one should “really call water rather than fire”, “in a way that employs a reliable and stable account” (49b). Water condenses into stones and earth, then dissolves into wind and air; air ignites into fire; fire turns back into air, air into cloud and mist, and these into water and stones again (49b–d).
3 · 49d–50a · “This” and “such”
None of them can be called “this”. Speak of fire “not as ‘this,’ but each time as ‘what is such’” (49d). Only that in which they appear to come to be may be called “this” or “that” (49e–50a). These lines are disputed (§05).
4 · 50a–b · The gold
Point at a shape moulded in gold and ask “What is it?”: “your safest answer by far, with respect to truth, would be to say, ‘gold,’ but never ‘triangle’”, because the shapes “change even while you’re making the statement”.
5 · 50b–51b · The receptacle
“We must always refer to it by the same term, for it does not depart from its own character in any way” (50b). It must have no character of its own, like the base that perfumers make “as odorless as possible” (50e). “The things that enter and leave it are imitations of those things that always are” (50c).
6 · 51b–e · The question, and the argument
“Is there such a thing as a Fire by itself?” (51b). “If understanding and true opinion are distinct, then these ‘by themselves’ things definitely exist—these Forms, the objects not of our sense perception, but of our understanding only” (51d).
7 · 51e–52d · Three kinds
The Form “keeps its own form unchangingly”; it is “invisible” and “it is the role of understanding to study it”. The copy “shares the other’s name and resembles it” and is perceived. Space “is itself apprehended by a kind of bastard reasoning” (52a–b). “There are being, space, and becoming, three distinct things” (52d).

04The what-is-it test on the elements

The test Socrates ran on definitions is run here on bodies. The order of the passage is the order of the argument.

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 2“First he talks about physical things, and then about things in themselves. Is this his priority of definition applied to water, earth, fire and air?”
The answer

Yes. Three things in the text show it.

The standard is laid down at the start. “Accounts of what is stable and fixed and transparent to understanding are themselves stable and unshifting.” Accounts of a likeness “are themselves likely” (29b–c).

49b applies it to the elements, and they fail. There is no “reliable and stable account” of which body is really water, because each keeps turning into the others.

50a–b is the question itself. “What is it?” is asked of something you can point to. Johansen reads the gold that way: “it is the point about the changeability of the shapes that gives Timaeus the conclusion that the receptacle is the only answer to the question of what the shapes are” (2004: 122 n. 8).

What passes the test is the receptacle, which “does not depart from its own character in any way” (50b), and, fully, the Form, which “keeps its own form unchangingly” (52a). What fails is fire, water, earth and air, which can only be called “what is such”.

What changes

In the Socratic dialogues a failed definition sent Socrates back to look for a better account. Here the failure is charged to the things. Bodies that keep turning into one another cannot carry a stable account, so a stable account must be of something else: the receptacle, and above all the Forms. This is the reading of this study, not a quotation.

05“This” and “such”: two readings

The Greek of 49c7–50b5 can be construed in two ways. Both make stability the test. They differ on what passes it.

Zeyl prints the traditional construal (T) in his translation and sets the alternative (A) beside it in his introduction (2000: lvi–lix). The difference turns on one clause, “mē touto alla to toiouton prosagoreuein pur” (49d5–7): “The Greek can be read either way” (lviii).

Traditional (T)Alternative (A)
49d“to characterize that, i.e., fire, not as ‘this,’ but each time as ‘what is such’”“to characterize not this, but what on each occasion is such, as ‘fire’”
50a“So fire—and generally everything that has becoming—it is safest to call ‘what is altogether such.’”“So what is altogether such it is safest to call ‘fire,’ and so with everything that has becoming.”
The claimPhenomenal fire is a “such”, not a “this”. Plato “is not telling us not to call them by their usual names … but to understand their status differently” (lviii).The name “fire” belongs only to what is always such. “To use a term such as ‘fire’ to refer to something is to imply that the referent has a stable character” (lviii–lix).
What the “such” isThe fire we see, correctly understoodFor most A-readers, “perpetually identical characteristics” (Cherniss) or “recurrent, stable and determinate characters” (Lee); a few say the Form (lix)
WhoGulley 1960; Zeyl 1975; Gill 1987; Johansen 2004Cherniss 1954, who first proposed it; Lee 1967; Mills 1968; Mohr 1980; support from Silverman 1992

The main objection to A is the count of kinds. Johansen: “Timaeus distinguishes three kinds where Cherniss’ reading would imply four” (2004: 120 n. 5). A-readers reply that Plato’s second kind was never the sensible particular as ordinarily understood (Zeyl 2000: lx).

Why the choice matters here

On A, the rule for names is the rule for definitions at full strength: a name like “fire” applies only to what has a stable character. On T, the elements keep their names but lose their standing as a “this”. Either way, stability is the test. Which reading fits the definitional reading of §04 better is an open question (§10).

06The argument for the Forms’ existence

It is short, and Plato says why. It comes only after the elements have failed the test.

Is our perpetual claim that there exists an intelligible Form for each thing a vacuous gesture, in the end nothing but mere talk? Now we certainly will not do justice to the question before us if we dismiss it, leaving it undecided and unadjudicated, and just insist that such things exist, but neither must we append a further lengthy digression to a discourse already quite long. Timaeus 51c, trans. Zeyl.
(P1) If understanding and true opinion are distinct, then the Forms exist.
“these ‘by themselves’ things definitely exist—these Forms, the objects not of our sense perception, but of our understanding only”. If they are not distinct, “all the things we perceive through our bodily senses must be assumed to be the most stable things there are” (51d).
(P2) They are distinct.
Four differences (51e): understanding comes by instruction, true belief by persuasion; understanding “always involves a true account”, true belief “lacks any account”; understanding is “unmoved by persuasion”, true belief “gives in”; all men share in true belief, but in understanding “only the gods and a small group of people”.
(C) So the Forms exist.
“that which keeps its own form unchangingly, which has not been brought into being and is not destroyed … is one thing. It is invisible” (52a).

Three things are worth keeping in view.

  1. Plato separates insisting from arguing. The earlier practice was a “perpetual claim”. Merely to “insist that such things exist” would not do the question justice (51c). This is the line Chapter 1 draws between saying and arguing.
  2. The Timaeus itself first posits, then argues. At 27d–28a Timaeus says “we must begin by making the following distinction” between what always is and what becomes. Here, at 51b–e, he argues for it.
  3. Kahn saw the same. “Perhaps his only direct argument for the existence of Forms is in Timaeus 51D–E … At Phaedo 74B–C there is an argument for the non-identity of Forms and sensible things … In general, however, Plato’s arguments proceed from the Forms, not to them” (1996: 330–31).

The step the critics attack, from two kinds of cognition to two kinds of object, and the scholars who use the passage, are in Chapter 1, §08.

Part Three

Old tools, new use

The passage feels new, but almost every tool in it comes from earlier dialogues. What is new is where Plato uses them: on physical bodies, and in an argument for the existence of Forms.

07What the Timaeus inherits

Seven parallels. Each earlier passage is quoted from Cooper (ed.) 1997, each Timaeus passage from Zeyl.

EarlierIn the Timaeus

08What is new

Three things. Aristotle noticed the first.

New in the Timaeus

The receptacle
A third kind, besides model and copy (48e–49a). Aristotle treats it as Plato’s attempt to say what place is (Physics 209b11–17; Claghorn 1954: 16).
The test run on bodies
The Socratic demand for a stable account is applied to fire, water, earth and air, not to proposed definitions of the virtues (§04).
An argument for existence
The one place where the Forms’ existence is the stated conclusion of an argument (§06).

What Aristotle objects to

Vagueness
What Plato wrote “is not based on any precisely-articulated conception” (Claghorn’s summary of On Generation and Corruption 329a14; 1954: 8).
Separation
Claghorn: “Plato does not state clearly whether the Receptacle exists in separation from the element” (on 329a14–15; 1954: 8).
No use
Claghorn: “Plato does not make any use of the Receptacle” (on 329a15; 1954: 9).

The physics is also framed in a new way. Because the world is a likeness, accounts of it “are themselves likely” (29c), and Timaeus asks his hearers to “accept the likely tale on these matters” (29d). Whether the argument at 51b–e falls inside or outside that likely account is left open (§10).

09Where this leaves Chapter 1

The Timaeus is the one text where the chain of Chapter 1 is run in full and its last link is argued.

LevelIn Chapter 1In the Timaeus
L1 · existenceConceded, agreed to, hypothesised or posited in the earlier dialoguesArgued at 51b–e, after being laid down at 27d–28a
L2 · the condition on an answerA stable account; the answer to “What is F?” must not changeStated at 29b, applied to the elements at 49b–50b
L3 · a theory of the categoryNot perceptible, unchanging, separateThree kinds, 52a–d: Form, copy, space

The Euthyphro has the L2 demand (Euth. 11b) but never runs it on bodies. The Timaeus does, and that is what carries it to L3. Chapter 1 takes the chain step by step in §§02–06 and maps it onto the three levels in §11.

10Open questions

What the next readings should settle. Add to this list as the study grows.

  1. T or A? Which reading of 49c7–50b5 fits the definitional reading of §04 better? Read Cherniss 1954 in full, then Gill 1987 and Silverman 1992.
  2. An answer to the Theaetetus? Is 49d–50a Plato’s reply to Theaetetus 183a–b, where universal flux leaves no word to use, not even “thus”? This was Owen’s second objection to the late date. Read Cherniss 1957.
  3. Inside or outside the likely account? Is the argument at 51b–e part of the “likely tale” (29d), or an account of what is stable, which 29b says should be “stable and unshifting”?
  4. Why so brief? Plato declines “a further lengthy digression” (51c). Is Republic V 476e–480a the long version?
  5. Aristotle’s objections. Are the three objections in §08 fair to the text? Read Claghorn’s first chapter in full.
  6. The ancient readers. How do Calcidius and Proclus read 48e–52d? The Proclus volume held covers only the opening of the dialogue.
