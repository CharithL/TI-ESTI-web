The Cogito Between Two Disciplines

How Descartes has been studied, how he ought to be, and where legitimate reconstruction turns into projection

Chat“Research task” (methodology)
Date2 August 2026 · DEscartes project
What the chat produced“The Cogito Between Two Disciplines: A Survey of the Historiography and Methodology of Descartes Scholarship”
Checked hereTwelve of the chat’s key sources are in your folder, including the Royaumont transcript and Hintikka 1996

Abstract

The first six chats reconstruct the cogito: as an object of doubt, an intuition, a performance, a two-tier certainty, a substance-theoretic inference. This chat asks by what right. It has two questions. One is descriptive: how has Descartes actually been studied? The other is normative: how ought he to be? Behind both is your worry: “Modern reconstructions of the cogito are not what Descartes said. … Where is the line between legitimate philosophical reconstruction and anachronistic projection?”

The chat’s answer. Descartes is claimed by two disciplines that will not merge, analytic epistemology and early modern intellectual history, and they produce “incompatible Descarteses.” The cogito is where the collision is sharpest and most checkable, because “Cogito, ergo sum” does not occur in the Meditations at all. The chat proposes constrained reconstruction under three rules: name the text; disclose the modern apparatus and show that a period substitute does the same work; and do not present an improvement as “what Descartes said.” On those rules Carriero and Menn survive fully, Frankfurt largely, and Dicker conditionally. Hintikka 1962 and the free-logic evaluations do not survive as exegesis.

What this review adds. The chat reported three of its load-bearing items as unverifiable. Your folder resolves all three. Hintikka’s 1996 confession is on the page: “What was said there may have been truth and nothing but the truth, but it was not the whole truth about the cogito” (RIP 1996, p. 15), in slightly different words from the ones the brief circulated. The Gueroult–Alquié exchange at Royaumont is in the Cahiers de Royaumont volume, p. 42, and the chat’s quotation is exact. The Williams chapter in Cottingham’s 1994 volume is confirmed from the volume itself, from its contents page and by reading its first page. A fourth finding complicates the chat’s taxonomy: Gueroult’s own method statement calls the Meditations “not … mere dry geometry, but the initiation of one soul by another soul acting as its guide” (p. 9).

The worry this chat was built around

“Modern reconstructions of the cogito are not what Descartes said. By what right does anyone reconstruct?”

Why Descartes is the test case, in the brief’s three reasons. He is claimed by two disciplines. He wrote in genres that do not say the same thing the same way (Regulae, Discours, Meditationes, Principia, letters, Replies). And “the most analysed sentence in his corpus may not be his”: cogito, ergo sum is not in the Meditations.

What the chat refuses is a neutral method. Its closing claim is that “on the cogito, more than almost anywhere else in the canon, the method you choose is not a lens on the object — it is a decision about what the object is.”

The thing that makes this chat worth reading twice

The object changes with the method. Gueroult and Alquié read the same sentence, ego sum, ego existo. For Gueroult it is “a load-bearing beam”; for Alquié it is “an opening onto the unsayable.” The chat calls this “the strongest single piece of evidence in the whole field that methodology is not neutral machinery but constitutive of the object.”

This page is where the series turns on itself. Chats 1–6 each chose a method, mostly rational reconstruction, and each produced its own cogito. Read after them, this chat asks which of those cogitos Descartes wrote.

The whole dispute in two sentences — Rorty’s two genres
Rational reconstruction
Treat the dead “as contemporaries, as colleagues with whom they can exchange views,” and give an account “in our terms.”
Historical reconstruction
Recover what the dead thinker meant in his own vocabulary, answering his own questions.
Rorty’s own position is ecumenical: do both, keep them separate, and be honest about which you are doing (pp. 49, 54 checked). The chat’s three rules are a way of enforcing that honesty on the cogito.

02Williams: “philosophy before it is history”

T1 · Williams 1994, the opening

“Discussing, some years ago, different ways of approaching the thought of Descartes, I made a broad distinction between two activities that I labelled ‘the history of ideas’ and ‘the history of philosophy’. The two are distinguished in the first place by their product. The history of ideas yields something that is history before it is philosophy, while with the history of philosophy it is the other way round. In particular, the product of the history of philosophy, being in the first place philosophy, admits more systematic regimentation of the thought under discussion.”

Williams, “Descartes and the Historiography of Philosophy,” in Cottingham (ed.), Reason, Will, and Sensation (1994), ch. 1 p. 19 checked by OCR

The attribution the brief asked to verify. The volume’s contents page lists “1. Descartes and the Historiography of Philosophy — Bernard Williams,” opening Part One, “Reason, History, and Method.” Cottingham is the editor. The chat confirmed this from Oxford Academic; your copy confirms it from the book. The next sentence adds what the chat does not quote: the history of ideas “naturally looks sideways to the context … to realize what their author might be doing,” while the history of philosophy “is more concerned to relate a philosopher’s conception to present problems.”

04The genre turn, and Gueroult’s own concession

The spiritual-exercise readers hold that the Meditations is a first-person exercise designed to transform the reader, so that extracting propositions and testing them misdescribes the text. The founding essays are in Amélie Rorty’s Essays on Descartes’ Meditations (1986), which is in your folder: Kosman, “The Naive Narrator” (p. 21), and Hatfield, “The Senses and the Fleshless Eye: The Meditations as Cognitive Exercises” (p. 45 checked). The chat’s question: if that is right, “what happens to the entire analytic literature on the cogito?”

A finding that complicates the taxonomy
The structural reader concedes the genre.

The chat sets Gueroult’s structural reading against the genre reading. But Gueroult’s own statement of method, the sentence the chat paraphrases as his “key formulation,” continues:

…there is then no other way of understanding the Meditations itself than to place in evidence the order by which it alone demonstrates the truths it contains. However, there is some difficulty in perceiving that order, for Descartes has not always chosen to make it immediately perceptible to us. The Meditations is not, in effect, mere dry geometry, but the initiation of one soul by another soul acting as its guide. Gueroult I, trans. Ariew, Introduction p. 9 checked

So for Gueroult, the meditative form is what hides the order and makes structural reading necessary. The two methods disagree about what the form is for, not about whether it is there.

05Gueroult and Alquié at Royaumont

The chat reports the exchange “via secondary sources.” The transcript is in your folder (first chapter / Cahiers de Royaumont 2 - Descartes (1957)). The colloquium was held in 1955 and published in 1957, which settles the date conflict the chat flagged. Here is the passage, as spoken:

T2 · Royaumont, the exchange

M. GUEROULT : Pour Descartes qu’est-ce qu’un être pensant qui n’est pas pensée ?
M. F. ALQUIÉ : Toute ma thèse consiste à affirmer que, chez Descartes, l’être n’est pas réductible au concept. … Je crois que l’être, l’existence ne se révèlent à la pensée qu’en une expérience familière, mais intraduisible. L’évidence du sum est première, et dépasse l’idée de pensée.
M. GUEROULT : Je n’ai jamais dit que l’être pensant était un concept. Le concept de pensée n’est pas la pensée, c’est la représentation que je me donne de la pensée.

Descartes, Cahiers de Royaumont, Philosophie II (Paris: Minuit, 1957) p. 42 checked

“What, for Descartes, is a thinking being that is not thought?” — “My whole thesis consists in affirming that, with Descartes, being is not reducible to the concept. … I believe that being, existence, reveal themselves to thought only in an experience that is familiar but untranslatable. The evidence of the sum is primary, and exceeds the idea of thought.” — “I never said the thinking being was a concept. The concept of thought is not thought; it is the representation I give myself of thought.” (This review’s translation.) The chat’s quotation of Alquié at p. 42 is exact. Gueroult’s reply, on the same page, shows why the exchange was a “dialogue of the deaf”: each accuses the other of reducing thought to a concept.

1

The first indubitable in the order of reasons. Its meaning is exhausted by its function in the demonstrative architecture. The intuited cogito is privileged; the remembered one is not (Chat 3 §11).

The confession, word for word

The brief circulated Hintikka’s confession as “how deep Descartes’s rootedness was in the ways of thinking of his masters and predecessors, up to and including Aristotle.” The English original says “how deeply Descartes was entrenched in the ways of thinking of his teachers and predecessors all the way back to Aristotle” (p. 15). The substance is the same. The chat was right to flag the wording as unconfirmed, and it should be quoted in the original form. Hintikka then adds: “I fully believe that Descartes grasped the performatory sources of the persuasiveness of the cogito. But even so, he somehow had to try to accommodate the cogito insight within the inherited scholastic…” He concedes the history. He does not withdraw the performance.

09Constrained reconstruction: three rules

Rule 1 · Name the text
The Discours “je pense, donc je suis,” the Principia “ego cogito, ergo sum” (explicitly inferential), and the Meditationes “ego sum, ego existo” (an intuition performed “whenever I conceive it”) are three different objects.
Rule 2 · Disclose and justify the apparatus
Speech acts, determinables, free logic: declare them, and show a period substitute (substance/mode, intuitus, the meditational first person) does the same work. If it cannot, label the result philosophy, not exegesis.
Rule 3 · Do not claim to report what Descartes said when you are improving on it
“Philosophy before it is history” is fine “so long as it is announced.”

11Where this leads

The line to keep

On the cogito, choosing a method is choosing an object. The honest course is to say which text, which apparatus, and whether the result is Descartes’ or ours.

The chat’s biggest gap is the French side of Test 5, which it could only report second-hand. Chat 8 goes after it: what of Alquié exists in English, and what the Royaumont quarrel actually was.
