Plato’s Metaphysics Chapter 1 The Missing Link

Why “What is F?” does not by itself give you a Form: the five steps that get you there, the two claims Plato leaves unargued in the middle dialogues, and the one place he states them as a conclusion

What this chapter shows

Failing to say what F is does not lead to a Form by itself. It leads there only through further premises — and even then the chain delivers a definiendum that is not sensible, not an object that exists, let alone one that exists separately.

Steps 1 and 2 (the priority of definition; a definition that never fails) are Socratic. Step 3 (compresence) and step 4 (so the definition is not of anything sensible) are argued in the Phaedo and Republic. Step 5 hides two further claims — that there is such an object, and that it is separate — which the middle dialogues agree to, hypothesise or posit, but do not argue.

The one place Plato states that Forms exist as the conclusion of an argument is Timaeus 51b–52a: if understanding (nous) differs from true belief, “these things do by all means exist, themselves by themselves” (51d; trans. Moss 2021: 224).

Charith’s own questions and arguments are labelled as his. Quotations were checked against the books in the library, and page numbers are the printed ones. Anything reported from general knowledge rather than a held source is marked reported.

Part One

The chain

Does the Socratic failure to say what piety or justice is lead, by itself, to Plato’s Forms? This part writes the route out as an argument, one premise per step, and asks of each step what it establishes and who argues for it.

00The question

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 1“Considering the problem generally (not just in Dancy): the difficulty the conversations run into — that no one can say coherently what piety is — is that what leads to the creation of the Form? Socrates needs to live rightly → needs to know what is right → needs the Form of Right.” (Spelling lightly edited.)
The answer

The first arrow is Socrates’ own: to live rightly one must know what the right is. The second arrow does not follow. Knowing what justice is requires a definition; nothing yet requires a Form. A correct definition could in principle be an account of a concept, of a property that sensible things share, or of a separately existing Form. Which of these it has to be is exactly what the argument must settle.

So the step needs further premises. Written out in full, the chain runs:

The chain, written out

  1. Live rightly → need to know what the right is. The priority of definition, or the “Intellectualist Assumption” (IA).
  2. Know what the right is → need a definition that never fails.
  3. Every sensible candidate is also its opposite: just in one respect, unjust in another (compresence: Phaedo 74b–c, Republic V 479a–d, Hippias Major 289a–d), or always changing (Aristotle’s flux).
  4. So the definition cannot be of anything sensible.
  5. So there is a non-sensible object that is never unjust → the Form.

Where the chain breaks

Steps 1–4 are valid as far as they go, but their conclusion is a condition on the definiendum: whatever F is, it is not a sensible thing or property. That is not yet an object. Step 5 adds existence (“there is such a thing as the F”) and, on the usual reading, separation (“it can exist whether or not the sensibles do”). Neither follows from steps 1–4. Dancy has to state existence as a separate premise (ARE) of his own reconstruction (2004: 18), and Fine insists that “Difference does not imply separation” (1993: 60).

02Step 1 · The priority of definition

The first arrow in the chain is Socrates’ own. It creates the need for a definition. It does not yet say what the definition is a definition of.

(IA) To know that . . . F —, one must be able to say what the F, or F-ness, is. Dancy 2006: 72. He calls this the “Intellectualist Assumption”, “often referred to elsewhere as the ‘Socratic Fallacy’ or the ‘Principle of the Priority of Definition’”, citing Benson 1990 and 2000: 112–63. Texts: Euthyphro 4d9–e8, 5c8–d5, 6d6–e7, 15c11–e1.

Socrates, Dancy adds, “gives us a reason for insisting on it when we are trying to determine how to live” (2006: 72). That reason is the first arrow of Charith’s chain.

Geach 1966 · the “Socratic fallacy”

“(A) that if you know you are correctly predicating a given term ‘T’ you must ‘know what it is to be T,’ in the sense of being able to give a general criterion for a thing’s being T; (B) that it is no use to try and arrive at the meaning of ‘T’ by giving examples of things that are T.”

Geach 1966: 371. The style of thinking that comes from these two assumptions “may well be called the Socratic fallacy” (ibid.).
Geach’s (A) is (IA) in semantic dress. Geach himself notes that one “can fall into it independently of any theory of Forms” (371). That is the point of this step: the priority of definition is a demand on knowledge, and the demand can be stated without any ontology at all.

What step 1 gives, and what it does not

It gives a need for a definition. It does not give an object. Dancy puts the existential side of it exactly: “It is clearly a presupposition of the whole enterprise that there are things to be defined” (2004: 14). A presupposition of the enterprise is not a conclusion of any step in it. That is why, in Dancy’s reconstruction, existence re-enters later as a premise, not as a result (§05).

A second route to the Forms: through the Meno

(IA) has a cost that the Meno makes visible: if one cannot know anything about F without its definition, how can one search for the definition at all? Dancy reads the Meno as the place where Plato gives (IA) up: “He is, it appears, now prepared to allow that we can use terms in the absence of an explicit definition, and that, when definitions are required, our way of getting at them is due to our prenatal grasp of what is to be defined” (2006: 81).

The Phaedo then attaches recollection to Forms: “our ability to answer ‘what is it?’ questions … depends on our prenatal acquaintance with a special realm of objects not encountered by the senses. These objects are the Forms” (Dancy 2006: 81–82). On this route, the Forms enter as the objects of a prenatal acquaintance, not as the conclusion of the five-step chain.

03Step 2 · A definition that never fails

What Socrates asks of an answer. These requirements are already in the early dialogues. Together they rule out any answer that is sometimes F and sometimes not.

Irwin 1999 · four features of the form, from the Euthyphro

“1. There must be just one form of piety for all pious things (actions, people, and so on) (5d1-5). 2. It must be usable as a paradigm that we can consult to say whether something is pious or not (6e3-6). 3. It must be describable without explicit reference to properties—just and unjust, fine and shameful, good and bad—that introduce disputes (7c10-d7). 4. The single ‘form’ (eidos) or ‘character’ (idea) of piety is that ‘by which all pious things are pious’ (6d9).”

Irwin 1999: 145 (in Fine ed. 1999; derived from Irwin 1995, ch. 10).

The fourth feature does the work. If the form is what makes pious things pious, it must never also make things impious. Irwin states the requirement Plato later makes explicit:

Irwin 1999 · the explanatory requirement

“A property G cannot be the explanation of x’s being F if either (1) G is present in y, but y is not-F or (2) G is not present in z, but z is F (97a5-b3, 100e8-101b2).”

Irwin 1999: 159. Fine gives the same condition: “if x is F and not F, it cannot explain why anything is F” (1993: 58).
Irwin is careful that the early dialogues do not yet state this as a general principle: “None of these passages, then, states the general principle that the F cannot be both F and not-F, whereas various candidates for being the F are in fact both F and not-F, and therefore cannot be the F. The Hippias Major comes closer to stating this general principle (291d1-3)” (1999: 159).

Dancy locates the same requirement inside his Argument from Relativity (§05): “Premise (ARF) figures as a criterion of adequacy for a correct definition, and instances of (ARO) are used in showing that various proposed definitions fail to meet that criterion” (2004: 19).

One disagreement to keep in view

Are these requirements already a theory of Forms? Allen says yes. Socrates “assumes, in pursuing his inquiry, that there is an ἰδέα, or εἶδος, a Form, of holiness”, a universal, a standard and an essence: “These assumptions constitute a theory of Forms” (1970: 67–68). “Logically, Forms play a regulative role in dialectic: as antecedents of ‘it’ in the question ‘What is it?’ they determine the kinds of answer which are acceptable, and more importantly, unacceptable” (68). Dancy says no: conceding that there is such a thing as the F “is not a piece of metaphysics, but simply a matter of fixing the topic for discussion” (2004: 19). The argument of this chapter does not need to settle that dispute. On either reading, step 2 is a condition on answers; it does not yet say that the answer names a separately existing object.

04Step 3 · Compresence, and flux

The first premise about sensible things, and the only step Plato argues from what we observe. It says nothing yet about Forms.

The texts are Phaedo 74b7–c5 (equal sticks and stones), Republic V 479a5–b10 (the many beautifuls are also ugly), Hippias Major 289a2–c6 and 293b5–e5, Symposium 211a2–5, and the “finger” passage at Republic VII 523a–524d.

Irwin 1999 · what compresence is

“Plato attributes ‘compresence of opposites’ (as some modern writers call it), not merely compresence of opposite appearances, to the many Fs. … A beautiful (kalon) girl is also ugly (aischron) in comparison with gods (Hp.Ma. 289a2-c6). Burying our parents and being buried by our children are fine (kalon) in some circumstances and shameful (aischron) in others (293b5-e5). The many beautifuls (justs, equals, and so on) are both beautiful and ugly (Rep. 479a5-b10).”

Irwin 1999: 153. On the burial example he adds: “In this case, compresence of opposites is attributed to types, not to tokens of the types” (154).
Of the Phaedo’s formula for beauty, Irwin says that “bright colour makes things beautiful and ugly” conveys Plato’s “main point most accurately” (1999: 160). The candidate fails as a cause. That is why step 3 connects to the explanatory requirement of step 2.

Flux is compresence

Aristotle reports that Plato posited Forms because sensibles are in flux (Met. A.6 987a32–b10; M.4 1078b12–32; M.9 1086a37–b11). The three commentators agree that the flux in question is mostly compresence, not change over time:

ScholarPageClaim
Dancy 200419“Flux as it appears in Plato is not straightforward change as you and I conceive it: baldly put, flux is relativity.”
Irwin 1999169“If his arguments appeal only to compresence, not to change over time, we are justified in concluding that the type of flux he attributes to sensibles in arguing for nonsensible Forms is compresence.”
Fine 199357“in the middle dialogues Plato takes compresence but not succession to require the existence of forms.”
CHARITH’S QUESTION — 2“What would happen if sensible things did not contradict each other — or if the contradiction itself showed what the thing is? Would we still need the Form?”
The answer

If there were no compresence, step 3 would fail and this route to the Forms would close. Plato grants as much for some predicates. In Republic VII, sight never reports that a finger is also not a finger, so “finger” does not provoke the question “What is it?”. Fine: “only some predicates have sensible instances that suffer narrow compresence—‘thick’ and ‘thin’ are such predicates, but ‘finger’ is not” (1993: 59). Irwin draws the consequence: “no Forms for (A) are mentioned; and if the argument about equals relies on the problems raised in Republic VII, Plato should not require them” (1977: 150).

If the contradiction itself showed what the thing is, you would have the Heracleitean view: justice just is returning and not returning what you borrowed. Plato’s reply, on Irwin’s reading, is that “the Heracleitean confuses different embodiments of justice in different circumstances with the property that is embodied in these different ways” (1999: 170). The reply still needs step 2: a single property that is never also its opposite.

05Step 4 · So the definition cannot be of anything sensible

The conclusion of the compresence argument. It is a claim of difference: what F is, is not the same as any sensible F.

Dancy’s reconstruction makes the logical form explicit. It is the Phaedo 74b–c argument generalised; at 74b4–c6 the F is the Equal (2006: 82):

(ARE) There is such a thing as the F.
The “Existential presupposition”: it “expresses Socrates’ presupposition that there were things to be defined” (Dancy 2004: 18).
(ARO) Any ordinary F is also conF.
“fill in here the predicate contrary to ‘F’” — step 3, compresence.
(ARF) The F is never conF.
Step 2: the criterion of adequacy for a definition.
∴ (ARC) The F is not the same as any ordinary F.
Step 4. Dancy 2004: 18; also 2006: 71 (Beautiful) and 82 (Equal).

“Although the Argument from Relativity first appears in the Phaedo, the materials for its construction are to be found in the Socratic dialogues” (Dancy 2004: 19). The existential premise sits above the argument. The argument does not produce it.

Irwin 1977 · Phaedo 74 in three steps

“1. Equal sticks and stones sometimes being the same appear equal to one man, and not to another (74b7-10). 2. But the equals themselves have never appeared to you unequal, or equality inequality (74c1-3). 3. Then these equals (sc. the sticks and stones) and the equal itself are not the same (74c4-5).”

Irwin 1977: 149.
Irwin’s later statement of the conclusion: “no sensible property can satisfy the metaphysical demand, since no sensible property can be the appropriate single explanatory property” (1999: 164); “Since justice itself cannot also be unjust in a different context, it cannot be identical to these sensible properties that embody it” (1999: 169–70).

Fine reaches the same result by way of explanation: “Since some sensible properties of F suffer compresence, reference to them does not explain why anything is F, and so they cannot be what F-ness is. Since explanation is possible, in these cases things are F in virtue of a non-sensible property, the form of F” (1993: 58). And she marks the limit: “The argument from compresence shows that forms are different from both sensible particulars and sensible properties” (60). It shows that they are different, and no more than that.

What step 4 establishes

A category condition: whatever answers “What is F?” is not a sensible thing or a sensible property. The conclusion is a non-identity — (ARC), “not the same as”. It is not an existence claim and not a separation claim. In the three-level terms of §11, steps 2–4 argue at level 2.

06Step 5 · Existence and separation: the gap

Step 5 says more than step 4. “So there is a non-sensible object that is never unjust” contains two claims that steps 1–4 do not supply.

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 3“So here, step 3 does not mean the Form exists separately — yes or no?”
The answer

Yes: step 3 does not mean the Forms exist separately. Step 3 is only about sensible things. Even step 5 gives you a non-sensible object that is different from them, not one that could exist without them. That extra step needs a further premise.

Claim A · Existence
A
There is such a thing as the F.
Dancy’s (ARE) (2004: 18) and (HF) (300). In every reconstruction below except Fine’s, this is a premise or an assumption, never a conclusion.
Claim B · Separation
B
The F can exist whether or not sensible Fs exist.
“A is separate from B just in case A can exist without B—that is, just in case A can exist whether or not B exists or, equivalently, just in case A exists independently of B” (Fine 1993: 51).

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 4“But then Dancy 2006 says: ‘Aristotle also tells us that Plato’s adoption of Socrates’ quest for definitions took a special turn: Plato made the objects of definition, “forms,” distinct or separate from perceptible things.’ What do you say?”
The answer

That sentence (Dancy 2006: 70) reports Aristotle; it is not Dancy’s argument. Its “or” joins two different claims. Distinct means that the F is not the same as any sensible F, which is what the Argument from Relativity proves: “(ARC) The Beautiful is not the same as any ordinary beautiful [thing]” (2006: 71). Separate means that the F can exist whether or not any sensible F exists (Fine 1993: 51), which nothing in the argument addresses.

The 2004 book shows he means “distinct”: distinctness is “at least part” of separation (13); separation rests on imperceptibility (247); and the flux argument is called “an argument for the existence of separate Forms” (14), though its conclusion is that “the forms are distinct from perceptible things” (14). The slide goes back to Aristotle: Met. A.6 “says only that flux … shows that forms are different (hetera …) from sensibles; separation is not mentioned” (Fine 1993: 60). Fine’s “Difference does not imply separation” applies directly to Dancy’s “or”.

CHARITH’S ARGUMENT — 1“Let us assume the sensible beautiful is Sb and its Form is FB. Since Sb exists, no one argues about that. But that Sb differs from FB does not show that FB exists — unless Plato says it himself. Without FB in the ontology it would still be logically coherent to talk about FB and its relation to Sb. Therefore the Timaeus statement is far more crucial evidence.” (Lightly edited.)
The response

The logic is right. “Sb ≠ FB” does not show that FB exists. One can talk coherently about FB and its relation to Sb — as a standard, a concept, or an ideal — without FB being in the ontology. That is why Dancy must add existence as a separate premise (ARE), and why Fine says that difference arguments do not give separation.

Plato raises the same worry himself, at Timaeus 51c: “Is it in vain on each occasion that we say there is a certain intelligible Form of each thing, and is this nothing but a word?” (trans. Prior 1985: 113). The argument at 51d–e is aimed at exactly that possibility (§08).

One correction: Plato does say that Forms exist in other places — Phd. 65d, 100b; Rep. 596a, and some twenty more passages (§07) — but as agreements, hypotheses, posits and the like. Only Timaeus 51d–e makes existence the stated conclusion of an argument. It is therefore the strongest evidence of Plato’s commitment, though not a proof that Forms exist, and it cannot by itself tell us what the early dialogues were committed to.

What step 5 needs

An existence premise (there is such a thing as the F) and, for Plato’s Forms, a separation premise (it can exist without sensibles). Steps 1–4 supply neither. In the middle dialogues, existence is agreed to, hypothesised or posited, and separation is asserted, typically through “itself by itself”, without argument. The next part looks at where Plato says it and at the one place where it is the conclusion of an argument.

Part Two

Where Plato says it, and where he argues it

Plato says that Forms exist in many places. The literature argues over four of them: in three the existence claim is agreed to, hypothesised or posited, and only in the fourth is it the conclusion of an argument. About twenty more passages, from the early dialogues to the Seventh Letter, concede it, posit it, reveal it in a speech or a myth, raise it as a question, or argue for it only conditionally.

The pattern

At 65d, existence is agreed to without argument. At 100b it is hypothesised; Dancy’s (HF) has the same form as his (ARE). At 596a it is “posited”, and scholars even dispute whether the sentence asserts existence at all. Of these four, only Timaeus 51d–e is read as an argument that concludes existence.

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 5“Do any other Platonic dialogues say anything about the existence of Forms? Search the folder.” (Lightly edited.)
The answer

Yes, many. The search turned up about twenty more passages: in eleven other dialogues and the Seventh Letter, and further passages in the Phaedo, Republic and Timaeus. None of them does what Timaeus 51d–e does. In each one the existence of Forms is conceded, agreed, posited, revealed in a speech or a myth, raised as a question, or argued for only conditionally.

What the other twenty add

Timaeus 51d–e is still the only passage where existence is the stated conclusion of an argument. Cratylus 440 and Parmenides 135b–c are “if … then” arguments that never assert the conclusion. Republic V argues for existence only on Fine’s reading, and Parmenides 132a states an inference only to attack it. White makes the same point: “nowhere in Plato’s works … does one find what could be called a full-fledged argument for the existence of Forms fully spelled out. There are fragments of such arguments in, for example, Phaedo 73-74 and the much later Parmenides 132a and 135b-c” (1976: 6). He does not mention Timaeus 51.

The early concessions are the existence presupposition said out loud (level 1 in §11). In the Hippias Major the “What is it?” question comes straight after. Vlastos contrasts existence “regularly conceded … without contest” in these dialogues with “a highly contestable thesis” in the middle dialogues (1991: 63).

Separation. Only two of these passages call the Forms “separate”: Parmenides 130b and Sophist 248a. Fine says 130b does not use the word “in the relevant sense” (2003: 34 n. 71).

Left out. Republic X 597c–d argues that each Form is unique, not that it exists. Aristotle’s reports of Platonist arguments, as in the Peri Ideōn, are not in the dialogues; Fine 1993 examines them.

08The Timaeus argument, and who uses it

The one passage where the existence of Forms is the conclusion of an argument. Timaeus raises the rival view before answering it: that talk of Forms is “nothing but a word”.

Is there any such thing as Fire just in itself, or any of the things of which we are always speaking thus: that each of them is just in itself? Or do those things at which we look, and all other things which we perceive by the body, alone have this sort of reality, but there is nothing else besides these in any way at any time? Is it in vain on each occasion that we say there is a certain intelligible Form of each thing, and is this nothing but a word? Timaeus 51b7–c5, trans. Prior 1985: 113. Crombie reads the rival view as holding that universals are “only ‘expressions we use’ (logoi)” (1963: 35).
If reason and opinion are different things, then Forms imperceptible by us, objects of thought alone, must exist by themselves; but if true opinion differs in no wise from reason, the things we perceive by means of the body must be deemed to be the most secure realities. Now we must say that reason and opinion are two things, since they differ both in origin and in nature. The one is produced by teaching, the other by persuasion; we can justify the one by true reasoning, the other is unreasoned; the one cannot be moved by persuasion, the other can; only God and a few men possess the one, all men possess the other. We must therefore agree that there are Forms distinct from the copies of them. Timaeus 51d3–52a4, as given by Ross 1951: 124. Ross: “Plato here treats as the essential reason for believing in the existence of Forms the difference between knowledge and true opinion” (124–25).

Its structure

(P1) If nous and true opinion are two different kinds, then Forms exist, themselves by themselves.
The conditional at 51d3–7. Its alternative is stated too: if they are not different, sensibles are “the most secure realities”.
(P2) Nous and true opinion are two different kinds.
Supported by what Broadie calls “four brisk reasons” (51e1–6): origin (teaching vs persuasion), account (a true logos vs none), stability (unmoved vs moved by persuasion), and distribution (the gods and a few men vs all).
(C) So Forms exist, themselves by themselves.
“these things do by all means exist, themselves by themselves” (51d; trans. Moss 2021: 224). Cherniss reads it as “an avowedly brief and casual proof of the separate existence of Ideas” (in Vlastos ed. 1971: 20).

Broadie sets out the logic as two questions: the “criterial question” — “are intelligence and true opinion two kinds (or is there only true opinion)?” — and the “original question”, whether there are Forms of fire and the like. “The answer, affirmative or negative, to CQ will yield the corresponding answer to OQ” (2012: 275). Pitteloud writes it as modus ponens, but with a weaker conclusion: “(P1) If intellect and true opinion are two different kinds (duo genē), then Forms are different from sensible objects. (P2) Intellect is different (chōris) from true opinion. (C) Forms are different from sensible objects” (2025: 189 n. 11).

The step the critics attack

The move from two faculties to two kinds of object. Kahn calls it “Plato’s characteristic realism: how could two cognitive faculties be distinct if they did not have distinct objects?” (2013: 195). Pitteloud: “The truth of (P1) is not evident as it is possible to conceive of two different faculties of the soul relating to the same object” (2025: 189 n. 11). Crombie: “it does not pretend to be conclusive. It rests on the admittedly controvertible fact that understanding is not the same thing as right opinion” (1963: 521). Annas: Plato “does not consider the option that our conception of knowledge might not answer to anything” (2003: 84). Against Crombie, Vlastos calls the argument “as pure an example of ‘deductive metaphysics’ as one could hope to find” (1981: 376).

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 6“So in the Platonic dialogues Plato never directly argues for Forms? Sometimes he asks what the model or ousia is, but he never makes the metaphysical case explicitly?”
The answer

Mostly yes. In the early and middle dialogues the Forms’ existence is assumed, agreed to or hypothesised (Phd. 65d, 100b; Rep. 596a), and arguments like Phd. 74 show only that the F differs from sensible things. The clear exception is the late Timaeus (51b–e). Rep. V 476e–480a comes close.

The scholars agree. Prior: “In the early and middle dialogues, he does not regard the existence of the Forms as subject to serious philosophical doubt” (1985: 113). Crombie: “the doctrine that there are forms is nowhere the object of what purports to be a deductive argument. Usually it is put forward as an article of the Socratic faith. There is indeed an argument for it in the Timaeus (51–2)” (1963: 521). Teloh: “Plato never questions that Forms exist” (1981: 180). On Republic V, Cross & Woozley call the argument “implicit in Book V” (in Vlastos ed. 1971: 90), and White thinks Rep. 477ff. “more substantial” than the Timaeus (1976: 25 n. 33).

Part Three

Why posit existing Forms at all?

If a definition is all that “What is F?” needs, why did Plato want an existing object as well? This part lists the work the existence claim does for him, asks whether the analogy with numbers in science helps, and maps the whole chain onto the three levels of the question.

09What Plato would lose without them

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 7“But then why did Plato decide there should be a Form that exists? Without the Form existing — just by having a separate definition — can we answer the What is F? question? If we remove the metaphysical commitment, what problem would Plato have faced?”
The answer

On Plato’s own terms, the existing Form does work that a definition by itself cannot do. Remove it and each of the following goes missing:

Aristotle’s answer to the same pressure

Aristotle accepts the need and rejects the separation. He says “it is not possible to acquire knowledge without the universal” (Met. M.9, as quoted by Fine 1993: 60). But in his view “only substance particulars are separate”, and universals exist when and only when they are instantiated (Fine 1993: 61). So every item in the table can be met by a stable, non-sensible definiendum that is not a separately existing Form. The pressure runs from level 2 toward some level-3 theory of the definiendum, but it does not by itself select Plato’s theory.

10The comparison with numbers in science

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 8“But let us take numbers, or abstract physical entities in science. We don’t need them to be assumed to exist as Forms for science to work?”
The answer

Correct, at least in practice. Science works without anyone deciding whether numbers exist, and idealisations like point masses are openly treated as fictions. Field’s Science Without Numbers (1980) and van Fraassen’s The Scientific Image (1980) go further: they argue that mathematics and unobservable entities only have to be useful, or match what we observe, not be real reported.

The other side is the Quine–Putnam indispensability argument: you are committed to whatever your best theory has to say exists (Quine’s criterion, the one Prior uses) reported. That is the same kind of argument Plato makes at Parmenides 135b–c. Joan Kung ties it to the Timaeus as well: Plato “thinks we shall have no science and no knowledge at all without positing the existence of other abstract items in addition to perceptible ones, as indeed Plato himself says in the Timaeus (51d3–7)” (1989: 320). So in both cases the real question is whether the thing is truly indispensable.

Even if Quine is right, science would be committed to abstract objects, not to Forms. Modern abstract objects are not models that things copy, they are not instances of their own property (the number 2 is not two of anything), and they cause nothing. So here too the pressure goes from level 2 to some level-3 theory, not necessarily Plato’s.

11The chain on the three levels

The three levels of the ti esti question (see What Justifies the Question?) sort the five steps by what each one needs and what each one delivers.

Existence presupposition: “There is such a thing as the F”
The presupposition of the question itself: Dancy’s (ARE) (2004: 18) and (HF) (300). In the dialogues it is conceded (Prt. 330c; H.Ma. 287c–d), agreed to (Phd. 65d, 74a), hypothesised (Phd. 100b) or posited (Rep. 596a); see §07. Steps 1–4 presuppose it; none of them proves it.
Presupposed, not argued
Category condition: the F is one, explanatory, never also not-F, so not sensible
Steps 2–4. The requirement is Socratic; the compresence premise and the conclusion of non-identity are argued in Phaedo 74 and Republic V. This is where Plato does his arguing.
Argued
Theory of the category: separate, eternal Forms, “themselves by themselves”
Asserted in the middle dialogues without argument (Fine 1993: 61; Vlastos 1991: 76). Argued only at Timaeus 51d–e, and even there the reading is disputed: existence (Cherniss, Ross, Kahn), difference only (Pitteloud), or imperceptibility only (Modrak).
Argued only at Timaeus 51
In one sentence

The Socratic failure to say what F is creates a demand for a definition that no sensible thing can meet. That demand fixes a category, not an object: the existence of the Form is presupposed, its separation is asserted, and only the Timaeus states either as the conclusion of an argument.
