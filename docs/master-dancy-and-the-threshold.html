Master document · one opponent, examined at length

Dancy and the Threshold

The criterion he does set, the swimming pool, and the page that is quoted by half

Why this document exists, and whose charge it states

R. M. Dancy holds that where Socrates is concerned with definitions “he is not concerned with metaphysics at all.” The review of Prior’s chapter treats that denial in two sections and moves on. It will not stay treated. Dancy is the only one of the five opponents who has written a three-hundred-page book on the question, and he is the only one whose denial rests on a device rather than a thesis — a two-page analogy about swimming strokes, given once, never defended, and never revisited.

The charge examined here is Charith’s, and it is prior to anything in the text. To say of a conversation that it is not metaphysics is to apply a criterion. Dancy nowhere states one. So either he has a criterion he does not state, in which case it should be extracted and examined; or he has none, in which case the denial is not a finding but a report of how the passage strikes him. This document shows that the first is true — the criterion is there, forty-seven pages before the pool — and that once it is stated, the denial cannot do the work it has been asked to do.

The result, in one line. Dancy’s threshold is possession of the Argument from Relativity; his three instruments all detect what a conversation is about; and what Allen asserts is a matter of what a conversation is committed to. Aboutness-detectors are blind to commitments by construction. That is why Dancy and Allen have never touched, in fifty years of citing each other.

00The argument as a map

Read §02 first. Everything in Part Two depends on the criterion recovered there.

The shape of the whole thing

Dancy’s denial has three moving parts, and they are in three different chapters. In ch. 1 he fixes what would count as metaphysics: the Argument from Relativity, an argument from the Phaedo. In ch. 3 he shows by analogy that the vocabulary of the early dialogues — existence, unity, the causal idiom — can occur with no metaphysics attached. In ch. 4 he answers Allen in a footnote, by pointing out that forms play no role in whether Socrates’ arguments are valid.

Each part is sound against a different opponent, and none of them is sound against Allen. The criterion is sound against anyone who thinks the word “form” settles the matter. The pool is sound against Burnet, who reads ontology straight off a dative. The validity test is sound against anyone claiming forms as premises. Allen claims none of these things: he claims the ontology is what makes the question askable. No test for what an argument states can find that.

What Dancy establishes
1
The idioms do not entail an ontology
A conversation can use “there is,” count its kinds, and say that someone is an F-er because he does F, and be about nothing but swimming p. 66.
2
Forms are not premises
“If his arguments work, they do so because his premises are true and his conclusions follow from them … the question of validity has nothing to do with forms” p. 80 n. 3.
3
The topic is never thematised
“There is no discussion of such questions in the Socratic dialogues” p. 67. True, and not in dispute.
What he needs, and does not establish
4
That virtues are relevantly like strokes
Carried by “just as” and “any more than” p. 66, and by nothing else anywhere in the book.
5
That non-mention is non-commitment
The step from “nothing has been said about” to “another thing entirely from doing metaphysics” is taken inside one paragraph and never argued.
6
That his own criterion is not question-begging
The test for metaphysics is possession of a middle-dialogue argument. Applied to an early dialogue it can return only one answer.
One concession made at the outset, so the rest is not read as hostile. Against the position it was built to defeat, Dancy’s pool is decisive, and the review says so at §12. What follows is not that Dancy is careless. It is that a device aimed at one opponent has been used for fifty years against a second opponent it was never shaped to hit.

Sections 01–06the denial · the criterion · the pool · the requirements · the footnote · the pattern
01

Dancy’s argument, as he gives it

The first job is to state the position without the objections, and to state it better than its summaries do. Dancy is usually reported as holding that the early dialogues contain a theory of definition rather than a theory of forms. That is true but thin. What makes his book an argument rather than an assertion is the pair of devices in ch. 1 and ch. 3 — a stated criterion for when a text has crossed into metaphysics, and a worked counterexample showing that the usual evidence does not cross it.

Both are easy to miss. The criterion is given forty-seven pages before it is used, in a section headed as a “forward glance” at dialogues the book has not reached. The counterexample is a joke about a swimming pool.

Bottom line: Dancy is not silent about the threshold. He is silent about the fact that he has set one.

01What Dancy denies, and what he does not

Four distinctions the secondary literature runs together, and which Dancy himself keeps carefully apart.

The denial is narrower than it is usually reported to be, and the narrowness is deliberate. Dancy sets it out in his own voice in ch. 3, immediately after the pool:

The question is not about what Plato believed when he wrote the Socratic dialogues. It is about what he does in those dialogues: what is involved in the arguments of those dialogues. In particular, I am not saying: the Socrates of these dialogues would not have conceded the existence of forms, of which things here below are merely pale reflections. If the Socrates of these dialogues is the historical Socrates, and Aristotle is right about the historical Socrates, he would have made no such concession. He might well have wondered what someone who asked for such a concession was on about … At any rate, there is no discussion of such questions in the Socratic dialogues. Dancy, Plato’s Introduction of Forms p. 67
  1. Not a claim about Plato’s mind. “When I deny (as I shall) that the Euthyphro has a ‘Theory of Forms,’ I do not suppose that I am commenting on what was or was not in Plato’s mind at the time he wrote the Euthyphro. I am talking only about what is required by the argument of the Euthyphro” pp. 11–12.
  2. Not a claim about dates. “The development in question is in the first instance a logical one” p. 4; and the groups are relabelled “Socratic” and “doctrinal” because “I am emphasizing argument rather than date” p. 5.
  3. Not a denial that virtues exist. The existence of the things to be defined is granted, and granted twice — at p. 19 as “fixing the topic,” at p. 66 as what “of course” the defining requires.
  4. Not a verdict on their ontological status. “Well, that is another question, and we need not go into it yet. Socrates does not go into it yet. He will, in the doctrinal dialogues” p. 67.

What is left, once the four are subtracted

Exactly one claim: the arguments of the Socratic dialogues do not require an ontology, and the dialogues do not discuss one. Everything Dancy is famous for denying is a consequence someone else drew from that.

It is worth noticing how much this concedes. On this statement Dancy is not an opponent of the claim that the Euthyphro’s idiom carried ontological weight for the Greeks who used it. He has declined to take a view. The review calls that Project B′ at its §19; here it is Dancy in his own words, standing outside it.

The opponent, named on the page where the denial is stated

The 2006 short statement makes the target explicit, in a parenthesis Prior does not quote:

The latter two especially will feed into the Theory of Forms. They do not, however, entail that theory; where Socrates is concerned with definitions, he is not concerned with metaphysics at all (against this see, e.g., Allen 1970). We shall see the turn toward metaphysics when we get to the Phaedo. Dancy, “Platonic Definitions and Forms” 2006: 71

So the confrontation is not something the review has arranged. Dancy names Allen 1970 as the position he is denying, on the sentence where he denies it — and in the 2004 book he names the page: Allen 1970: 68, at p. 80 n. 3. That page is read in full at §13, and it does not say what the footnote takes it to say.

02The threshold he does set — the Argument from Relativity

The criterion is in chapter 1, in a section about dialogues the book has not yet reached. This is the section on which everything here turns.

It is regularly said — by Zeyl in his review, by Prior quoting him, and by the review at its §04 — that Dancy never says what makes a theory metaphysical. That is true of any definition. It is not true of his practice. Forty-seven pages before the pool, he names the thing that has to be present:

AR — the Argument from Relativity

“The pattern of argument we shall see emerging from the Socratic dialogues and made explicit in Phaedo 74a–c, whose upshot is to contrast forms and perceptible objects” p. 18.

(ARE) There is such a thing as the F.
(ARO) Any ordinary F is also conF.
(ARF) The F is never conF.
∴ (ARC) The F is not the same as any ordinary F.

And then, on the next page, the line that does the work of a definition:

Interlocutors in Socratic dialogues sometimes concede (ARE) as a preliminary to discussing the F … The concession by itself is not a piece of metaphysics, but simply a matter of fixing the topic for discussion. We shall consider cases of this kind below. But the rest of the argument is intended to bring out what … we committed ourselves to in conceding the truth of (ARE): the Theory of Forms. Dancy p. 19

The same criterion reappears in ch. 3, in the sentence that launches the survey the pool was staged to justify:

“That argument begins by affirming the existence of something and then investing the affirmation with metaphysical significance. Let us look at some cases in the Socratic dialogues in which the existence of something is affirmed without the subsequent apparatus that turns the affirmation into metaphysics.” pp. 67–8
DANCY’S THRESHOLD, STATED

A text is doing metaphysics when the existence claim has been joined by the rest of AR — the premise about ordinary Fs and the premise about the F. Existence alone is topic-fixing. Existence plus the apparatus is metaphysics. The apparatus is a particular argument, first made explicit at Phaedo 74a–c.

This is a real criterion, it is operable, and it is the one the book runs on. It also has two properties that the rest of this document is about, and the first of them is visible four sentences later.

The admission on the same page

Having said that the concession is not metaphysics, Dancy immediately tells us where the other two premises live:

Although the Argument from Relativity first appears in the Phaedo, the materials for its construction are to be found in the Socratic dialogues. Premise (ARF) figures as a criterion of adequacy for a correct definition, and instances of (ARO) are used in showing that various proposed definitions fail to meet that criterion. Dancy p. 19

All three premises are in the early dialogues on Dancy’s own account. (ARE) is conceded as topic-fixing; (ARF) operates as the adequacy condition on definitions; (ARO) is deployed in the refutations. What is absent is not the material but the assembly — the explicit statement of the conclusion.

This is exactly what the Euthyphro does at 7e–8a. The god-loved is both loved and hated by the gods, hence both pious and impious: that is an instance of (ARO), used to refute a candidate, against a standard that is (ARF). Dancy describes the procedure on p. 19 and declines to draw the consequence.

So the denial, restated in Dancy’s own terms: the Euthyphro does not state (ARC). It is not the claim that the Euthyphro lacks the materials of the theory, because Dancy says it has them. “Does not state” and “is not committed to” are different claims, and the whole weight of the book rests on treating them as one.

03The pool, in full

Two pages of chapter 3, reproduced closely enough that the reader can check the objections in Part Two against what is actually there.

Chapter 3 is called “Fixing the topic,” and its opening sentence states the thesis the pool is staged to illustrate: the existential admissions are “merely ways of isolating the subject to be discussed” p. 65. Then:

The dialogue p. 65

I: Well, there’s the Australian Crawl.
YOU: Oh, and what’s that?
I: It’s when you thrash about like this, and . . . {there follows an explanation, more or less, of this stroke}; like what that fellow over there is doing.
YOU: Talk about thrashing about! There’s also the Butterfly Stroke, you know.
I: No, how does that go?

Two pairs of eavesdroppers then read the exchange. He and She are appalled Platonists-spotters — “isn’t it awful? But perhaps they’re only immanentists.” Him and Her are delighted ones — “isn’t it wonderful? They have transcended the nominalism that threatens the fabric of our society.” Dancy: “These two people strike me as mad. No less mad are the other two.”

What the analogy then does is assemble, one at a time, every piece of evidence his opponents rely on — and each time note that nothing metaphysical has happened.

The evidence, reproduced in the pool
1
The existential prefix
“It is one thing to list the swimming strokes, another to do metaphysics. This is so even if one lists the swimming strokes, as we did, using ‘there is,’ the so-called ‘Existential Prefix.’” p. 66
2
Unity — one over many
Three swimmers, two strokes, so two must be doing the same one. “Here He and She would be in great pain, while Him and Her are in raptures.” p. 66
and two more, less often noticed
3
Paronymy, with the causal idiom
“someone would be an Australian crawler because he was doing the Australian Crawl, or a butterfly stroker by virtue of swimming the Butterfly Stroke” p. 66. This is Burnet’s instrumental dative, in English.
4
A standard, and a case falling short of it
“two of the swimmers were, despite appearances, really performing one and the same swimming stroke, the butterfly: one of them, say, is doing it quite badly” p. 66. A παράδειγμα, and a deficient participant.

And then the sentence the whole device exists to deliver:

In all of this, nothing has been said about the structure of the really real, and the only depth is in the water. But Him and Her are thinking: they have conceded the Existence, and then the Unity, of each of the Strokes; now they have gone on to admit their Priority: they have realized that the Strokes are a precondition for the intelligibility of the world of changing strokers, and that one can only become a stroker by swimming a Stroke. I am saying: nonsense. Dancy p. 66

The transfer to Socrates follows immediately, in a single sentence, and it is the sentence Part Two is about:

We are about to consider passages in which Socrates makes claims involving existence and unity, and says that people control themselves because they have temperance. Is he doing metaphysics, or something else? Something else, I think, mostly what we should call “ethics,” his approach to which centrally involves defining things like virtues. This of course requires that there be things like virtues to define, just as listing the swimming strokes requires that there be swimming strokes to list. It does not require consideration of the Ontological Status of things like virtues, any more than the other requires consideration of the Ontological Status of swimming strokes. Dancy p. 66

Three facts about the pool that matter later

1 · It appears once. Two pages, ch. 3 §3.1. It is never returned to, never defended against an objection, and not indexed. The framing of a three-hundred-page book rests on a device used a single time.

2 · The capital letters are carrying the distinction. Existence, Unity, Priority, the Strokes, Ontological Status. Dancy’s way of marking the move from using a word to asserting a doctrine is typographic. Nothing in the paragraph states the condition the capitals stand for.

3 · The explanation is bracketed out. “{there follows an explanation, more or less, of this stroke}”. The one part of the exchange where definitional content would appear is the one part Dancy declines to write. §10 is about what that omission costs.

04The three requirements, and how they are derived

The constructive half of the book, and the sentence in which the conclusion is assumed.

Chapter 4 opens the positive project. Dancy does not find a theory of definition stated in the dialogues; he builds one from the refutations, by a stated procedure:

The theory is not supposed to be Socrates’ (or Plato’s) own. Rather, it is derived in the following way. When Socrates rejects definitions he uses certain arguments against them. We ask: what would an answer have to be like in order not to fall to that argument? We identify the assumption, and ask what general claim it might most naturally be taken to instantiate. The assumptions in question are not ones that require reference to any special entities that figure as the objects of these definitions. Dancy, §4.1 p. 80
  1. The Substitutivity Requirement — “its definiens must be substitutable salva veritate for its definiendum” pp. 80–1.
  2. The Paradigm Requirement — “its definiens must give a paradigm or standard by comparison with which cases of its definiendum may be determined” p. 81.
  3. The Explanatory Requirement — “its definiens must explain the application of its definiendum” p. 81.

Where the conclusion enters the procedure

Read the last sentence of the derivation rule again. The procedure is: find the argument, find the assumption, generalise the assumption. Then, without a step, an extra clause: “The assumptions in question are not ones that require reference to any special entities.”

That is not a further instruction to the reader. It is the book’s conclusion, announced as a property of the method before the method has been run once. The footnote attached to it is the reply to Allen, and it is treated at §05.

Note also what immediately follows in the text. Ten lines after stating the requirements, Dancy quotes Euthyphro 6d9 as “a preliminary reading” — “I did not direct you to teach me this, some one or two of the many pious things, but that form itself by which all the pious are pious” p. 81. That is the refusal of a list. Fifteen pages earlier the framing rested on a conversation that lists. §10.

05The reply to Allen — a test of validity

Dancy answers Allen once, in a footnote, and the footnote is the second of his three instruments.

Note 3 to the derivation rule quotes Allen and replies:

Allen (1970) 68 (and passim, also in Allen [1971]): “Forms play a regulative role in the dialectic: . . . they determine the kinds of answer that are acceptable, and . . . unacceptable in Socrates’ search for definition.” But the hypothesis of a form to which Socrates has access is irrelevant to the logic of his argument. If his arguments work, they do so because his premises are true and his conclusions follow from them. These premises are either ones he states or ones close by what he states, and the question of validity has nothing to do with forms. Dancy p. 80 n. 3
THE TEST, ISOLATED

Take Socrates’ argument. Ask whether it is valid, and whether its premises are true. If the answer does not require a form, forms are not doing any work. Premises are what the test detects.

Against a certain opponent this is exactly right, and it should be said plainly: if anyone claimed that a Form appears among the premises of the argument at 10a–11b, or that Socrates’ access to one is what makes his refutation go through, Dancy has answered them. Nobody in this literature claims that.

What a validity test cannot do. A premise is something an argument uses. A presupposition is something an argument needs in order to be an argument at all — and a presupposition never appears among the premises, because if it did it would be a premise. So a test that inspects the premise set is blind to presuppositions by construction, not by oversight. Allen’s claim is about the second kind of thing. §13 shows this is visible on the page Dancy cites.

06Three denials, one instrument

The pattern only appears when the three are laid side by side, because they are in three different chapters and addressed to three different opponents.

The result

All three are aboutness-detectors. Each asks, in its own register, what the text puts forward: as topic, as discussion, as premise. None of them asks what the text must already have in place for the putting-forward to make sense.

That is why the disagreement with Allen has never produced an exchange. It is not that one of them has misread the Euthyphro. It is that Dancy’s apparatus has no register in which Allen’s claim could be entered, either to be confirmed or to be refuted.

07The threshold must be known before it can be denied

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 1

“To Dancy, to say that is not metaphysics, he must know what metaphysics is. Why is existence, unity, priority not metaphysics?”

The question is correct in form, and its answer is not the one the literature gives.

The standing answer in the literature is that Dancy has no criterion. Zeyl says so in his review; Prior quotes him approvingly and adds it in his own voice — “he does not offer an account of what makes a theory metaphysically innocent” Prior p. 75; and the review repeats it at its §04. That answer is too quick, and this section withdraws it.

Dancy has no definition. He has an operational criterion, stated at §02 above: the Argument from Relativity. Existence, unity and priority are not metaphysics because they are not yet AR. The concession of (ARE) is “simply a matter of fixing the topic for discussion” p. 19; metaphysics is what you get when the affirmation acquires “the subsequent apparatus” p. 68.

Two things wrong with the criterion — neither of which is that it is missing
It is taken from the destination
AR is not a general account of what makes a claim metaphysical. It is one argument, identified by where it occurs: “made explicit in Phaedo 74a–c” p. 18. Run it as a test on an early dialogue and the result is settled before the dialogue is opened, because the test for “is this metaphysics?” just is “does this contain the middle-dialogue apparatus?”
Its materials are already there
By Dancy’s own p. 19, (ARF) works as the adequacy criterion on definitions in the Socratic dialogues and instances of (ARO) do the refuting. So the difference is the assembly, not the materials — and “the conclusion is not stated” is a much weaker claim than the one the book is read as making.
Compare Prior, who borrows Quine. A borrowed criterion is external to the dispute and can in principle come out either way. Dancy’s criterion is his opponent’s conclusion — the middle-period theory — turned into the measure of whether the early dialogues have reached it.

What this does to §04 of the review

The threshold complaint gets stronger, not weaker. It was: nobody in this literature states a criterion. It becomes: one of them does, and it is the thing to be measured, used as the measure. That is a sharper charge and an easier one to check, and it is the reason this document exists.

08What “the only depth is in the water” does and does not say

CHARITH’S QUESTION — 2

“Does this mean that for Dancy the category of swimming strokes cannot be considered in an ontological sense, because they only exist in the depth of the water — when the swimmers leave the pool it ceases to exist, therefore it is not real? So for Dancy, if it continues to exist, is it real?”

And, after the first reply: “Does he mean that within the context of the analogy, ontological matters never come up, never arise?”

The first reading is wrong; the second is right, and the second is what makes §11 work.

The sentence is about the conversation, not about the strokes. The verb is said: “nothing has been said about the structure of the really real.” “The really real” is the stock English for Ἕντως ὄν, used derisively; and “the only depth is in the water” is a pun on deep — the exchange has no profundity, only a pool. Dancy is reporting that a question did not arise. He is not answering it.

Two reasons the transience reading cannot be his

1 · He says the opposite on the next page. “But if it means that there is an entity called a ‘norm,’ ‘over and above’ the actions and people to be judged or doing the judging — well, that is another question, and we need not go into it yet. Socrates does not go into it yet. He will, in the doctrinal dialogues” p. 67. The ontological status of virtues is expressly left open, to be settled later. Nothing is being declared unreal.

2 · The strokes would pass a persistence test anyway. The Australian Crawl does not cease when the pool empties; it is a stroke-type, and it survives the swimmers. If Dancy’s point turned on transience the example would refute him. It does not turn on transience, and he offers no criterion of the real at all — not persistence, not anything.

So the answer to the second form of the question is yes, and it should be stated exactly: within the analogy the ontological question never arises. It is not raised, and therefore not answered either way.

The ambiguity this creates

Dancy needs two claims here and the paragraph supplies one.

Aboutness: the conversation is not about the ontological status of strokes. True by inspection, and nobody disputes it.
Commitment: the conversation does not commit its speakers to any ontological status. Asserted, not shown.

“Nothing has been said about the structure of the really real” is the first. “It is one thing to list the swimming strokes, another to do metaphysics” is the second. The slide between them happens inside one paragraph, and the missing step is the whole question.

09For us, or for them?

CHARITH’S ARGUMENT — 3

“Even if his analogy is internally logically coherent, and would show that the swimming-strokes category cannot be ontological or metaphysical, and even if his analogy can be smoothly mapped onto the Socratic dialogues — still what he would do is show that Socratic conversation does not carry any metaphysical theory for Dancy, or for modern readers. It would not show that Socratic conversations did not carry any metaphysical theory for fifth-century BC Greeks, nor for Socrates.”

The objection is right, and Dancy concedes it in advance — which is a more interesting result than his resisting it would have been.

The question is not about what Plato believed when he wrote the Socratic dialogues. It is about what he does in those dialogues: what is involved in the arguments of those dialogues. In particular, I am not saying: the Socrates of these dialogues would not have conceded the existence of forms … He might well have wondered what someone who asked for such a concession was on about: he might have supposed that such questions were merely irrelevant to what he himself was after, namely how he ought to live. Dancy p. 67
Consequence one — the project assignment
1
Dancy is Project A, by self-declaration
The review’s §19 distinguishes asking what the claims yield when set out as a modern philosopher would set them out (A) from asking what they conveyed in their own idiom (B′). Dancy states that he is doing the first and declining the second — not as a limitation confessed, but as the definition of his question. His philology serves the argument-analysis; it is not an inquiry into the idiom.
2
So the review’s table can stop hedging
The earlier placement had him split — Project B′ on the philology, Project A on the p. 80 validity test. P. 67 resolves it. The philology is in the service of A throughout.
Consequence two — and this is the sharper one
3
His evidence does not discriminate
What Dancy offers in place of an argument here is an absence: “there is no discussion of such questions in the Socratic dialogues” p. 67. But the hypothesis he is denying predicts that absence. An idiom that carries ontological weight for its users is not thematised by them — that is what makes it idiom rather than doctrine.
4
Silence is the wrong kind of evidence
Two hypotheses, one observation, and the observation is entailed by both. A test whose result is the same whichever hypothesis is true has not tested anything.
Note what is not being said. This is not the objection that Dancy is anachronistic. He is not. It is that the question he answers and the question Allen answers are different questions, and that his answer has been read for fifty years as an answer to Allen’s.

10The mapping — how strokes were made to stand for virtues

CHARITH’S ARGUMENT — 4

“Here Dancy is mapping the ontological status of swimming strokes onto virtues. But how did he map it? Take what he says about swimming strokes as coherent, for the benefit of the argument. How would it then be mapped onto Socratic conversations and concepts like virtue? What if, while the swimming strokes’ status might be compromised exactly as Dancy wants, Socratic virtue’s is not?”

The load-bearing question of the whole document. The answer is that he does not decide it; he asserts it, and the assertion is two connectives long.

“This of course requires that there be things like virtues to define, just as listing the swimming strokes requires that there be swimming strokes to list. It does not require consideration of the Ontological Status of things like virtues, any more than the other requires consideration of the Ontological Status of swimming strokes.” p. 66

Just as and any more than carry the transfer, and nothing in the book defends either. Four ways the transfer fails, in order of increasing force.

  1. Listing is not defining. The pool conversation is a list. Socrates refuses a list — and Dancy knows the sentence that refuses it, because he quotes it fifteen pages later: “I did not direct you to teach me this, some one or two of the many pious things, but that form itself by which all the pious are pious” p. 81, quoting Euthyphro 6d9. His own sentence pairs “listing the swimming strokes” with an approach that “centrally involves defining things like virtues,” as though the two demands were interchangeable. The Euthyphro turns on their not being.
  2. The pool contains Euthyphro’s answer, not Socrates’ question. Asked what the Australian Crawl is, “I” replies: “It’s when you thrash about like this, and . . . {there follows an explanation, more or less}; like what that fellow over there is doing” p. 65. An ostension to an instance — which is Euthyphro’s answer at 5d, and precisely what Socrates rejects. And the definitional content is bracketed out unwritten. A conversation in which the Socratic demand is never made cannot show what the Socratic demand does or does not require.
  3. Strokes are practice-constituted; whether virtue is, is the question at issue. The pool case is innocuous because a stroke’s identity conditions are fixed by the community that swims it — the standard by which one swimmer is “doing it quite badly” is a rulebook. The argument at Euthyphro 10a–11b is a refusal of exactly that construal for piety: the pious is not constituted by being loved. So Dancy’s analogue has the property the dialogue argues against. The choice of example is not neutral; it settles in advance the thing the example is used to establish.
  4. The parody adds a premise Allen does not hold. “The Strokes are a precondition for the intelligibility of the world of changing strokers” p. 66. The intelligibility clause is Allen’s. The flux is Phaedo and Republic machinery, and Allen’s reading of the Euthyphro expressly does not use it — he distinguishes the earlier theory from the middle one by the absence of exactly that: “an ontology of Two Worlds, separated by a gulf of deficiency” Allen 1970: 68. The composite is ridiculed; the position is not engaged.

The dialogues supply their own analogy, and it is not a swimming stroke

This is the part of the objection that can be settled from the texts rather than by argument. Socrates, asked what kind of thing a virtue is, reaches for a comparison himself — at Laches 189e–190b, which Allen quotes at length on the page where he says “the aim of dialectic is real definition”:

If we know that sight added to the eyes makes that to which it is added better, and further, if we are able to add it to the eyes, then clearly we must know the essential nature of sight … if one did not know the thing itself, the essential nature of sight or hearing, one could hardly qualify as a consultant or physician in the matter of eyes and ears … So the first requirement is to know the essential nature of virtue. Laches 189e–190b, as translated and quoted at Allen 1970 p. 106

Allen draws the consequence in one line: “As sight is to the eyes, and hearing to the ears, so virtue is to the soul. As sight and hearing are things which are, so is virtue a thing which is” p. 106.

Two analogies, two results. Dancy picks a practice whose identity is fixed by its practitioners, and gets no ontology. Socrates picks a capacity whose nature is fixed independently of anyone’s practice, and gets one. Neither analogy proves anything by itself. But one of them is in the dialogues, and it is not Dancy’s.

So the answer to the closing form of the question. Yes — the strokes’ status may be compromised exactly as Dancy wants while virtue’s is not, and nothing in the book closes that possibility. The analogy transfers only if virtue is the same kind of kind as a stroke, and whether it is, is the question under dispute.

11Aboutness is not commitment

CHARITH’S CONCLUSION — 5

“Even if Dancy added that to his example and a hundred per cent mapping could be shown, the best he could do is show that the analogue displays no metaphysical theme, no metaphysically thematised discussion — and therefore none in the Socratic discussion. Unless Dancy shows why that is the case with his swimming-pool analogue, it would not succeed.”

This is the general form of which §§08–10 are instances, and it is correct.

Grant the analogy at a hundred per cent. What is then established is: the pool conversation contains no thematised metaphysics, and neither does the Euthyphro. True, and not in dispute — nobody claims Socrates announces an ontology. What is needed is: and therefore neither is committed to one. That step is never taken, in ch. 3 or anywhere else.

What the instruments see
Topic. Discussion. Premises.
All three are things a text puts forward. Dancy’s apparatus reads them accurately.
What the claim is about
What the text must have in place for the putting-forward to be intelligible.
A presupposition is, by definition, not put forward. Non-mention is the normal condition of a presupposition, not evidence against one.

What Dancy would have to add — and why more detail in the pool would not supply it

The natural repair is to enrich the swimming case: put a ti esti question into it, have “YOU” refuse the ostension and demand the stroke itself. That would not help. It would only make the swimming case controversial in exactly the way the Euthyphro is controversial, and the analogy would stop being an analogy to something obvious.

What he would need instead is a demonstration that the pool conversation and the Euthyphro exchange stand in the same relation to their own presuppositions — not merely that neither of them discusses ontology. That requires an account of what the ti esti question presupposes: the existential presupposition, the condition on admissible answers, and a theory of the category to which admissible answers belong. The review sets that out at its §27.

Dancy has no such account. What he has is a validity test, and a validity test is the one instrument guaranteed not to find one.

The audit

A case made only against a position is worth less than a case that says what its target gets right. This part begins with what Dancy establishes, because it is substantial and because the rest is only credible if it is said first.

Bottom line: Dancy killed an inference. He has been read as killing a position, and the position was never the inference.

12What survives of Dancy — and it is not nothing

The pool is decisive against the reading it was built to defeat. That reading is Burnet’s, and it is still the commonest one in the commentary tradition.

Burnet’s note on Euthyphro 6d9 reads the ontology straight off the grammar: the dative is instrumental and productive, so “the universal makes the particulars” Burnet 1924: 116. If that inference were good, then any construction of the same shape would carry the same freight.

The inference, and the counterexample
The inference
“X is F by the F” — a causal or instrumental construction linking an item to the kind it falls under — asserts that the kind produces the character in the item, and so asserts an ontology.
Dancy’s counterexample
“Someone would be an Australian crawler because he was doing the Australian Crawl, or a butterfly stroker by virtue of swimming the Butterfly Stroke” p. 66. Same construction. No ontology, and no temptation to find one.
The inference is dead, and Dancy killed it. Any reading that gets its metaphysics out of the shape of the sentence has to answer the pool, and none of them can. This is a real result and the review should keep it.

The difference between what he refuted and what he is read as refuting

Burnet’s claim is an entailment from an idiom. A single counterexample defeats an entailment, and analogies are the right tool for producing one. Allen’s claim is not an entailment from an idiom at all. It is that the form is what the question is a question about — the antecedent of the pronoun — and that real definition and the existence of forms are two descriptions of one commitment.

A counterexample to an entailment is not an answer to a presupposition claim. Dancy’s device does one job well and has been asked to do a second job it cannot do.

13The page Dancy truncates

Dancy’s single engagement with Allen cites a page. The page was read in full for this document, and two things in it are not in the footnote.

Here is the note again, with its ellipses as Dancy prints them:

Allen (1970) 68 (and passim, also in Allen [1971]): “Forms play a regulative role in the dialectic: . . . they determine the kinds of answer that are acceptable, and . . . unacceptable in Socrates’ search for definition.” But the hypothesis of a form to which Socrates has access is irrelevant to the logic of his argument … the question of validity has nothing to do with forms. Dancy p. 80 n. 3

And here is the passage on the page, at length:

These assumptions constitute a theory of Forms. That theory is both logical and metaphysical. Logically, Forms play a regulative role in dialectic: as antecedents of ‘it’ in the question ‘What is it?’ they determine the kinds of answer which are acceptable, and more importantly, unacceptable, in Socrates’ search for definition. They define the conditions for deciding when dialectic has succeeded, and when it has failed. Metaphysically, Forms affect the career of the world: they are the real natures of things, and the world is what it is because they are what they are. These two sides of Plato’s theory meet in the notion of real definition … To say that Forms exist is to say that real definition should be pursued; to say that real definition should be pursued is to say that Forms exist. The theory of Forms in the Euthyphro is not a superstructure gracelessly added on to dialectic: it is the foundation of dialectic. Allen, Plato’s ‘Euthyphro’ and the Earlier Theory of Forms p. 68
  1. The first ellipsis removes the presuppositional clause. What Dancy’s “. . .” stands in for is “as antecedents of ‘it’ in the question ‘What is it?’” — which is not decoration. It is Allen’s statement of the thing in dispute: the form is what the pronoun in the question refers to, so the form is presupposed by the asking, not asserted by the answering. It is the one sentence on the page that a validity test could not have been designed to answer.
  2. The half quoted is the half labelled “logical.” Allen says in the preceding sentence that the theory is “both logical and metaphysical,” and then gives a paragraph to each. Dancy quotes from the logical paragraph and replies that forms are irrelevant to the logic. The metaphysical paragraph — and the biconditional that follows it — is not quoted and not answered.
  3. “Access” is not Allen’s. The footnote refutes “the hypothesis of a form to which Socrates has access.” Allen nowhere claims Socratic access to a form; his claim is about what the question ranges over, not about what the questioner knows. The epistemic version is easier to refute and is the one the note refutes.

What the biconditional does to Dancy’s own project

Allen’s threshold is the biconditional: to say that Forms exist is to say that real definition should be pursued, and conversely. Dancy’s book is three hundred pages of deriving what a correct definition must do. On Allen’s threshold, that derivation is itself the commitment — which is why Allen 1970 gets named in the parenthesis at 2006: 71 and in the footnote at 2004: 80.

Dancy’s reply is the clause appended to the derivation rule: “the assumptions in question are not ones that require reference to any special entities” p. 80. That denies the biconditional. Denying it is what needed the argument, and what it gets is a subordinate clause and a footnote.

14The thresholds compared

Seven positions, seven answers to “how much must a text contain before it is doing metaphysics?” — and the answers are not even the same kind of answer.

The review’s §04 arranges the five opponents along one axis, from a high threshold to a low one. That arrangement is useful and it is incomplete, because it treats the disagreement as one about where the line falls. Once each criterion is stated in the words its author gives it, a second and deeper division appears: they are not measuring the same thing.

What the table shows that the one-axis arrangement cannot

1 · Three kinds of test, not one scale. Prior, Fine and Fronterotta ask what the claims commit their user to. Allen asks what the question presupposes. Vlastos and Dancy ask what the text takes as its topic. A commitment test and an aboutness test can both be correctly applied to the same passage and return opposite answers without either being wrong — which is exactly what has happened for fifty years.

2 · The two “no” verdicts share an instrument. Vlastos’s reflective-investigation test and Dancy’s thematisation test are the same test in different words. Both ask whether the text turns and looks at its own furniture. Neither asks what the furniture is doing while nobody looks.

3 · Prior has already refuted that instrument once. Against Vlastos: “it is too restrictive. True, Socrates does not ask ‘What is Form?’ in the early or Socratic dialogues, but neither does he ask it in later dialogues, such as the Phaedo and Republic, which Vlastos admits are metaphysical. Not until the Parmenides does Socrates ask, ‘What is Form’” Bloomsbury p. 81. Applied evenly, an aboutness test either promotes the Euthyphro or demotes the Phaedo. The same objection reaches Dancy and has never been made against him.
