Prologue

About this part

Menn’s own statement of what the book is, and is not.

In short

It is not a study of Augustinianism, and not a comparison of doctrines, which Menn calls “unhistorical.” It reads Descartes as trying to fulfil a seventeenth-century hope: a new philosophy, built out of Augustine, to replace Aristotle’s.

01What Menn says he is doing, and not doing

In Descartes’ time there were many such Augustinianisms, and there was a hope of constructing out of Augustine a new philosophy to replace that of Aristotle. I propose to read Descartes as trying to fulfill that hope. Menn, Preface p. ix checked
…also not a comparison between Descartes and Augustine, let us say on the cogito, or on the nature of the mind, or on the relation between faith and reason. I think the idea of such a comparison is unhistorical. Menn, Preface p. x checked

The second passage matters for how this project uses Menn. The protocol asks for “Note for the project” callouts wherever Menn bears on the cogito. It tells the notes-taker to flag the cogito material in ch. 1, ch. 5 §B and ch. 6 §A “thoroughly.” Menn’s own rule is that a cogito-to-cogito comparison is the wrong unit: the likeness is between projects. So the callouts should carry Menn’s caution with them. In his book the cogito is “the beginning of its path to knowledge of God” (p. xiii), not a freestanding certainty.

SM
Stephen Menn
Descartes and Augustine, 1998; paperback with a new preface, 2002
Historian of ancient and early modern philosophy. The book reads Plotinus (ch. 3) and Augustine (ch. 4) at length before turning to the Meditations (chs. 5–8), so the order of reading is itself an argument: “it is more crucial to understand Augustine than to understand (say) Bérulle or Jansen” (p. ix).
“…a seventeenth-century French thinker could not simply resemble Augustine, any more than a twentieth-century Russian thinker could resemble Marx.” — p. x
Part One

About this part

The protocol’s first “track especially”: “Arnauld’s citation of Augustine on the cogito.” Here are the passages, from both sides of the 1641 exchange.

In short

Arnauld was struck by the parallel. Descartes thanked him in one sentence and, in private, said the uses were “very different.” Menn calls the famous si fallor, sum the least interesting of the Augustine passages. The one he thinks matters is De Trinitate X, where Augustine uses self-certainty to tell mind from body, as Descartes does.

02Arnauld’s marvel

T1 · the first reader to name Augustine

“The first thing that I find remarkable is that our distinguished author has laid down as the basis for his entire philosophy exactly the same principle as that laid down by St Augustine — a man of the sharpest intellect and a remarkable thinker, not only on theological topics but also on philosophical ones. In Book II chapter 3 of De Libero Arbitrio, Alipius … says the following: ‘First, if we are to take as our starting point what is most evident, I ask you to tell me whether you yourself exist. Or are you perhaps afraid of making a mistake in your answer, given that, if you did not exist, it would be quite impossible for you to make a mistake?’”

Arnauld, Fourth Objections, AT 7:197–8, CSM 2:139 checked

A correction Menn makes and CSM does not. “Arnauld’s reference is to Augustine De Libero Arbitrio II,iii,7, and the textual citation is correct; the speaker, however, is not Augustine’s friend Alypius, but Augustine himself” (Menn p. 4 n. 2 checked). Menn’s own translation of Arnauld’s opening, “Here it first occurs [to me] to marvel [mirari],” keeps the Latin verb that CSM renders “find remarkable.” Arnauld then quotes Descartes’ “In that case I too undoubtedly exist, if he is deceiving me,” the demon-proofing line of Chat 3.

Menn’s gloss on what came next: “Descartes in his reply (AT VII,219) briefly thanks Arnauld for the citation from Augustine, and moves on to other business. But Arnauld’s comment marks the beginning of a long history of philosophers upholding Cartesianism as Augustinianism ‘in philosophical matters’” p. 5 checked.

03What Descartes said back

WhereDescartes on the Augustine parallelChecked
Fourth Replies, AT 7:219“I shall not waste time here by thanking my distinguished critic for bringing in the authority of St Augustine to support me…”CSM 2:154 checked
To Colvius, 14 November 1640“I am obliged to you for drawing my attention to the passage of St Augustine relevant to my I am thinking, therefore I exist. … he does use it to prove the certainty of our existence. He goes on to show that there is a certain likeness of the Trinity in us… I, on the other hand, use the argument to show that this I which is thinking is an immaterial substance with no bodily element. CSMK’s note to the Colvius letter makes Chat 7’s Test 1 for it. The I am thinking, therefore I exist “refers to the Discourse (AT vi 32; CSM 1 127), not to the Meditations, in which the famous catchphrase does not occur. See Augustine, De Civitate Dei xi, 26” (CSMK 3:159 n. 2 checked). The Latin is si enim fallor, sum, “if I am mistaken, I am” (as quoted by Carriero, Between Two Worlds, p. 75 checked).

T2 · Menn on the banner line of this page

“There are three letters to Mersenne … and one to Colvius … where Descartes responds to his correspondents’ urging him to look at a passage of Augustine, apparently in each case the same passage (De Civitate Dei XI,26), a not very interesting instance of the standard Augustinian argument that my conviction of my own existence cannot be deceptive, since, if I am deceived, I exist; Descartes tells Colvius that he has looked up the passage in Augustine, without expressing much interest in it.”

Menn, ch. 2 §D p. 66 n. checked (the AT references in this note are OCR-uncertain)

The irony is useful. The line most often quoted to show that the cogito is Augustine’s is the one Menn thinks tells us least. His reason is Descartes’s own. The inference is common property (“it could have occurred to any writer”). What differs is what it is for: for Augustine, an image of the Trinity; for Descartes, “an immaterial substance.” On p. 68 Menn explains why Descartes welcomed the parallel anyway. He makes “an external, defensive use of authority, to show that, since his opinions agree with those of respected Christian authorities, they cannot be absurd or heretical, and deserve a hearing on their own terms.”

04The better passage: De Trinitate X

In a later letter (AT V, 186, from 1648) Arnauld calls Descartes’ attention to another passage of Augustine (De Trinitate X,x, more interesting than De Libero Arbitrio II,iii,7, and much more interesting than De Civitate Dei XI,26) on the mind’s certainty of its own existence: in this passage Augustine tries to use this certainty to reach a knowledge of the nature of the mind, and in particular to distinguish it from bodies… Menn p. 67 n. checked

That is the Augustine who matters for this series. Chat 5 and Chat 6 were about the step from “I think” to “a thinking thing.” Chat 8 was about whether that thing is more than its thinking. De Trinitate X is where Augustine takes the same step, and Menn compares the two arguments at length in ch. 6 §A (index: “Descartes’ argument about the soul in the second Meditation compared with Augustine’s in De Trinitate X, 251–5” checked). His verdict on the difference:

But whereas Augustine had thought that these inferences were straightforward (as we have seen, Augustine argues in De Trinitate X,x,16 that the mind cannot be (e.g.) air, because it does not know air in the interior way that it knows itself), Descartes’ project of destroying and rebuilding our knowledge of bodies forces him to be more critical. Menn p. 254 checked

On the same page Menn quotes the Preface to the Reader, where Descartes denies he meant to exclude other attributes “in the order of truth … but only in the order of my perceptions” (AT 7:8). That is the distinction Chat 8’s quarrel turned on. In Chat 8 it was argued from the Gibieuf letter; here it is argued from the Preface.

02

Menn against the French consensus.

“Note precisely what Menn concedes to Gilson and what he denies.”

05Gilson and Gouhier: no augustinisme de Descartes

The consensus Menn opposes is dated and named: there is “a fundamental break between Descartes and the earlier Augustinian tradition … Such was the conclusion in the 1920s of Etienne Gilson and Henri Gouhier, and later, from a different point of view, of Martial Gueroult” p. 6 checked. The twist is that Gilson began on the other side. His 1913 thesis called the spirit of Descartes’ metaphysics that “of a neo-Platonic theology, itself renewed by the Fathers and above all by St. Augustine” (quoted p. 7). He then “was later to lead a massive reaction against this view” p. 7.

T3 · the concession and the denial

Gilson: “…the Augustinian filiation of the cogito, once admitted, would not authorize us to speak without qualification [sans plus] of an augustinisme de Descartes; for Descartes remains opposed to St. Augustine on the most essential point of Augustinianism: the relations between reason and faith…”
Gouhier: “It matters little that the matter is the same in the two systems; it matters little that the cogito is found in the one and in the other… above all this there is a soul which these resemblances do not touch, and it is to this soul that a study like ours should lead.”

Both as quoted and translated by Menn p. 8 and n. 6 checked (originals not held)

What Menn concedes and what he denies, as the protocol asks. He concedes the facts: the cogito is in both, and so are the materials. He denies the inference. “The conclusion of Gilson and Gouhier, that Descartes’ thought is radically anti-Augustinian, follows directly from their interpretation of Augustinianism as a certain ‘spirit,’ as Christian devotion expressing itself through reason” p. 9. Change what Augustinianism is, and the verdict changes. Gouhier’s “soul which these resemblances do not touch” is also the ground Alquié would later claim (Chat 8). It echoes Gueroult’s “initiation of one soul by another soul” (Chat 7 §04). All three Frenchmen reach for the soul behind the text. Menn reaches for the project.

06Gueroult: structure without history

When Gueroult explains the text of the Meditations, he ignores the historical background completely. In a book longer than the present study, he mentions St. Thomas four times, Aristotle five times, and Augustine eight times, mostly in passing. But he refers to Husserl eight, to Fichte fifteen, and to Kant twenty-seven times, often at some length: not, of course, as sources for Descartes, but as points of comparison to elucidate the structure of Descartes’ idealism. Menn p. 12 checked

Menn then names the motive. Gueroult, “just as much as Gilson, is influenced by the contemporary situation: standing on the secularist side of the debate, he wishes to claim Descartes as the founder of modern critical idealism, from Kant and Fichte to Husserl and Brunschvicg” p. 10 checked. But he does not throw out the method: “Gueroult is quite right that we cannot understand the meaning of Descartes’ philosophy without observing the order and connection of his doctrinal affirmations” p. 13. His own formula for the disagreement is “Gueroult’s anti-historical approach to the Meditations” p. 14.

08What the instructions miss

Menn’s own erratum. “Finally, I would like to flag the most important error I have noticed in the book, my acceptance of Baillet’s dating of Descartes’ encounter with Bérulle to the fall of 1628.” Following Rodis-Lewis, he now dates it to fall 1627. On that dating, Descartes’ resolve “to base [a new philosophy] on a metaphysics of Augustinian inspiration, dates to the winter of 1627–8” p. xiv checked.   2. Matthews. Menn marks off his project from Gareth Matthews’s Thought’s Ego in Augustine and Descartes (1992). Matthews is “not interested in the question of influence … but in the different ways that Augustine and Descartes ask first-person questions”. The two books also differ “about the functions of the examples of dreaming and of the deceiving God or evil genius” p. xiii checked. That last point bears on Chat 1 and should get a “Note for the project.” Matthews not held.
  3. The cogito caution. The protocol asks for the cogito to be flagged “thoroughly.” It should also tell the notes-taker that Menn calls a cogito-to-cogito comparison “unhistorical” (p. x). Otherwise the notes will extract exactly what the book declines to offer.

10Where the series ends

The line to keep

The inference “if I am deceived, I exist” is common property. What Descartes claimed as his own is its use: “to show that this I which is thinking is an immaterial substance” (CSMK 3:159).

That sentence gathers the series. Chats 1–2 asked whether the doubt that makes the inference necessary is coherent. Chats 3–4 asked what kind of act makes it immune. Chats 5–6 asked what it yields: an occurrence, a self, a substance. Chat 7 asked by what right we reconstruct it. Chat 8 found that the French quarrel over it was a quarrel over that same “use,” the thinking thing. Menn’s book, which this chat prepares, argues that the use itself was inherited. It is the Augustinian turn from the senses, to the soul, to God, rebuilt as the foundation for a physics. See the series index for the whole path.
